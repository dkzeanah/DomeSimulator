# Why Radial Log Wedges Can Be a Better Dome Building Member Than Store-Bought Lumber

This presentation incorporates current NAHB cost data, Census/NAHB construction-time data, historical NAHB material/labor rules of thumb, the 2021 lumber-price shock, and a normalized 33-week construction-phase model.

**Important:** The phase-time percentages are an illustrative schedule normalized to the 2024 average physical construction duration. They are not federal measurements of the median time spent by each trade.

WHY RADIAL LOG WEDGES CAN BE A BETTER DOME BUILDING MEMBER THAN STORE-BOUGHT LUMBER

A persuasive argument for designing the building around the tree instead of machining the tree until it resembles a conventional building product.

INTRODUCTION

Modern construction has trained us to think of wood as rectangular.

A tree enters a mill, and what comes out is a 2x4, a 2x6, a sheet, a truss, or some other standardized product.

That is incredibly useful for conventional buildings.

But it does not mean the rectangle is the natural or universally best structural form of a tree.

A geodesic dome is not a conventional rectangular building.

It is based on triangles, changing planes, radial geometry, repeated angular joints, and a shell that distributes loads through a network rather than through rows of ordinary stud walls.

That changes the question.

Instead of asking, "How do I turn this tree into store-bought lumber?"

I can ask, "What useful structural shape can I obtain from this tree with the fewest operations?"

For an approximately 8-inch-diameter log, one answer is simple.

Split the log through the center.

Split the halves into quarters.

Split the quarters into eighths.

One round log section becomes eight long 45-degree circular-sector wedges.

The result is not scrap pretending to be lumber.

It is a purpose-designed structural member for a purpose-designed triangular building system.

THE MATERIAL YIELD ARGUMENT

An 8-inch-diameter trunk has a radius of 4 inches.

Its cross-sectional area is pi times 4 squared, or about 50.27 square inches.

Divide that by eight.

Each raw wedge contains about 6.28 square inches of cross-sectional wood.

A modern store-bought 2x4 is actually about 1.5 inches by 3.5 inches.

That is 5.25 square inches.

So an eighth-sector from an 8-inch log contains about 20 percent more cross-sectional wood than a modern 2x4.

That does not mean it is automatically 20 percent stronger in every bending direction.

Section shape, defects, moisture, grain, buckling, and connection geometry still matter.

But it proves something important.

A modest round log can produce eight substantial structural members without first squaring the log into a cant and repeatedly cutting away material to chase rectangles.

The wedge keeps a large share of the original tree in the building.

THE TREE DOES NOT WANT TO BE A RECTANGLE

Conventional sawing is extremely mature and efficient, but geometrically it still solves the problem of fitting rectangles inside a circle.

Every rectangular board has to be bounded by straight faces.

A tree is round.

The closer we insist on dimensional uniformity, the more processing, handling, edging, trimming, kerf loss, and offcut management is required.

The wedge method does something different.

It divides the circle instead of inscribing rectangles inside it.

The geometry of the cut determines the product.

Half.

Quarter.

Eighth.

That is a very short manufacturing chain.

The objective is not perfect lumber.

The objective is a usable dome member.

Those are different manufacturing goals.

THE FORM FITS THE DOME

A conventional 2x4 begins with four faces at right angles.

A radial eighth-sector from a log begins with two flat radial faces separated by 45 degrees, plus a curved outside surface and a point toward the original center of the tree.

Each radial side is naturally 22.5 degrees from the sector centerline.

A standard 2V dome has two important face-fold families, approximately 18.03 degrees and 22.46 degrees.

Those are not identical to the raw wedge geometry.

But they are close enough that the wedge already contributes useful angular geometry before I machine anything.

That is the conceptual advantage.

With rectangular lumber, I manufacture the angular relationship from a square blank.

With the wedge, the tree gives me angular surfaces for free, and I only solve the remaining mismatch.

THE CONNECTOR CAN ABSORB PRECISION

This leads to a powerful design principle.

Do not machine every long structural member more than necessary.

Preserve the mass of the structural member and concentrate precision in a smaller repeating connector or jig.

If neighboring triangular panels meet at a dihedral seam, I can keep the wedge raw and use a trapezoidal or wedge-shaped key to absorb the remaining angular difference.

Or I can shave only a narrow bearing land on each wedge and use a flat key.

For the approximately 18.03-degree seam, a 2-inch bearing land requires roughly 0.48 inch of maximum removal at the fat end.

For the approximately 22.46-degree seam, a 2-inch bearing land requires roughly 0.40 inch of maximum removal.

That is very different from milling the whole member into dimensional lumber.

Most of the wedge stays intact.

The precision is concentrated where the parts actually touch.

THE JIG CHANGES THE FABRICATION PROBLEM

Natural timber varies.

Bark is irregular.

Diameter varies.

Taper exists.

The solution is not to make the jig a tight mold around the bark.

The jig should reference the repeatable sawn radial faces and the intended longitudinal axis.

The bark side receives clearance.

The red and green radial faces establish rotation.

The member axis establishes position.

The jig enforces the triangle.

This allows natural variation to exist where it does not matter while preserving precision where it does.

That is the manufacturing philosophy.

Precision where precision matters.

Tolerance where nature varies.

SACRIFICIAL OVERLENGTH IS AN ADVANTAGE

The wedge does not have to enter the jig with a perfectly calculated compound butt cut.

It can enter long.

The jig positions the member correctly first.

The butt end is allowed to hang past the final cut plane.

That overlength is sacrificial.

Once the wedge is locked into its proper axis and red-green orientation, the actual receiving surface or a proxy cut fence establishes the exact flush-cut plane.

Then I mark or cut the waste away.

The process becomes:

Locate accurately first.

Trim accurately second.

For rough natural timber, that can be more forgiving than trying to manufacture every compound end perfectly before the pieces ever meet.

THE DOME IS A NETWORK, NOT A ROW OF STUD WALLS

The wedge should not be judged only by asking whether it behaves like a rectangular floor joist.

A geodesic dome is a triangulated network.

Triangles are useful because they redirect loads through connected members and resist shape change without relying entirely on bending stiffness at every joint.

In a well-designed dome, members are intended to participate strongly in axial compression and tension.

That means the relevant question is not simply:

"Is this wedge a better 2x4?"

The better question is:

"Is this wedge an effective structural member in this particular triangulated system?"

That is a much more appropriate comparison.

LONG CONTINUOUS GRAIN IS RETAINED

The wedge is still a longitudinal piece of the original tree.

The fibers generally continue along the length of the member.

The process does not require laminating short pieces together or repeatedly cutting away the outside solely to create a rectangle.

Natural timber is not automatically structural grade.

Knots, checks, spiral grain, decay, juvenile wood, moisture, and other defects still have to be evaluated.

But the radial method itself preserves a great deal of the original continuous fiber architecture.

THE CURVED SIDE STOPS BEING WASTE

In ordinary milling, the curved outside of the tree is something we normally try to remove from a structural board.

In this system, the curved side can remain part of the member.

It provides section depth.

It preserves material.

It provides an obvious orientation reference.

It may offer attachment opportunities for skins, battens, or exterior systems.

And visually, it tells the truth about where the material came from.

The building does not have to hide the tree in order to use the tree.

THE PRODUCTION RHYTHM IS EXTREMELY SIMPLE

One long log section becomes two halves.

Two halves become four quarters.

Four quarters become eight wedges.

Every major division multiplies useful members.

There is very little decision-making about where the next board is located inside the log.

The geometry decides.

That makes the method naturally compatible with purpose-built guides, chainsaw fixtures, splitting systems, track saw arrangements, portable mills, and repeatable shop processes.

It is a least-actions, maximum-material approach.

NOW LOOK AT WHAT A CONVENTIONAL HOUSE ACTUALLY COSTS

The cost argument becomes more important when we stop thinking only about the price of a bundle of lumber and look at the entire house.

The National Association of Home Builders' 2024 Construction Cost Survey reported an average sales price of about 665,298 dollars for the homes in its sample.

The average direct construction cost was about 428,215 dollars.

That means construction itself represented 64.4 percent of the final sales price.

The finished lot represented 13.7 percent.

Overhead and general expenses represented 5.7 percent.

Financing represented 1.5 percent.

Marketing represented 0.8 percent.

Sales commission represented 2.8 percent.

Builder profit represented 11 percent before taxes.

This is important because it answers a common question.

Are we mostly paying for labor?

Not exactly.

But we are mostly paying for the act of creating and delivering the physical building.

Labor alone is not a majority of the final selling price.

Historically, NAHB has used a rough rule that building materials can represent about 25 to 30 percent of selling price and onsite labor about 20 to 25 percent.

Those historical shares should not be mixed mechanically with the 2024 table as though they came from the same survey.

But they are useful for scale.

Together, materials and onsite labor have historically represented roughly 45 to 55 percent of the selling price.

So when a building system attacks both material consumption and labor operations, it attacks two of the biggest cost centers at the same time.

THE 2024 CONSTRUCTION DOLLAR BY STAGE

Inside the 428,215-dollar construction cost, the 2024 NAHB survey found the following major shares.

Site work was 7.6 percent of construction cost.

Foundations were 10.5 percent.

Framing was 16.6 percent.

Exterior finishes were 13.4 percent.

Major plumbing, electrical, and HVAC rough-ins were 19.2 percent.

Interior finishes were 24.1 percent.

Final steps were 6.5 percent.

Other costs were 2.1 percent.

Translated to the final 665,298-dollar sales price, framing as a whole was about 10.7 percent of the sale price.

Foundations were about 6.7 percent.

Exterior finishes were about 8.6 percent.

Major system rough-ins were about 12.4 percent.

Interior finishes were about 15.5 percent.

These are not abstract numbers.

They show where simplification actually has economic leverage.

FRAMING IS A LARGE TARGET

The 2024 survey put framing at 70,982 dollars on average.

The framing-and-roof subcomponent alone was about 49,763 dollars.

Trusses were another 12,903 dollars.

Sheathing was another 6,513 dollars.

A panelized wedge dome directly challenges this category.

The structure and roof geometry are not separate conventional systems in the same way they are in a rectangular house.

The dome shell integrates wall direction, roof direction, and structural triangulation.

If the frame can be produced from local trees with low processing and repeated in a jig, the system has the potential to reduce purchased framing material and reduce specialized framing operations.

That is one of the strongest economic arguments for the dome.

EXTERIOR FINISHES ARE ANOTHER LARGE TARGET

Exterior finishes represented 57,510 dollars in the 2024 survey.

That includes exterior wall finish, roofing, and windows and doors.

A dome does not magically eliminate cladding, waterproofing, windows, or doors.

But it does merge the conventional wall-and-roof distinction into one continuous shell.

That can eliminate many conventional transitions, eaves, gables, valleys, hips, and separate roof-to-wall framing operations.

A good panel system can potentially make enclosure more repetitive.

The savings are not guaranteed.

A badly detailed dome with dozens of difficult leaking seams can erase the advantage.

The case depends on designing the panel seam and skin system as carefully as the wood frame.

FOUNDATION AND CONCRETE CAN ALSO CHANGE

Foundations represented 44,748 dollars in the 2024 survey.

The excavation, foundation, concrete, retaining-wall, and backfill subcomponent alone was about 43,002 dollars.

A dome can offer a compact perimeter relative to enclosed volume, and a circular or polygonal footprint can simplify some foundation concepts.

But the floor area still requires support.

So this is not a claim that domes eliminate concrete.

A slab is still a slab.

Soil still matters.

Code still matters.

What the dome can do is reduce complicated wall geometry above the foundation and potentially simplify the load path around the perimeter.

MECHANICAL, ELECTRICAL, AND PLUMBING ARE NOT AUTOMATIC SAVINGS

This is where the argument needs discipline.

Major system rough-ins were 82,319 dollars, or 19.2 percent of construction cost.

Electrical rough-in alone averaged 27,383 dollars.

Plumbing rough-in averaged 27,180 dollars.

HVAC averaged 26,938 dollars.

A dome does not automatically make these trades cheaper.

In fact, curved geometry can make them harder if the systems are forced to follow unconventional paths with no service strategy.

The dome advantage has to be designed.

A central utility core, accessible service chases, radial runs, modular panel penetrations, and short distribution paths can create savings.

Without those decisions, electrical and plumbing can become more complicated instead of less.

So I should not claim that dome geometry alone reduces every trade.

I should claim that a dome gives me the opportunity to reorganize those trades around a much simpler shell.

DRYWALL AND FLOORING ARE SIMILAR

Drywall averaged 13,962 dollars in the 2024 survey, about 3.3 percent of construction cost.

Flooring averaged 15,388 dollars, about 3.6 percent.

A dome does not eliminate flooring if it encloses the same floor area.

And ordinary rectangular drywall does not naturally love triangular curved interiors.

If I insist on conventional drywall everywhere, the dome may actually create more cutting and waste.

The labor advantage appears only if the interior finish system is designed for the geometry.

Triangular liners, panel skins, wood finishes, fabric systems, sprayed finishes, or intentionally exposed structure may be more compatible.

The correct argument is not that every conventional task gets cheaper automatically.

The correct argument is that the dome lets me delete or redesign entire conventional task families if I stop forcing a conventional house finish system onto an unconventional shell.

HOW LONG DOES A CONVENTIONAL HOUSE TAKE?

The Census Bureau's Survey of Construction reported that single-family homes completed in 2024 averaged about 9.1 months from authorization to completion.

About 1.4 months of that was authorization-to-start delay.

The physical start-to-completion construction period was about 7.6 months.

That is approximately 33 weeks.

The Census series reports averages rather than a single national median stage-by-stage schedule.

So the following phase percentages are a normalized representative 33-week professional schedule, built from common industry phase ranges and scaled to the federal average physical construction time.

It should be treated as a planning model, not as a Census measurement of each trade.

A REPRESENTATIVE 33-WEEK CONSTRUCTION CLOCK

Site preparation, excavation, and utility work can consume about 1.5 weeks.

That is roughly 4.5 percent of the physical construction clock.

Foundation and concrete work can consume about 3.5 weeks.

That is roughly 10.6 percent.

Framing and dry-in can consume about 5 weeks.

That is roughly 15.2 percent.

Exterior envelope work can consume about 4 weeks.

That is roughly 12.1 percent.

Mechanical, electrical, and plumbing rough-ins can consume about 4.5 weeks.

That is roughly 13.6 percent.

If those three rough trades are treated as roughly equal sequential slots for illustration, plumbing, electrical, and HVAC each represent about 4.5 percent of the total build clock.

Insulation can consume about 1 week.

That is roughly 3 percent.

Drywall and sheetrock can consume about 2.5 weeks.

That is roughly 7.6 percent.

Interior finishes can consume about 6.5 weeks.

That is roughly 19.7 percent.

Within that finish period, a typical flooring operation of roughly 1.5 weeks would equal about 4.5 percent of the total 33-week clock.

Final exterior work, driveway, landscaping, inspections, punch list, and closeout can consume about 4.5 weeks.

That is roughly 13.6 percent.

Real jobs overlap these trades.

That is why adding every subcontractor's calendar duration independently can exceed the actual elapsed build time.

The percentages are a normalized critical-path model.

They are most useful for seeing where large labor blocks occur.

WHERE A DOME CAN ATTACK THE CONSTRUCTION CLOCK

The strongest targets are framing, dry-in, exterior enclosure, and any interior finish system that can be integrated into prefabricated triangular panels.

In the representative schedule, framing plus exterior envelope is about 27 percent of elapsed construction time.

Add site-independent panel fabrication, and much of that work can potentially happen away from the final foundation and in parallel with other site work.

That is a major conceptual advantage.

The conventional house waits for one trade to create the conditions for the next trade.

A panelized dome can move some of those operations into manufacturing.

The triangle can be built in a jig.

The wedge can be aligned.

The butt end can be flush-cut.

The skin can potentially be attached.

Openings can be prepared.

Then finished modules can arrive at the site.

That changes the critical path.

A DOME DOES NOT NEED TO WIN EVERY CATEGORY TO MATTER

Suppose a dome does nothing to reduce the cost of the floor.

Suppose electrical still costs approximately the same.

Suppose plumbing still costs approximately the same.

It can still be economically significant if it substantially reduces the purchased framing package, structural labor, roof framing labor, wall framing labor, and some enclosure operations.

Framing and exterior finishes together represented about 30 percent of construction cost in the 2024 NAHB survey.

That is about 19.3 percent of the entire final selling price.

That is a large target.

And that does not count possible savings in foundations, insulation strategy, interior finish strategy, overhead from shorter schedules, or financing carrying cost from faster completion.

TIME ITSELF COSTS MONEY

A long construction schedule is not merely inconvenient.

It costs money.

Construction financing accrues.

Equipment sits.

Temporary utilities remain active.

Insurance remains in force.

Subcontractors have to be scheduled and rescheduled.

Weather windows are missed.

Materials are exposed longer.

Management overhead continues.

The 2024 NAHB survey attributed 1.5 percent of the final home price directly to financing cost and 5.7 percent to overhead and general expenses.

Not every dollar of those categories is schedule-dependent.

But some of it clearly is.

A system that shortens the critical path can create second-order savings beyond the wage hours directly removed.

THE 2021 LUMBER SPIKE SHOWS WHY MATERIAL INDEPENDENCE MATTERS

The pandemic lumber crisis provides a useful stress test.

NAHB reported that the four-fold lumber-price spike in 2021 added more than 30,000 dollars to the price of an average new single-family home.

NAHB testimony at the time described the increase as nearly 36,000 dollars.

That was the increase caused by the spike, not the entire lumber package.

Using the 2021 mean new-home price of roughly 458,300 dollars as a rough comparison, a 36,000-dollar increase was nearly 7.9 percent of the entire home price.

One material market moved enough to add almost eight percent to a home's price.

That is exactly why locally harvested structural material can matter.

The wedge system cannot eliminate the cost of every manufactured building product.

It still needs fasteners, membranes, glazing, electrical equipment, plumbing, and other components.

But every major structural function shifted from volatile purchased lumber to locally recovered timber reduces exposure to that supply chain.

HOW MUCH WOOD DOES A CONVENTIONAL NEW HOUSE USE?

NAHB cites Home Innovation Research Labs estimates that an average new single-family home uses roughly 15,000 board feet of framing lumber.

It also uses more than 2,200 square feet of softwood plywood and more than 6,800 square feet of oriented strand board.

That does not include every wood-derived product in the home.

Cabinets, doors, windows, trusses, engineered wood, and finishes add more.

The lesson is not that a wedge dome makes all of that disappear.

The lesson is that conventional residential construction consumes an enormous standardized wood package because the entire system is organized around standardized wood products.

If the main shell can instead be organized around a small number of local trees processed directly into structural sectors, one of the largest purchased-material streams can be attacked at its source.

REGULATION AND FEES ARE ALSO A LARGE PART OF THE PRICE

It is important not to blame the entire home price on sticks and labor.

The 2024 NAHB construction table included building permits, impact fees, water and sewer fees, inspections, architecture, and engineering inside site-work costs.

Those explicit line items together were tens of thousands of dollars.

A separate 2026 NAHB regulatory-cost study estimated that regulation at federal, state, and local levels added about 131,734 dollars to the price of a new single-family home, or 26.4 percent of the 499,500-dollar average sales price used in that study.

That broader regulatory estimate overlaps both land development and construction and should not simply be added on top of the 2024 cost pie.

But it makes one point very clear.

Even if I could make the structural shell almost free, I would not make the finished house free.

Land, utilities, permits, engineering, inspections, financing, and regulation remain real.

That is why a serious affordability argument needs to separate the shell problem from the total housing-cost problem.

WHAT THE WEDGE DOME CAN ACTUALLY CONTROL

The wedge dome has the most direct control over the structure.

It can reduce purchased framing material.

It can reduce the number of transformations required to turn a tree into a structural member.

It can shift precision from every long member into reusable jigs and connectors.

It can allow panel fabrication before site assembly.

It can merge wall and roof structure.

It can reduce conventional roof complexity.

It can use sacrificial overlength instead of precision pre-cutting.

It can turn irregular local timber into repeatable modules.

It can potentially shorten the shell critical path.

It can reduce exposure to retail lumber price spikes.

It can potentially reduce waste.

It can turn low-value local timber into high-value building structure.

Those are real and defensible advantages.

WHAT IT CANNOT CLAIM WITHOUT PROOF

It cannot honestly claim that every wedge is structurally superior to every graded 2x4.

It cannot claim that every dome is automatically cheaper.

It cannot claim that electrical, plumbing, HVAC, flooring, or drywall become cheaper simply because the exterior shell is curved.

It cannot claim that a nonstandard structure will automatically pass code.

It cannot ignore moisture, shrinkage, checking, fire resistance, fastener design, buckling, joint strength, wind, snow, uplift, foundation loads, or engineering.

The argument becomes stronger when it admits those limits.

The goal is not to prove that rectangles are bad.

The goal is to prove that rectangles are not mandatory.

THE ECONOMIC ARGUMENT IN ONE SENTENCE

A conventional home spends a very large share of its selling price on materials and labor, and a very large share of its construction budget and construction schedule on creating and enclosing the structural shell.

The radial-wedge dome attacks exactly that shell by reducing processing, retaining more of the tree, simplifying repetitive geometry, shifting precision into jigs, and enabling panelized fabrication.

THE MANUFACTURING ARGUMENT IN ONE SENTENCE

Instead of spending labor turning a round tree into a rectangular commodity and then spending more labor cutting that commodity back into angular dome parts, I can preserve the radial form and make the building system accept it directly.

THE ARCHITECTURAL ARGUMENT IN ONE SENTENCE

The wedge is not inferior because it fails to resemble a 2x4.

It becomes superior when the structure is designed so that its natural shape is useful.

THE LOCAL-RESOURCE ARGUMENT IN ONE SENTENCE

A storm-damaged tree, clearing tree, low-value pine, or locally available log can move directly from land to jig to structural panel instead of moving through a forest-to-mill-to-distributor-to-retailer-to-jobsite supply chain.

THE LABOR ARGUMENT IN ONE SENTENCE

The system replaces repeated bespoke measuring and compound cutting with a fixture that says where the wood goes and where the waste gets cut off.

THE DOME ARGUMENT

A geodesic dome is already an exercise in making geometry do work.

The triangle stabilizes the frame.

The sphere-like shell distributes forces.

Repeated panel types turn complex three-dimensional construction into repeated manufacturing.

The wedge method extends that same philosophy to the raw material.

Let geometry do work before labor does.

Do not remove useful wood merely because conventional lumber is rectangular.

Do not ask every builder to reproduce a difficult angle by hand.

Put the angle into the jig.

Do not demand perfect natural timber.

Reference the surfaces that matter and provide clearance for the surfaces that do not.

Do not precut irregular stock to theoretical perfection.

Let it hang long and flush-cut it after the fixture has established reality.

That is not primitive construction.

It is process engineering.

CONCLUSION

Store-bought lumber is one of the most successful standardized building products ever created.

For conventional rectangular construction, it is difficult to beat.

But a geodesic dome is not conventional rectangular construction.

When the structure is designed around radially split wedges from the beginning, the natural sector can offer something dimensional lumber cannot.

It can retain more of the original tree.

It can require fewer primary processing operations.

It can preserve substantial section depth and continuous longitudinal grain.

It can provide built-in angular faces.

It can be indexed by simple red-and-green radial references.

It can be fabricated with sacrificial overlength.

It can be forced into repeatable geometry by a reusable jig.

It can be converted into complete triangular panels before final assembly.

And it can reduce dependence on one of the most volatile and expensive material streams in conventional home construction.

The strongest claim is not that a wedge is universally better than a 2x4.

The strongest claim is more precise.

For a purpose-designed geodesic dome whose joints, keys, jigs, skins, and assembly sequence are built around radial timber from the start, the wedge can be a more direct, material-efficient, locally producible, and potentially labor-efficient structural form than first converting those same trees into ordinary dimensional lumber.

A rectangular board is a tree heavily processed to serve a rectangular building system.

A radial wedge is a tree lightly processed to serve a radial triangular building system.

When the structure is designed around that fact, the wedge stops being an unconventional substitute.

It becomes the native building block of the structure.
