# Hybrid metal/wood systems

Steel where the strength is and timber everywhere else is a sound principle
and the reference build already uses it in one place.

WHERE IT ALREADY APPEARS

The mast: a steel core running the height of the utility column, clad in
timber.

The floor: a steel hub clamping that mast, steel spokes radiating to the base
ring, timber decking over them.

In both cases the metal is doing a job timber is bad at -- carrying a
concentrated load through a small section -- and the timber is doing the job
metal is expensive at, which is covering area.

WHERE ELSE IT WOULD WORK

A steel base ring. The ring is a compression ring, it works in pure hoop
stress, and that is exactly what a steel band is for. It would also stop the
ring being the thing that limits how many openings you can cut.

Steel connectors at high-load vertices. The apex, and the base vertices where
the dome delivers its load to the pad.

Steel headers over openings.

WHERE IT WOULD NOT

Everywhere. A steel-framed dome is a different building with different
economics, and it loses the harvest argument entirely.

THE JOINT IS THE PROBLEM

Timber moves and steel does not.

A timber member bolted rigidly to a steel plate will, over a few seasons of
shrinking and swelling, either crush around the bolt or split from it. The
fix is a connection that allows movement in the direction timber moves --
slotted holes, or a single central fastener rather than a rigid pattern of
them.

That is well understood and routinely got wrong.

CORROSION

Wet timber and steel together is a battery. Use stainless or hot-dip
galvanised at every point where the two meet, and particularly at the base,
where water collects.

The reference build specifies stainless throughout its bolted connections,
which costs more and removes a whole category of failure from the building's
future.
