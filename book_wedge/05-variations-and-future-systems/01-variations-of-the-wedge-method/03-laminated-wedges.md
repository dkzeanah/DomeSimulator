# Laminated wedges

A laminated wedge is built up from thinner pieces glued face to face, then
cut to the wedge section.

WHAT IT BUYS

Size beyond the tree. A laminated member can be any length and any section,
which removes the constraint that the log sets.

Straightness. Laminating averages out the movement in each lamina, and a
glued-up member stays where you put it in a way a solid one does not.

Defects spread. A knot in one lamina is surrounded by six that are clear, so
its effect on the whole is small. That is why glulam is graded higher than
the timber it is made from.

Grain direction, chosen. You can put the strong direction where the load is.

WHAT IT COSTS

Boards, which means milling, which means the 45 percent recovery rather than
the 88.

Glue, clamps, a flat surface long enough, and the time for it to cure.

And a level of shop practice that most of this book is written to avoid
needing.

WHERE IT EARNS ITS PLACE

Big domes. Above a certain span the member a tree can give you is not enough,
and lamination is the only way to keep going with timber.

Long-span headers over openings, where one member is carrying what several
used to.

And repairs: a failed member replaced with a laminated one, because the right
tree is not standing there today.

THE HONEST POSITION

Lamination is better engineering and worse economics, and this method is an
economic argument. It belongs in the book because at some sizes there is no
alternative -- not because it is an upgrade to reach for at this one.
