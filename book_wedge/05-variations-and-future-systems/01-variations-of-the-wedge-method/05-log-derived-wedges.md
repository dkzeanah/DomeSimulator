# Log-derived wedges

This is the reference build, and it is worth setting out as a variation
rather than as the default, because the numbers only work for a particular
kind of tree.

THE LOG THE MODEL ASSUMES

15 inches at the butt,
5.5 at the top,
60 feet usable, cut into
12-foot sections.

That is an ordinary plantation softwood. Not a veteran, not a specimen, not
anything anybody would object to felling.

WHAT ONE GIVES YOU

442 board feet solid. Take off
51.2 for kerf and 391 board feet end up in
wedges -- 40 of them, making 80 struts at
4.89 board feet each.

Two trees make 160 members. The dome needs
120. So a 2V hemisphere of this size is two trees, with a
third of the stock spare for mistakes.

THE COMPARISON THAT MATTERS

Milled, the same log yields about 45 percent of itself as usable lumber. The
rest is slab, edging and sawdust.

Split into wedges, it yields about 88 percent. Nothing is edged because
nothing needs a rectangular section.

That is a 1.95x harvest advantage. Against it, the pinwheel frame uses 1.80x
more stock than a mitred one, because every joint overlaps rather than
meeting. Net: a wedge dome uses about 92 percent of the trees a mitred dome
of the same size would.

Eight percent, which is small -- and the point was never the eight percent.
It is that the eight percent comes with no mitre saw, no jig, no shop, and a
log instead of a lumber order.

THE CONSTRAINTS TO CHECK BEFORE COUNTING ON IT

Straight grain, adequate diameter, and enough of them. A stand of crooked
scrub will not do this, however many stems it has.
