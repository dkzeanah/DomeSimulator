# Standardized interfaces

An interface is a promise about where two things meet, and this building has
about six of them. That number is the design.

THE LIST

The pad port: where services enter, and where the column lands.

The apex sleeve: where services leave, under a gasketed cap.

The bay: two triangles, forty openings, four inserts each.

The rim: where the shell latches to the pad.

The wall port: where a utility panel connects to the exterior routing.

The mast: where the floor's hub clamps, and where a hoist lifts.

WHY SO FEW IS THE POINT

Every interface is a thing that has to be documented, manufactured
consistently, and kept compatible forever.

Six is a number a person can hold in their head and a drawing can show on one
page. Sixty is a system that needs a manual and a version number, and it will
drift.

WHAT STANDARDISING THEM BUYS

Parts from different years fit. A panel made now fits a frame made in five
years, because the bay did not change.

A supplier can make one part without making the whole building.

And an owner can make a part. A plywood blank with four holes on the right
pitch is a panel, and anybody can cut one.

THE DISCIPLINE

Fix the interface, vary everything else.

The bay outline does not change, so panels can be anything. The pad port does
not change, so the column can be improved. The rim does not change, so a
shell can be replaced with a different kind of shell entirely -- which is
exactly what happened when the hull became the cap and nothing else in the
building needed touching.

That last one is the proof the discipline works. The most expensive component
in the design was swapped for a completely different technology, and the
frame, the panels, the column and the pad did not notice.
