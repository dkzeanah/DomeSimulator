# Larger examples

Above about twenty feet the 2V starts to strain, and the strain shows up in
three places.

WHAT A 24-FOOT 2V LOOKS LIKE

    diameter                     24.00 ft
    apex height                  12.00 ft
    A strut, cut length         86.750 in
    B strut, cut length         77.478 in
    floor, inside the ring       423.2 sq ft
    shell surface                839.9 sq ft
    timber in the frame            821 ft

THE THREE PROBLEMS

Member length. Over seven feet, which is past comfortable one-person
handling and past a pickup bed.

Faceting. The panels are over 20 square feet and they
are flat. At this size a 2V reads as a faceted polyhedron rather than a dome,
and the flat panels start carrying bending they should not.

Load. The same 2V geometry at a bigger span means bigger forces in the same
number of members, and at some point the section has to grow -- which means
a bigger log, which the harvest argument may not be able to supply.

WHAT TO DO INSTEAD

Go to 3V. More, smaller triangles, three strut lengths, panels that fit
sheets, and a shape that reads as round.

The member count roughly triples from 120, which is the cost.

OR BUILD TWO

Two twenty-foot domes have more floor than one twenty-eight-foot one and
cost less per square foot, because the frame line does not change between
them.

That is the manufacturing argument again, and it is the reason this method
would rather make the same building four times than make a bigger one.

THE LIMIT NOBODY HAS TESTED

At what span does a wedge frame need engineering rather than reasoning?

This book does not know. Nothing here has been load-tested, and everything
above about twenty feet is extrapolation from a building that has been
solved but not built. That is worth knowing before anybody scales it.
