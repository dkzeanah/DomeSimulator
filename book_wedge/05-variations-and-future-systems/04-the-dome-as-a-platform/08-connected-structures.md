# Connected structures

Joining two domes is the detail that decides whether a compound is a compound
or a house, and it is harder than it looks.

THE THREE WAYS

A covered walk. Two separate buildings, a roof between them, and you go
outside-but-sheltered to move from one to the other.

This is the easy one. No structural connection, no thermal bridge, no
difficult junction, and each dome stays a dome.

A short link. A small rectangular structure joining the two, with a door at
each end.

Harder. Two junctions of a flat thing to a curved thing, which is the same
problem as the entry extension, twice. Flash to the frame, not to the shell,
and let the shell overlap.

Direct intersection. Two spheres overlapping, with the shared region open.

This is the one everybody draws and almost nobody builds, because the
intersection of two spheres is a circle in three dimensions and every member
meeting it is cut at a different angle. It is beautiful and it is a
geometry problem rather than a carpentry one.

WHAT THE METHOD PREFERS

The link, with a proper structure of its own, joined at reinforced openings
in both domes.

It keeps each dome intact and independent -- which means each can still be
taken apart, which is the thing this whole book is protecting.

A direct intersection welds two buildings into one and gives up every
demountable property they had.

THE THERMAL DETAIL

A link is a corridor, corridors are unheated in practice, and an unheated
corridor between two heated buildings is four cold walls where there were
two.

Either heat it and accept the cost, or insulate the two doors properly and
treat the link as outside. Deciding by accident gives you the worst of both.
