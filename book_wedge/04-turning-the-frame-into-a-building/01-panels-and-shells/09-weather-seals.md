# Weather seals

The dome has 55 interior seams, 10 base edges, one apex penetration and
however many openings you cut. Every one of them is a place water can get in.

THE GENERAL RULE

Shed, do not seal.

A detail that relies on a bead of sealant is a detail with a maintenance
schedule. A detail where water runs downhill, over a lap, and away needs
nothing from anybody for twenty years.

Use sealant as a backup to a shed detail, never as the detail.

THE SEAM

This is the interesting one, because the reference build does something
unusual with it.

Two sawn faces meeting at a dihedral angle do not close flush. There is a
wedge-shaped gap along every one of the 55 interior seams -- 309 feet of it,
reaching every vertex.

Every other way of building a dome machines that out. The reference build caps
it and uses it: as a conduit for services, and as an air duct, blown to keep
the frame dry or drawn to take what lands on the shell down into a tank.

So the seam detail is not only a weather detail. It is a channel with a cap on
it, and the cap is doing the shedding.

THE SHELL OVERLAP

The removable shell lands on the *frame*, not on the panels, and it overlaps
every seam.

That is what makes the seams a secondary line of defence rather than the
primary one. The shell sheds; the seams catch what gets past.

Two lines is the whole strategy and it is the same one used in the shower: an
inner barrier that takes the spray and an outer one that catches what gets
through.

THE APEX

One penetration in the whole weather surface, closed by a gasketed cap with
over-centre catches.

Meant to stay shut for years and come off in ten minutes. Every service that
gets added later leaves under that cap and runs down the outside, so nothing
ever cuts a new hole in a weathertight surface.

That is the single best weather decision in the building and it is worth
protecting. Do not let anybody drill the shell.
