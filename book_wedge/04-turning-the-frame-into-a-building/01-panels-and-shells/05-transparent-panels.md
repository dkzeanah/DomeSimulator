# Transparent panels

Glazing a dome is easy to overdo and easy to get wrong in one specific way.

THE OPTIONS

Glass. Best optically, heaviest, most expensive, and a special order in
triangles.

Polycarbonate, twin-wall or solid. Light, tough, insulating in the twin-wall
form, and it yellows and goes brittle under UV unless it has a coated face --
and the coated face has to go outward, which people get wrong.

Acrylic. Clearer than polycarbonate, more brittle, scratches easily.

Polyester or PVC corrugated sheet. Cheap, agricultural, and short-lived.

WHERE TO PUT THEM

Low, mostly.

A dome's upper panels face the sky. A glazed panel up there is a skylight: it
will light the room beautifully, cook it in summer and lose heat all night.
The reference build's own performance model finds the shape is already worth
41 percent less envelope than a box, and putting glass in the top gives a
chunk of that back.

Lower panels face outward and give you a view, which is usually what people
actually wanted.

THE THERMAL PROBLEM

Any glazing is a hole in the insulation.

A single-glazed panel is roughly R-1. A quilted layer is R-1.6, and the
reference build's seven-layer stack is R-12.7. So one glazed bay undoes rather
a lot of stacked quilt.

That is not a reason to avoid glass. It is a reason to count the bays.

THE ONE THAT CATCHES PEOPLE

A dome is a reflector. A curved glazed surface facing a room concentrates
light and heat toward the centre, and in a small dome the centre is where you
are standing.

Two or three glazed bays are pleasant. Eight is a greenhouse, and it will
behave like one.
