# Metal

Sheet metal over a dome is common, cheap and worth knowing the failure modes
of.

WHAT IT IS GOOD AT

Water. A metal roof sheds it and keeps shedding it for decades.

Fire. In a place where that matters, nothing else in this chapter compares.

Cost per square foot, if you buy it flat and cut it yourself.

WHAT IT IS BAD AT

Compound curves. A dome is not a cone and it is not a cylinder: you cannot
wrap flat sheet round it without either cutting it into small facets or
stretching it.

Small facets means a great many pieces and a great many laps.

Noise. Rain on metal over an uninsulated shell is memorable, and a dome is a
very good acoustic reflector -- it focuses sound at the centre of the room.

Heat. Uninsulated metal in sun radiates into the space below it.

Condensation. Metal is cold, inside air is damp, and the underside of a metal
sheet is where that meets. Without a vented cavity or a vapour control layer
you get water dripping off the inside of the roof and everybody blames the
roof for leaking.

THE PANEL-BY-PANEL APPROACH

The one that suits this dome: a flat triangle of sheet per bay, fixed to the
frame with the edges lapped or capped at the seams.

Forty pieces, all one of two shapes, all flat. That is a manageable job and it
fits the method's logic of doing everything forty times.

THE DETAIL THAT DECIDES IT

The seams. Forty triangles is 120 edges, of which 55 are interior seams where
two panels meet at a dihedral angle.

Every one of those is a lap that has to shed water downhill and accommodate a
fold of 18 or 22 degrees. Get the seam detail right and metal is excellent.
Get it wrong and you have 55 places for water to get in.
