# Insulated panels

An insulated panel puts the insulation in the bay rather than on top of the
shell, and that is a real architectural choice rather than a detail.

THE BAY AS A CAVITY

The wedge gives you the cavity for free.

Because the member's inward face stands proud of where the panel sits, every
bay has a lip already cut into it by the shape of a split log -- three-quarters
of an inch on the reference build. An inner panel drops onto that lip, and
behind it is a cavity as deep as the member: six inches.

Forty bays of six-inch cavity is a substantial volume of insulation if you
want it there.

WHY THE REFERENCE BUILD SHIPS IT EMPTY

Two reasons, and the first is the honest one: money. A dome that ships
insulated is a dome that costs more on day one, and the campaign's entire
argument is a low entry price.

The second is better. An empty cavity is a duct, and the seam channels connect
all forty of them. Fill the bays and you lose the airflow experiment
completely.

WHAT GOES IN, IF ANYTHING DOES

Quilted recycled fabric, a layer at a time. Roughly R-1.6 a layer.

Blown cellulose, if you accept that it settles and that you will never get it
out again.

Rigid foam, cut to triangles. Best performance per inch, most waste, and
forty triangles of offcut.

Nothing. Which is a legitimate answer for a workshop, a store or a summer
building.

THE DECISION UNDERNEATH

An insulated panel decides its R-value at the factory. A cavity you can open
does not.

This book's bias is toward the second, and it is the same bias that runs
through the whole method: prefer the thing you can change your mind about.
