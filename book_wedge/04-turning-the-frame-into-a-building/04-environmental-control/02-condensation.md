# Condensation

This is the failure mode most likely to ruin a building like this one, and
the reference build has an unproven answer to it. Both halves of that
sentence matter.

THE PHYSICS, BRIEFLY

Warm air holds more water than cold air. Inside air in an occupied building
is warm and wet -- people, cooking, washing, breathing.

That air moves outward through the envelope. Somewhere in the thickness of
the wall it reaches a temperature where it cannot hold its water any more,
and the water comes out as liquid.

That place is the dew point, and it moves depending on the weather.

WHY THIS BUILDING IS EXPOSED

The reference build's shower cap is a plastic membrane, then quilted fabric,
then a rain-slick cap.

Two impermeable layers with fabric between them is a moisture trap. Water
arrives in the fabric, cannot get out through either side, and stays. Wet
insulation does not insulate and wet fabric against timber rots it.

That is said out loud in the campaign film, on camera, because it is a real
objection and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

THE ANSWER, AND ITS STATUS

The seam network.

Every interior seam of a wedge dome is a wedge-shaped channel -- 309 feet of
it, reaching every vertex, connecting all forty bays. Blow air through it and
the frame stays dry. Draw air through it and what condenses gets taken down
into a tank.

It is a duct that the geometry gives you free, and it is exactly the right
shape for this problem.

It is also unproven. Nobody has built it and measured it. The reference build
prices the fans and ports and says plainly that the performance is an
expectation rather than a measurement.

WHAT TO DO IN THE MEANTIME

Ventilate generously. Vent moisture at source -- a cooker hood, a bathroom
fan -- because air that never gets wet cannot deposit water in a wall.

And if you build this before anybody has measured the duct, open a bay in
spring and look.
