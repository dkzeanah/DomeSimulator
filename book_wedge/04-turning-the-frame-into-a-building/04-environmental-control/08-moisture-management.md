# Moisture management

The whole of environmental control in this building comes down to where the
water is, so this section collects what the others have been circling.

THE FOUR PLACES WATER ARRIVES

Rain, on the outside. Handled by the cap and by the seams -- shed, do not
seal, two lines of defence rather than one.

Ground, from below. Handled by the pad, the drainage and a membrane under the
floor. 40 inches a year is what the reference build
assumes falls, and all of it has to go somewhere that is not under the
building.

Occupants, from inside. Cooking, washing, breathing. Handled by ventilation
at source.

Condensation, in the middle of the wall. Handled by -- as the section on it
says -- an unproven duct, and until somebody measures it, by generous
ventilation and by looking.

THE ORDER OF PRIORITY

Keep it out. Cheapest, most effective, and where nearly all the effort
belongs.

Let it escape. A wall that can dry in both directions forgives a lot. A wall
sealed on both faces forgives nothing, which is the shower cap's structural
weakness as a design.

Manage what is left. Fans, dehumidifiers, and opening a window.

THE CATCHMENT, WHICH TURNS THE PROBLEM AROUND

A dome is an excellent rainwater catchment: a smooth curved surface, one low
edge all the way round, and a seam network that already reaches every part of
it.

The reference build assumes 72 percent of what
falls can be caught. On this footprint, with
40 inches a year, that is thousands of gallons.

Which is the useful way to think about the whole chapter. The water is going
to arrive regardless. The only question is whether the building is arranged
to get rid of it or arranged to keep it.
