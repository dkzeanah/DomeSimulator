# Cooling

The dome's shape helps in winter and hurts in summer, and it is worth being
straight about which.

WHAT THE MODEL SAYS

The reference build sizes a window unit at a computed 3,365 BTU/h and buys a
6,000 BTU/h one, because that is the smallest thing actually sold.

Design temperature difference 20 degrees, solar
gain 2.6 BTU/h per square foot of glazing,
SEER 14.

Three thousand BTU is very small. A single room in a conventional house is
often twice that. The 41 percent envelope saving is doing most of the work.

THE SUMMER PROBLEM THE SHAPE CREATES

Upper panels face the sky.

In a box, the roof is the only surface facing up and it is the one everybody
insulates hardest. In a dome, the upper third of the *wall* faces up too, and
it is getting sun for the whole day rather than for the part of it when the
sun is on that side.

That is the real summer penalty of this shape and it does not get talked
about much.

WHAT ACTUALLY WORKS

Shade the top, which the next two sections are about.

Ventilate at night, when the outside air is cooler than the building, and
shut it up during the day. In a building with this little mass that works
less well than it does in a stone house, but it is free.

Do not glaze the top. A glazed upper bay is a skylight, it will light the
room beautifully, and it will cook it from May to September.

THE THING THAT SURPRISES PEOPLE

A dome is a reflector. A curved glazed surface facing a room concentrates
light and heat toward the centre, and in a nineteen-foot dome the centre is
where you are standing.

Two or three glazed bays are pleasant. Eight is a greenhouse, and it will
behave like one.
