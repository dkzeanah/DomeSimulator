# Heating

A small dome is easy to heat and easy to overheat, and the second is more
common.

HOW LITTLE IT TAKES

At R-12.7 with 277 square feet of floor, the heat load is small
enough that most domestic heaters are oversized for it.

That is not a boast; it is a warning. An oversized heater cycles -- full
blast, off, cold, full blast -- and a building with almost no thermal mass
follows it exactly. The room is alternately too hot and too cold and never
comfortable.

THE WOOD STOVE ARGUMENT

The reference build prices a small wood stove, and it suits this building for
reasons beyond the obvious.

It is mass, in a building that has none. A stove and its shield hold heat and
give it back slowly, which is the smoothing the envelope cannot do.

It is independent of the grid, which matters on the kind of land this
building goes on.

And its flue leaves through the apex, which is already a designed
penetration, so it does not cut a new hole.

The cost is floor area and a clearance zone, in a room that has 277 square
feet of it.

THE ALTERNATIVES

Electric resistance: cheapest to install, dearest to run, trivial to control.

Heat pump: dearest to install, cheapest to run, and the same unit does the
cooling.

Radiant, in the floor: only really available if the foundation is a slab,
which is one more thing the slab quietly buys.

THE DOME-SHAPED PROBLEM

A curved ceiling focuses. Heat rising from a stove in the middle collects at
the apex, and the apex is nine feet up where nobody is.

A slow ceiling fan pushing down solves it, and the reference build prices a
central light and fan as one fitting for that reason.
