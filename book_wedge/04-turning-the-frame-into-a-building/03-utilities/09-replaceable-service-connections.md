# Replaceable service connections

Every service in this building is meant to be disconnected, and designing for
that is what makes the rest of the method possible.

THE PRINCIPLE

Nothing is plumbed or wired in permanently. Everything lands on a connector.

Water on a union or a quick-connect. Waste on a slip joint. Electrical on a
plug or a terminal block rather than a spliced run. Each of those costs a few
dollars more than the permanent version and takes the same time to fit.

WHAT IT BUYS

The dome moves. The column comes out and goes into the next one --
$260, rated for
25 years of service.

A fault is isolated rather than hunted. A connector is a place you can test
either side of.

A part is replaced rather than repaired. The fitting that failed comes off
and a new one goes on, by somebody who is not a tradesman.

And an upgrade is an afternoon. Better pump, bigger tank, more circuits: all
of them land on the same connectors.

THE ARGUMENT AGAINST, WHICH IS REAL

A connector is a joint, and every joint is a place that can leak or work
loose. A permanently plumbed system has fewer failure points than a
demountable one, and that is simply true.

The trade is between fewer failures and easier fixes, and this building
chooses easier fixes -- because it is a building that expects to be altered,
and one that cannot be altered without a tradesman will not be.

THE DISCIPLINE THAT MAKES IT WORK

Label everything. Standardise the fittings so there are three sizes in the
building and not eleven. Keep the spares in the under-floor storage that is
already there.

None of that is clever. All of it is the difference between a demountable
building and a building somebody once took apart and could not put back.
