# Avoiding weak discontinuities

A discontinuity is any place the shell stops being a shell -- an opening, a
join, a change of material, a place where the triangulation is interrupted.

They are not avoidable. They are manageable, and the management is mostly
about not putting them next to each other.

THE RULES THAT MATTER

Do not stack them. Two openings in the same vertical line, one above the
other, leave a strip of shell with nothing bracing it sideways.

Do not mirror them. Two large openings directly opposite each other across the
dome halve the base ring's ability to work as a compression ring -- and it is
the compression ring that stops the dome spreading at the bottom.

Do not cluster them. Three openings within a few panels of each other make one
large weak region rather than three small ones.

Spread them round. If you need four windows, put them at roughly 90 degrees to
each other rather than in a row.

THE APEX

Leave it alone.

Five triangles meet at the top and they are the most interdependent group in
the building. The reference build puts one penetration there -- a gasketed
sleeve for services -- and that is a hole through a panel, not a missing
panel.

THE BASE RING

The base ring is a compression ring and it is the single most important
continuous element in the dome. It is what turns outward thrust into hoop
stress and stops the dome spreading.

Interrupting it is the one thing to avoid if you can. A door that takes a base
triangle interrupts the panels but should not interrupt the ring member itself
-- keep the member at the bottom continuous and take the opening out above it,
with a threshold over the member.

That is a small detail and it is worth more than most of the reinforcement in
this chapter.

THE TEST

Walk round the finished frame and look for any place where you could push the
shell sideways and it would move.

If there is one, that is a discontinuity that needs bracing. If there is not,
the openings you have cut are fine.
