# 53. A Zome Without a Bevel

<!-- concept: cabin_panel_method/zome -->

Chapter {{ch.zome}} said a zome's hubs are where zome builders spend their time,
because the sticks arrive at a hub in a pattern the joint has to hold. That is
true of a zome built as a frame of single sticks. It stops being true the
moment each panel is framed on its own, the way Chapter {{ch.panel_jig}} frames
a dome's triangles. Then the panels meet each other along their edges, and
there is no hub at all.

It is called the *non-beveled* method, and builders who have framed zomes
both ways overwhelmingly choose it. The figures are this book's, solved for its
own zome:

    {{pm.constants}}

The zome is nine-sided -- {{zm.panels}} rhombus panels in {{zm.rows}} rows of
{{zm.per_row}} -- about {{zm.rim_ft}} feet across its rim and {{zm.height_ft}}
feet tall above it, every panel side {{zm.side_in}} inches.

![One stick, one miter, per row.](plate-zome-model.png)

## Four sticks in a pinwheel

<!-- concept: cabin_panel_method/zome_joint -->

Each panel is four sticks in a pinwheel, the arrangement of Chapter
{{ch.pinwheel}} once more: each stick runs to one corner and butts the next at
the other. A rhombus's corners alternate sharp and blunt, and the sine of an
angle is the sine of its supplement, so the shortening at every tail is the
same -- **all four sticks of a panel are one stick**. Its two ends are cut at the
same angle and parallel, long point to short point.

Nothing is bevelled. The sticks stay square through their whole depth, so
there is no compound cut, no tilted blade, and no jig with a bevel in it.
Cut one end, butt two sticks together on the bench, mark the panel length
across the joint, scribe the miter and cut to the mark; set a stop block and
cut the rest of that row's sticks from full-length boards. Screw pairs into
halves and halves into panels.

![Both ends cut parallel; nothing bevelled.](plate-zome-joint.png)

## The gap, and what fills it

Square-edged panels meet only along their *inside* edges. Where two panels fold
away from each other, that leaves a V-shaped gap on the outside whose width is
twice the stick's depth times the tangent of half the fold. In a two-by-four on
edge it opens from **{{zm.gap_lo}} to {{zm.gap_hi}} inches** on this zome.

The gap is allowed on purpose. On a small zome, screw the inside edges together
and let the roof bridge it -- a zome's panels each slope one way, so roofing
laps down the structure like shingles. On a large one, rip a **wedge block** to
half the fold on each face and fix it between the panels: it fills the V and
gives the joint something to bear on. The block's bevels are in the table.

Assemble with two screws per stick near the inside edge, not tight, so the
panels can hinge. As the inside edges line up, the fold sets itself. Brace the
top rows -- they sag under their own weight until the ring closes -- then go back
and add the rest of the fasteners.
