# 2. Water Is the Silent Killer

A frame that is out by an inch stands for fifty years.

A skin that leaks for one winter takes the frame with it.

That asymmetry is the most useful thing in this book, and almost every dome
book has it the other way round.

![One watertight layer over everything else.](plate-shower-cap.png)

## Why water gets to decide

Structural failure is loud. Something cracks, something sags, something you
can see from across the room. You get warning, and the warning arrives in a
form that makes you do something about it.

Rot is not loud. Rot is a slow chemical argument between a fungus and your
building, conducted at about twenty per cent moisture content, entirely out of
sight, and it is usually two or three years old by the time anything shows on
a surface you look at.

By then you are not repairing a leak. You are replacing members, and the
members are the thing this whole method spent its cleverness on making cheap
— so you are not even losing much money. You are losing the fortnight.

That is the trade nobody states plainly: the frame is cheap to build and
expensive to replace, because replacing it means taking the building apart.

## Where a dome actually leaks

Not through the panels. A panel is a large flat sheet with no holes in it and
it is the easiest part of any building to keep water out of.

Domes leak at seams, at penetrations, and at the apex, in that order — and a
2V dome has a great many seams.

This one has {{dome.seams}} interior seams, which is {{dome.seam_ft}} feet of
joint. Every foot of that is a place where two panels meet and where somebody
has to have done something deliberate.

![Every seam in the dome, and the key in each one.](keys-everywhere.png)

Three hundred feet of joint is not a detail. It is the single largest risk
surface the building has, and it is spread out so evenly that no one part of
it looks important enough to worry about.

## The move that removes the category

You do not solve three hundred feet of joint by making each joint better.

You solve it by putting one continuous layer over all of it, so that the
joints stop being the outside of the building.

That is what the shower cap is. One watertight membrane over the whole shell,
strapped down, with nothing structural fastened through it and no seam in it
that is not a welded one. The panels underneath do not have to be watertight.
The seams underneath do not have to be watertight. They have to be dry, and
they are dry because something above them is doing that job and only that job.

The chapter on the cap ({{ch.one_layer}}) has the physics and the arithmetic.
What matters here is the ordering: **one layer whose only job is water, and
it goes on last and comes off first.**

## And the floor

The floor is the second thing, and for a different reason.

It is the surface you are in contact with all day. It carries everything you
own. It is the only part of the building that is in permanent contact with
the ground, which means it is where ground moisture arrives, and unlike a wall
it cannot dry to the outside because there is no outside — there is dirt.

A floor that is cold, or damp, or that flexes, is a floor you notice every
hour you are awake. A frame that is out of true is a frame you never think
about again after the first week.

Get the floor right. Chapter {{ch.floor}} says what right means, and what the
five ways of doing it cost on this footprint.

## The hierarchy, stated once

1. **The skin.** Water. One continuous layer, no compromises, no penetrations
   you did not design.
2. **The floor.** Comfort, dryness, and everything you own.
3. **The frame.** Standing up, which it will do whether or not you were
   careful.

If you read nothing else in this book, invert your instincts in that order.
It is worth more than every angle in the appendix.

## The catch, and the next chapter

There is an obvious objection, and it is a good one.

The skin and the floor are the expensive parts. They are the parts you cannot
cut out of a tree with a chainsaw. Telling somebody that the two layers that
matter are also the two layers that cost money is not obviously helpful.

The answer is that they are also the two layers you can *defer* — and the
next chapter is about the four things you do while building the frame that
keep that option open for the life of the building.
