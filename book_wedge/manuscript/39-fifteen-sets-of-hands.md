# 39. Fifteen Sets of Hands

<!-- concept: why/middlemen -->
<!-- concept: why_build/chain -->

Almost none of a stud's shelf price is the tree.

There are two ways to get from a standing tree to a wall. The long chain: earn an
income, pay tax on it, drive to a store, buy lumber somebody else has sawn,
dried, graded and shipped, pay a contractor to put it up, and very often borrow
to do all of that. The short chain: tree, saw, wall. This chapter counts what the
long chain is made of, so the short one can be priced honestly against it.

## Counting the machines out

<!-- concept: why/chain -->
<!-- concept: why/mills -->

Every conversion step between a log and a stud is a building full of machinery,
with its own setup, labour and margin. Station by station, which of them the
wedge path still needs:

    {{econ.mills}}

The wedge path drops the sawmill, the edger, the kiln and grader, and the planing
mill. It keeps the chainsaw, the crosscut and the joinery.

## What a stud has been through

<!-- concept: why/middlemen_math -->

And the hands. A stud on a rack has been sold and handled this many times on its
way there, listed rather than rounded, because a round number is easy to say and
hard to check:

    {{econ.middlemen}}

Each of them is a real job done well. Each of them also takes a margin, and all of
those margins are in the price on the rack.

bowed, twisted or wet, the ones lost on the way home, the truck, and the hours of
driving, picking and queueing. The overheads are estimates, and they are all on
