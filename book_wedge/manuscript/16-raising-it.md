# 16. Raising It

Forty panels are stacked against a fence. This part takes three days and it is
the only part that looks like building a dome.

![The ten panels that stand on the ring.](course-first.png)

## Before anything goes up

**Set the base ring out first.** A regular decagon, {{dome.base_sides}} sides,
each one an A chord — so each side is {{cut.a_chord}} inches and the
perimeter is sixty feet. Chapter {{ch.floor}} is what it sits on.

Get this right and the rest is assembly. Get it wrong and every course above
it inherits the error, because the base is the one place in this building
where error does accumulate.

Check it by diagonal, not by angle. Opposite corners of a regular decagon are
a known distance apart; measure two of them and compare.

**Sort the panels into two piles.** {{dome.aaa}} equilateral, {{dome.bab}}
isosceles. Mixing them up costs an hour on the second course.

**Have more bracing than you think.** You will need it for about six hours
total and you will need all of it at once.

## The first course

Ten panels stand on the ring. They lean inward from the moment they go up,
which is the first surprise: a dome is not a wall that gradually curves, it
is leaning in from the ground.

Stand two adjacent panels, key the seam between them, and brace the pair
externally. Then work round. Each new panel keys to the one before, and the
run of them wants to fall inward until the ring closes.

**The ring closing is the event.** Until the tenth panel keys to the first,
you have a curved wall held up by props. After it, you have a structure.

Do not take the bracing out yet.

## The second course

![The band above: where the dome starts holding its own shape.](course-second.png)

Thirty panels, leaning further in, each one keying to two below it and to its
neighbours either side.

This is where the dome starts holding its own shape — and it is where the
bracing can come out, once the course is closed all the way round. Not before.
A half-closed second course is the least stable the building will ever be,
because you have added weight and height without adding the ring that
resolves it.

Work from opposite sides toward a closure point rather than round in one
direction. It keeps the load symmetric and it means the last panel of the
course is a panel rather than a wedge-shaped gap you have to persuade.

## The apex

![Closing the top.](course-apex.png)

Five panels around one vertex.

This is the last thing to go in and the only part of the assembly that has to
be *held* rather than propped. Two people, or one person and a strap from
below.

If you have built the mast of Chapter {{ch.floating}}, the apex ring is
already up there and the last five panels close around it. If not, the apex is
five keys and a cap.

## What goes wrong

**The last panel of a course does not fit.** Almost always the ring is out,
not the panel. Check the base diagonals before you start cutting anything.

**A seam gaps at the outside.** The key is not driven far enough. It is a
wedge; drive it.

**A panel will not key to its neighbour.** One of them is in upside down.
This happens with the isosceles ones, whose two short sides look alike. Mark
the long edge of every BAB panel on the jig, in the shop, before it ever comes
outside.

## Then stop

The frame is up. It is weather-tight in the sense that a bird cage is
weather-tight, and it will stand like this indefinitely.

Do not stop here for a winter. That is the failure Chapter {{ch.hotswap}}
describes, and this is the moment it happens: the frame is standing, it looks
finished, it is intoxicating, and the skin is the expensive part.

The skin is the part that matters. It is the next chapter, and it is the
reason all of this stands up in thirty years' time instead of in three.
