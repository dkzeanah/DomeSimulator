# 18. A Dome Is a Head, and It Wears Hats

I learned how to dress a building standing on the outside of a ship in the
Arctic Circle, on forward lookout, at night.

![A quilt, and a bigger cap for each one.](plate-quilt.png)

## What you wear out there

Nobody wears one warm coat. You wear layers — several of them, each thin,
each doing one job — and on top of all of it goes a pumpkin suit: an
immersion suit, bright orange, entirely waterproof, and utterly useless as
insulation on its own.

The suit does not keep you warm. The layers keep you warm. The suit keeps the
layers *dry*, and a dry layer is worth several wet ones.

The other thing about that arrangement is that it is adjustable in the middle
of a watch. Too cold, add a layer. Too warm, lose one. You never take off the
suit.

That is the whole shell design in this book, and I did not arrive at it by
reading about vapour barriers.

## The dome as a head

A dome is a head. It is round, it is the top of something, and it wears hats.

It can wear a bare skin — nothing at all, a frame with panels on it.

It can wear a toboggan: a quilted layer, then another, stacked, each one
adding warmth.

And over all of it, always, one shower cap. Waterproof, thin, useless as
insulation, and the reason every layer under it is dry.

People understand this instantly in a way they do not understand "vented rain
screen with a vapour-permeable weather-resistive barrier", which is the same
idea in a language that makes people stop listening.

## Each hat is a size up

This is the part that is arithmetic rather than analogy.

A layer goes *over* the layers already there, so it is bigger than the one
under it. Not a lot bigger — the shell is only a few inches thicker each time
— but the areas compound, and by the seventh layer the outermost is
meaningfully larger than the innermost.

So "one quilt costs fifty dollars" is a sentence about the first quilt. The
model works out each layer's own area, and the cap that goes over all of them
is sized for the stack you intend to end up with rather than the stack you
have.

![The envelope gaining a layer a winter.](plate-layering.png)

## It gets warmer every winter

Here is what the layering model buys that a conventional wall does not.

A conventional wall is insulated once, when it is built, to whatever you could
afford that year. Changing it later means opening the wall.

This one is insulated from the outside, in increments, forever. Each autumn
you can add a layer — an evening's work with a sewing machine and a box of
old clothes — and the building is warmer than it was last winter.

![What each quilted layer is actually worth.](plate-layers-worth.png) The cap goes
back over the top and nothing else about the building changes.

A house that improves as you live in it is a different relationship from a
house that is as good as it will ever be on the day you move in.

## And the quilts are made of clothes

The insulating layers in this design are quilted from recycled textiles —
clothing that would otherwise be waste.

That is partly an economic argument and it is mostly a participation one. It
is the only part of this entire method that somebody can do at a kitchen table
with no land, no saw and no site. A layer is a real object with a number on it
that goes on a named building, and it takes an evening.

The arithmetic is in the model: how many garments, how many pounds, what
R-value the stack reaches, and what a layer is worth to the person who sews it
for somebody else's dome. That last one has to be a real number or this is not
a network, it is a request for free labour.

## Where the analogy stops

Two places, and both matter.

**A person moves and a building does not.** Your layers work partly because
you are generating heat inside them and moving air through them. A building
generates less heat per unit of surface than you do and does not move at all,
so it relies on the vented gap of Chapter {{ch.one_layer}} to do what your
movement does.

**You take your suit off indoors.** The dome does not. The cap is on it in
July, in the sun, which is the ultraviolet problem at the end of the last
chapter and the reason the cap is a consumable.

Neither of those breaks the model. They are the two places where a builder
who reasons from the analogy alone will get it wrong, so they are written
down.
