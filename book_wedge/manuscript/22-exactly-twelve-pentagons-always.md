# 22. Exactly Twelve Pentagons, Always

Everybody reaches for hexagons. Honeycomb, graphite, the compound eye — the
hexagon is nature's answer to tiling a plane, and it is the shape people
sketch when they imagine a dome that is not made of triangles.

There is a catch, and it is absolute.

![Exactly twelve pentagons. Always.](plate-hex-twelve.png)

## A sheet of hexagons will not curve

Six regular hexagons meet at a point with their angles summing to exactly 360
degrees. A full turn. No angle left over.

That is what makes hexagons perfect for a **flat** surface, and it is exactly
what stops them curving. Curvature is bought with *missing* angle: to make a
surface close up into a ball you need the angles around each point to sum to
less than a full turn, so that the surface has to pucker.

Hexagons have nothing to give. A sheet of them stays flat forever, however
large you make it.

## So where does the angle come from

A pentagon.

Put five hexagons and one pentagon around a point and the angles no longer
reach 360. The surface puckers. Do it enough times and it closes into a ball.

And the number of times is not a design choice. Euler's formula fixes it: a
closed surface tiled with hexagons and pentagons, three faces meeting at every
corner, needs **exactly twelve pentagons**. Twelve. Not eleven, not thirteen,
and no amount of hexagons changes it.

A football has twelve. A fullerene has twelve. A hexagonal dome has twelve,
or it is not closed.

## The single-hexagon dome

The smallest useful case is charming: one hexagon at the crown, a ring of
pentagons, and you are done.

It has **one strut length**, cut ninety times, and **two flat panel
templates** that cut the entire skin. For a small structure — a shelter, a
shade, a play house — that is an extremely cheap parts list.

## Then you want it bigger

![The panels stop being flat.](plate-hex-warp.png)

Here is where it goes wrong, and it goes wrong in a specific way worth
knowing before you commit.

Scale a hexagonal dome up by subdividing and two things happen at once.

**The hexagons stop being regular.** Away from the pentagons the tiling
stretches, so the hexagons become slightly different from each other — and
each different one is another panel template. Every step up multiplies the
shapes.

**And the panels stop being flat.** A hexagon has six corners. Three points
define a plane; six do not, and on a curved surface the sixth corner will not
lie in the plane of the other five. The panel warps.

A triangle cannot warp. That is the property Chapter {{ch.triangles}} opened
with, and this is where the bill for not having it arrives.

## What the warp costs

You have three ways out and all of them cost something.

**Accept the warp** and force the panel flat when you fix it. It goes in
stressed, it wants to spring back, and every fastener is holding that.

**Fold the panel** along a diagonal, which is admitting it is two triangles
and building it as one.

**Make the panels smaller** until the warp is below what the material
tolerates, which means more of them.

None of those is fatal. All of them are work that the triangulated dome
simply does not have.

## The two domes, side by side

![The two domes, side by side.](plate-hex-compare.png)

The honest summary:

**A hexagonal dome is better** when it is small, when you want few strut
lengths, and when big flat panels matter more than anything else.

**A geodesic dome is better** when it is large, because the triangle's
flatness is guaranteed at any size and the hexagon's is not.

The crossover is not far up. Which is why this book builds triangles — and
why it is worth understanding the hexagon properly rather than dismissing it,
because for a chicken coop the hexagon wins and this method would be
over-engineering.

## The thing to carry away

Curvature costs angle, and something has to pay.

In a hex dome, twelve pentagons pay it. In a geodesic dome, the subdivision
pays it — every one of those twenty faces becomes triangles that are slightly
different from each other, which is precisely why there are two chord lengths
instead of one.

There is no tiling that curves for free. Every round building in this book is
a different answer to the same bill.
