# 1. Your Skull Is Not Symmetrical Either

Every dome book I have read opens by telling you to be precise.

Measure twice. Hold your angles. A geodesic dome is a mathematical object and
you are about to approximate one out of wood, so the closer you get to the
maths the better your building will be.

I believed that for about a year, and it cost me most of a year.

![The reference build. Nothing about it is as accurate as it looks.](standard-dome.png)

## The afternoon I stopped believing it

I had a frame up. Not this one — an earlier one, made of whatever the
chainsaw had produced that week, with no two sticks alike. I had built it to
prove to myself that the method could not work, so that I could stop thinking
about it and go and buy some lumber like a normal person.

It stood. It stood with a stick in it that was out by most of an inch, and a
vertex where three members met that I could put my whole thumb into.

I pushed it. I hung off it. I put a ladder against it and climbed on it,
which I do not recommend and did anyway.

It did not care.

## Why it did not care

A dome carries its loads *axially*. The members are in compression along
their own length — pushing end to end, the way a stack of bricks pushes —
and a triangulated shell passes a load from one triangle to the next all the
way round, so no single member is ever being asked to do very much on its own.

Put a stick in slightly out of position and two things happen. The triangle
it is in is very slightly a different triangle. And the load path shifts by
that same very slight amount.

Neither of those is an error that grows. A frame is not a tower of tolerances
stacking up toward a failure; it is a shell that absorbs the difference and
carries on. The error goes into the seam — and there is already a key in the
seam, because there was always going to be one.

## The lumpy skull

You have an asymmetric skull.

Genuinely: measure it. One side of your head is further from the midline than
the other, one eye socket is fractionally lower, your jaw is not centred.
This is true of essentially everybody and it is why nobody's face looks right
when you mirror it.

Nobody likes you any less for it, and your skull opposes a baseball bat
exactly as well as a perfectly symmetrical one would — because the thing that
stops the bat is the *shape*, a closed curved shell in compression, and not
the precision.

That is a dome. The geometry does the work. The accuracy is decoration.

## What this book does with that

It spends it.

If the frame does not need precision, then the frame does not need a mill. It
does not need a planer, or a moisture-controlled shed, or lumber bought to a
tolerance somebody else held for you. It can be made of logs split with a
wedge, on the ground, in a field, by one person who is not very good at it
yet.

That is the whole method. Everything else in this book — the {{cut.sector}}-degree
sector, the pinwheel joint, the one flat board that makes {{dome.panels}}
identical panels — is a consequence of deciding that the frame is the part you
are allowed to get wrong.

## And what it does not spend

Here is the part that took me another year to learn, and it is the reason
this book exists rather than being a pamphlet about splitting logs.

The permission is not general. It applies to the frame and it applies to
nothing else.

The skin has to be right, because water does not negotiate and does not
announce itself. The floor has to be right, because everything you own sits
on it and because the ground sends its water up.

So the advice most dome books give is not merely unhelpful. It is aimed at the
wrong layer. It asks for care where care is cheap to skip, and it treats as
finishing work the two layers that decide whether the building is still there
in thirty years.

The next two chapters are about those two layers. Then we can go and cut
something.
