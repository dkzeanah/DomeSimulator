# 15. Doors, Windows, and What Not to Cut

An opening is a panel you did not close.

That is the whole rule, and every expensive mistake people make putting holes
in domes comes from not believing it.

![Take whole panels. Cutting a strut means replacing a load path.](plate-openings.png)

## Why a whole panel

Chapter {{ch.triangles}} said a triangulated shell carries load because the
triangles cannot change shape. Take one triangle out and the ones around it
close the gap between themselves — the load goes round, the way it goes round
a missing tile rather than through it.

Cut a **strut**, and you have not made a hole. You have removed a member from
three triangles at once and given the shell nowhere for that load to go. Now
you need a header, and posts either side, and something to carry what the
header carries, and you are framing a wall in a building that did not have
walls.

So:

**Take whole panels. Never cut a member.**

A panel left out is free. A panel cut into is a structural alteration.

## Where the door goes

Ten panels stand on the base ring, so the ground floor has ten places a
doorway can be, and they are all the same.

That is the first surprise for anybody used to rectangular buildings: you do
not choose where the door goes on a plan, you choose *which panel*, and there
are ten.

**Take two adjacent first-course panels** for a comfortable door. One is
enough for a doorway you duck through; two gives you a proper opening with
room either side.

**Frame the perimeter, do not frame the hole.** The three members bounding
each missing panel are already there and already doing their job. The door
frame hangs *inside* them, on the same threaded inserts of Chapter
{{ch.hotswap}}, and carries nothing but the door.

**The threshold is the base ring.** It is already level, already continuous,
and already the thing the floor meets. Do not build a sill on top of it.

## The sequence

1. **Decide after the shell is up.** This is the one decision in the whole
   build that gets better for being left late. Stand inside, see where the
   light actually comes from and where you actually walk, then pick the
   panel.

2. **Leave the panel out, or take it out.** If you are still raising, simply
   do not fit it. If the shell is closed, undo its keys and lift it out — the
   whole argument for panels being objects, collected.

3. **Fit the jamb inside the triangle.** Three sides, following the members,
   bolted to inserts rather than screwed into end grain.

4. **Hang a door in a rectangle inside that triangle.** Doors are
   rectangular; triangles are not. The leftover triangle above the head is
   either a fixed light, a vent, or infill — and a fixed light is the best
   answer because it puts daylight above head height where it does the most
   good.

5. **Flash it under the cap.** The cap of Chapter {{ch.one_layer}} runs over
   the whole shell and the door is a hole in the shell, so the cap has to be
   returned into the opening and terminated on the frame. This is the one
   place in the building where the single watertight layer is interrupted,
   and it is where a dome leaks if it leaks.

## Windows, and why they go high

The wall leans in. That changes everything about glazing.

A window low in a dome looks at the ground a few feet away and lets in very
little light, because the aperture is tilted toward the floor. A window high
up looks at the sky, which is the bright thing, and it is why every dome
photograph you have ever liked has the glass up near the crown.

So: **put glazing above head height** unless you need a view of something
specific.

The other consequence of the lean is that every opening in a dome is partly a
roof opening. Water arriving at a window in a wall runs down the wall; water
arriving at a window in a leaning surface runs *onto the glass and sits at the
bottom edge*. Detail every window as you would a rooflight — head flashing
over, sill drained outward, and the cap returned properly — not as you would a
window in a house.

## The panel you do not take

One warning, from the geometry rather than from experience.

The first course is ten panels and they are the ones standing on the ring.
Taking two adjacent ones is comfortable. Taking three in a row is the point
where the ring between them has nothing above it for a third of its length,
and the base ring is the one part of this building that is genuinely carrying
in bending rather than in compression.

Three is probably fine. This book does not know that it is fine, because
nothing here has been load-tested, and a building with a third of its ground
floor open is a different structure from the one that was solved.

If you want a wide opening, take two panels and put the third somewhere else.

## Doing it on a zome

![Take out a whole panel and no strut needs cutting.](plate-openings-zome.png)

The same answer, on a shape built the opposite way.

A zome's panels are bigger — one parallelogram is a lot of opening — and the
rule is identical: take the whole panel, frame inside the struts, cut nothing.
That the rule survives a completely different geometry is a good sign that it
is about triangulated shells in general rather than about this dome.

## What this chapter is not

It is not a schedule of door and window sizes, because those come from what
you can buy where you are and from a panel whose dimensions are
{{seam.narrowest}} inches across at the narrowest — Chapter {{ch.size}}.

And it is not permission. An opening in a load-carrying shell is exactly the
kind of thing an engineer exists for, and the rule at the top of this chapter
is what keeps you out of their office rather than a substitute for it.
