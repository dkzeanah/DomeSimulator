# 40. How Much Shelter Does an Hour Buy

<!-- concept: why_build/why -->
<!-- concept: why_build/sources -->

The usual question is how cheap a house is. The better one is how much shelter an
hour of your life buys -- because an hour is the thing you actually have a fixed
number of, and money is only stored hours.

The figures here are a mix, and they are labelled: the author's own round numbers
for one cycle of the shell, beside published ones.

    {{econ.why_sources}}

## What an hour produces

<!-- concept: why_build/production -->
<!-- concept: why_build/leverage -->
<!-- concept: why_build/geometry -->
<!-- concept: why_build/jig -->

One cycle builds {{why.sqft}} square feet of shell in {{why.hours}} hours:
{{why.sqft_per_hour}} square feet an hour. At ${{why.usd_per_sqft}} a square foot,
that floor is worth ${{why.space_value}} finished, but framing did not create all
of it; plumbing, wiring, windows and finishes are still to come. So take only the
framing, {{why.framing_pct}} percent: **${{why.framing_value}}**. Divided by the
hours, that is **${{why.rate}} an hour**.

Nobody hands that over as a wage. It is value you no longer have to buy. And it is
more than the labour in the framing would earn: labour is {{why.labor_pct}} percent
of that framing, ${{why.labor_value}}, or ${{why.labor_rate}} an hour. The dome way
produces {{why.leverage}} times as much, because you supply the labour *and* replace
the wood. Two other things help: a wedge arrives with its angle already in it
(Chapter {{ch.member}}), and the jig solves the geometry once instead of for every
panel (Chapter {{ch.jig}}).

## Debt makes it larger

<!-- concept: why_build/debt -->
<!-- concept: why_build/interest -->

Now suppose that framing would otherwise ride on a {{why.mortgage_years}}-year
mortgage at {{why.mortgage_pct}} percent. ${{why.framing_value}} does not stay
${{why.framing_value}}; it becomes ${{why.financed}} of payments. Over the same
hours that is ${{why.financed_rate}} an hour. That is not a wage and it is not
today's money: it is payments that never have to be made, spread across decades.
Today's value is still ${{why.rate}} an hour.

Take a whole ${{why.loan}} mortgage at the same rate. Over its term you pay
**${{why.loan_interest}} of interest**, ${{why.loan_total}} in all. At
${{why.usd_per_sqft}} a square foot, the interest alone would buy
{{why.interest_sqft}} square feet. Interest builds no bedroom, no workshop, no wall.
It is the price of having the house before you have the money. Building directly
does the other thing: effort becomes equity, one hour at a time.

![Interest builds no bedroom.](plate-interest.png)

## Count it in hours of your life

<!-- concept: why_build/life_hours -->

Money is stored labour, so price the frame in hours. At an example take-home of
${{why.wage}} an hour, buying that framing costs {{why.buy_hours}} hours of pay.
Building it costs {{why.hours}} hours of work plus {{why.cash_hours}} hours of pay
for the ${{why.cash}} of cash it still needs: {{why.build_hours}}. **{{why.kept_hours}}
hours of life kept**, about {{why.kept_weeks}} working weeks.

## Out of the commodity chain

<!-- concept: why_build/shocks -->

A conventional house also runs a long critical path -- about {{why.weeks}} weeks
from start to finish, by the Census count -- carrying financing, weather,
scheduling and overhead the whole way. And it rides on commodity lumber, whose
record price in one recent spring added tens of thousands of dollars to an average
new home (Chapter {{ch.house_money}}). A dome still needs fasteners, glazing and wiring
from the same supply chain, but every member that comes from a nearby tree is one
less thing exposed to that chain's shocks.

## When to build, and when to buy

<!-- concept: why_build/rule -->
<!-- concept: why_build/honest -->

This is a rule, not an ideology. What building earns per hour is the money it
avoids, less the cash it still costs, over the hours it takes: ${{why.net}} over
{{why.hours}} hours, **${{why.breakeven}} an hour**. If your take-home is under that,
those hours are worth more building than working extra to buy the same frame. If it
is well over, buying may be the better use of your time, and the model says so.

What the model does not promise: it is a model. The author's figures are round, the
mortgage is illustrative, and the payments avoided are nominal dollars over decades.
It is a way to see a decision clearly, not a guarantee.
