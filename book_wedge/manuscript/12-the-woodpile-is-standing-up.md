# 12. The Woodpile Is Standing Up

<!-- concept: cabin_wedge_explained/grain -->
<!-- concept: wedge/open -->
<!-- concept: why/open -->
<!-- concept: seed_pitch/frame -->

This is a house frame before it is cut.

Not a pile of lumber from a yard, and not a drawing. A tree, standing on the
land you are going to build on, already the right shape for something. The
question this Part asks is what happens if the tree gets a vote in the design:
if instead of forcing it into rectangles, you ask what it is already good at
and build around that.

The answer, it turns out, is a frame. Split a round log through its heart and
every piece is a structural member, and nothing but the width of the saw blade
leaves the log. The frame is already on your land. It is standing up.

![A whole house frame, before it is cut.](plate-one-log.png)

## What is standing there

<!-- concept: wedge/trunk -->

Before anything is cut, measure what you have. A trunk is a cone with its tip
cut off, so its wood is a formula in its two end diameters and its length.
This is the tree the films that come with this book work through -- one pine,
{{work.log_butt}} inches at the butt, {{work.log_top}} where it stops being
useful, {{work.log_ft}} feet between:

    {{work.trunk}}

That is the starting balance. Every way of turning the tree into a building,
in this chapter and the next, is measured against it.

## The fell line

<!-- concept: harvest/fell -->

Felling gets its own section because nothing after it is safe until it is
right, and because the part of it that does the work is not the saw.

1. **Cut the notch** on the side you want the tree to fall toward: a wedge of
   wood taken out of the trunk, about a quarter of the way in.
2. **Cut the back cut** from the other side, a little higher than the floor of
   the notch.
3. **Stop before the cuts meet.** The strip of wood left between them is the
   **hinge**. It steers the tree all the way to the ground; cut through it and
   the tree goes wherever it likes.

The hinge steers the tree. The saw only sets it free.

That is the whole technique in a paragraph, and it is not enough to learn it
from. Fell your first trees with somebody who has done it, wear the gear, and
clear an escape path at an angle behind you before the first cut. This book's
saw is an ordinary hardware-store chainsaw; the skill is not in the machine.

![The hinge steers the tree.](plate-fell-line.png)

## All at once

<!-- concept: harvest/explode -->
<!-- concept: harvest/why -->

Then everything happens at once. The crown comes off. The trunk is bucked
into sections the length of the longest member you need. And every section
splits the same way: in half, in half again, and once more, into
{{cut.splits}} sectors. One tree's whole contribution to the frame comes off
the saw in the same few operations, repeated, and the arithmetic of it is
short:

    {{work.yield}}

The honest cost is the stick itself, not the wood: from a small log, a single
wedge is the weaker member in bending, and it is the triangulated shell, not
the wedge, that makes it enough. Chapter {{ch.member}} puts numbers on exactly
where that stops being true.

## The trees size the building

<!-- concept: wedge/sizing -->
<!-- concept: wedge/m_dome -->
<!-- concept: why/dome -->
<!-- concept: why/sizing -->
<!-- concept: why/stick -->

Here is the inversion that runs through this book. A normal building is
designed first and the timber is bought to suit. Here the timber comes first,
and the building is solved **backwards from the longest member the wood can
give**.

Buck a section, cut it once across the middle, and that half-length is your
longest member. The dome that member makes is then fixed by geometry -- a size
nobody chose:

    {{work.dome}}

That is the worked tree of these films: a different, slightly larger dome than
this book's reference build, because those two trees gave a slightly longer
member. The method is the same; only the tree changed. And that is the point:
**the number that depends on the tree is the trunk's diameter**, because it
sets every member's section and so its strength. Everything else -- the
angles, the two lengths, the panel count -- belongs to the geometry and does
not care what tree you started with (Chapter {{ch.hemisphere}}).

![The dome two trees make.](plate-two-trees-dome.png)

One log at a time, {{cut.splits}} rip cuts to a section, and the frame of a
house comes off the land it will stand on. The next chapter is why splitting
