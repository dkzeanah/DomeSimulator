# 25. Hubs, or No Hubs

This is the decision every dome builder makes, usually without noticing they
have made it, and this entire method is one answer to it.

![Hubs, or no hubs.](plate-framing.png)

## The two ways

**Hubbed.** Every edge of the geodesic mesh is one strut. Where five or six
edges meet, their ends all arrive at the same point, and a connector holds
them there.

* Fewest members: {{dome.edges}} struts rather than {{dome.members}}.
* Least timber, by a long way.
* But the hub is now the whole engineering problem. It has to hold five or
  six sticks at five or six different angles, in three dimensions, and it has
  to do it {{dome.vertices}} times.

**Hubless.** Every panel is a closed triangle of its own three members, and
panels meet panels rather than sticks meeting sticks.

* Nearly twice the timber — {{dome.members}} members for {{dome.edges}} edges.
* But there is no connector to design, buy, machine or get wrong.
* And a panel is a *thing*: it can be built flat, checked flat, sheathed and
  lifted as a finished unit.

## Why the hub is worse than it looks

The strut count makes hubbed framing look obviously cheaper, and on a
materials list it is.

The hub's cost is not on the materials list. It is:

* **Design.** Angles that are specific to the frequency, the vertex type and
  the strut section.
* **Fabrication.** Either bought, which is expensive per unit, or made, which
  needs a machine most people do not have.
* **Tolerance.** The one place in a triangulated frame where error *does*
  accumulate — five sticks whose ends all want the same cubic inch, and if
  they are each a little long the hub cannot close.
* **Time at height.** A hubbed dome goes up stick by stick, up a ladder, with
  somebody holding things.

Chapter {{ch.lumpy}} argued that a dome forgives error. That is true of the
shell and untrue of the hub, and the hub is the place a hubbed dome's
precision requirement lives.

## What the duplicated timber buys

Everything in Chapter {{ch.jig}}.

The extra members are what make a panel a closed object. A closed object can
be made on a bench at waist height, in a shed, in February, by one person, and
checked before it goes anywhere. Then the site work is lifting
{{dome.panels}} finished things into place instead of assembling
{{dome.edges}} sticks in the air.

Timber is the cheapest thing in this method — Chapter {{ch.creator}} prices it
against seven alternatives and it wins by a factor of several. Buying more of
the cheapest input to remove the most expensive process is a good trade, and
it is more or less the only trade this book makes.

## The cheapest wall is the one you never build

![Envelope per floor, measured.](plate-efficiency.png)

One more argument from the same family, and it is about the shape rather than
the joint.

A dome encloses a given floor area with less envelope than a box does. Less
envelope is less material, less cladding, less insulation, less surface losing
heat, and less area for water to find a way through.

That saving is permanent and it compounds with everything else: a smaller
envelope means the skin of Chapter {{ch.skin}} — which is the part this book
says matters most and costs most — is smaller too.

It is measured rather than asserted, across the whole catalogue, in the
figure above.

## When to take the hub anyway

Be fair to the other side.

If you are working in steel or aluminium, the hub is a bracket and a bolt and
none of the above applies — the machine that makes it is a drill press. If
you are buying a kit, somebody else has solved the hub and you are paying for
it once. If the dome is very large, the timber duplication stops being cheap.

The hubless argument is strongest exactly where this book is: **timber, split
rather than sawn, one person, no shop.** Outside that, it is a real trade with
two defensible sides.
