# 28. One Watertight Layer, and Only One

Put a waterproof layer on the outside of a building and you have kept the rain
out.

Put a second one on the inside of the same wall and you have built a bag.

![The frame with its skin on.](with-skin.png)

## The design this replaces

The obvious shell is a waterproof membrane *under* the panels and a
rain-slick cap *over* them, with the insulation between the two. Two skins,
belt and braces.

It is a vapour trap. Water gets into every wall — from the weather, from the
air inside, from somebody's breath — and the only question a wall design
answers is where it goes next. A wall with an impermeable layer on both sides
has no answer. Moisture arrives, cannot leave in either direction, and
condenses on whichever surface is colder that night.

So: **one watertight layer, on the outside, like a shower cap** — and under
it, a route for vapour to leave. The rest of this chapter is that route.

## The stack, outside in

**The cap.** One continuous rain-slick layer over the entire shell. Welded
seams, no fasteners through it, strapped down to ground anchors at the base
vertices. Its only job is liquid water and it is very good at that job because
it is doing nothing else.

**A vented gap.** Three quarters of an inch between the cap and what is under
it. This is the part people skip and it is the part that makes the rest work:
any moisture that gets past the cap, or arrives from inside, has somewhere to
go and something moving through it to take it away.

**The breather.** A vapour-permeable sheet at around ten US perms. Not a poly
sheet. It stops bulk water and lets vapour through, which is the exact
opposite of what a waterproof membrane does and is why the distinction matters
more than the price difference.

**The quilted layers.** As many as you have. Chapter {{ch.hats}}.

**The panels.** Wood. Structural, not weatherproof, and not asked to be.

**The frame.** Dry, because everything above it is doing its job.

## Which way the vapour goes

Warm air holds more water than cold air. Inside your building the air is warm
and full of you, your cooking and your breathing. Outside it is cold.

So vapour moves outward through the wall, and it will condense at whatever
point in the wall is below its dew point. The design question is: **can it get
out before it gets cold?**

With a breather and a vented gap, yes — vapour passes through the permeable
layer into moving air and leaves. With a second impermeable layer, no. It
stops at the membrane, at a temperature partway through the insulation, and
condenses there, inside the insulation, where you will not see it for years.

That is the whole argument, and it is why the cap is the only waterproof thing
in the building.

It is not exotic. It is a wood-cored composite skin -- a sheet core, chopped
strand mat, stitched biaxial cloth, laminating resin, and gelcoat on the weather
face -- which is how small boats have been built for sixty years, from products
you can order this afternoon. In the stem-cell product of Chapter
{{ch.stemcell}} it latches to the pad with over-centre catches, the kind that
seal a preserving jar, and lifts off whole from a ring laminated into its rim.
So it can come off. What it cannot do is change: a hull is one stiff, finished
shape, so the quilted layers of Chapter {{ch.hats}} have no room to grow under
it, and when it fails it fails as a repair on a bonded laminate rather than as a
component you unstrap and replace.

## The three things a cap has to get right

**It has to be one piece, or welded.** A cap made of overlapping panels is
just a roof with more seams, which is the problem it exists to remove.

**It has to be strapped, not fastened.** Every fastener through a waterproof
layer is a hole in a waterproof layer. Straps over the outside to anchors at
the base put the load into the ground without putting anything through the
membrane.

**It has to be bigger than it needs to be.** Chapter {{ch.hotswap}}: a cap cut
to fit a bare shell will not go over a shell wearing two quilted layers.

## If you skin it in panels anyway

<!-- concept: build/skin -->
<!-- concept: build/shelter_nest -->

Some builders will sheathe the frame panel by panel and waterproof the panels,
and the rules for that are old ones. Start at the rim and work up, so each panel
laps over the one below it like a shingle and sheds water downhill. Every corner
where several panels meet is a leak point: flash or tape each one before the next
course covers it. And give the base a real drip edge that throws water clear of
whatever the dome stands on. A dome almost never fails structurally; it fails at
its seams.

For a small dome the panels can be the whole building. Cut the {{dome.panels}}
triangles as solid sheet, pair the congruent ones into parallelograms and
strip-pack them: on one {{skin.nest_sheet}} sheet the largest dome that fits is
{{skin.nest_d}} inches across, with {{skin.nest_floor}} square feet of floor. A
shelter, a kennel, a seed store. Not a house, and it does not pretend to be.

## One shell, any skin

The bones do not care what is painted on them. The same panels and the same
{{dome.members}} sticks wear a baseball, a basketball or a disco ball with a
facet on every face. And be honest about what a "wall module" is: plywood,
insulation and sheet metal, in a frame that holds them. The value is not in the
materials, which anybody can buy. It is in the socket they drop into (Chapter
{{ch.frames}}).

## The part that is not solved

Ultraviolet.

A rain-slick outer layer sits in the sun for its whole life, and that is what
eventually finishes it — not water. The cap is a consumable on a decade-ish
timescale, and the design's answer is that it is a consumable **by design**:
unstrap it, pull it off, put the next one on, in a day, with the building
occupied throughout.

That is a genuinely different answer from a roof, and it is the right one for
a component whose failure mode is known and gradual. It is still a cost, it is
still a thing you will do four or five times in the life of the frame, and
this book would rather say so than let you find out in year twelve.
