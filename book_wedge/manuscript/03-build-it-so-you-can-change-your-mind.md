# 3. Build It So You Can Change Your Mind

The two layers that matter most are the two layers you can put off.

That sounds like a consolation and it is actually the design. A frame is a
commitment: {{dome.members}} members, cut to two lengths, and once the first
panel is closed the shape of the building is decided. A skin is not. A floor
is not. Those are things the building *wears*, and a building can change what
it wears.

![New insulation? Swap it. Solar panels? Add them.](plate-swap-everything.png)

## The condition

It only works if you build for it, and the decisions are all made in the first
week, before there is a building to look at.

None of them costs anything meaningful at the time. All of them are
effectively impossible to add later.

## The four things

**Threaded inserts in the wedge faces, not screws into end grain.**

A panel that will be taken off and put back needs somewhere to bolt to, and
end grain is not it — the second time you drive a screw into the same hole it
is holding about half of what it held the first time, and the fourth time it
is holding nothing. An insert set into the flat sawn face of a wedge is a
machine thread in a piece of metal. It does not care how many times you use
it.

This is the single highest-leverage decision in the build, and it happens
while the panel is still flat on the jig.

**A cap sized a layer larger than it needs.**

The outer skin goes over whatever is under it, so its size depends on how much
is under it. A cap cut to fit a bare shell will not go over a shell wearing
two quilted layers, and you find that out on the day you want to add the
second layer, in November.

Chapter {{ch.hats}} has the arithmetic — each hat is a size up, and the
sequence is not one number. Buy one size up from what you need on day one.

**A floor that clamps rather than is built in.**

A floor framed into the base ring is part of the structure. A floor that is a
hub, radial spokes and decking, clamped to something in the middle, is a
component — it can come out, go back, be replaced with a better one, or be
left behind.

It also turns out to be the thing that makes a floating dome possible at all,
which was not the reason for doing it.

**Services in the seam channel, not in the wall.**

The seam leaves a gap that has to be filled with something. Fill it with a
hose and the building's plumbing is in its structure, reachable from outside
by taking off one panel, and no pipe has ever been buried in anything.

An A seam's channel takes a line up to {{seam.hose_a}} inches across. That is
not a tight fit for the things a small building actually needs.

## What deferring costs

Honestly: some money, later, in exchange for less money now.

The standard article in this book's cost model lists at ${{money.price}},
built to ${{money.cost}}. The shell inside that is ${{money.cap}}. The bare
version of the same cap — no quilted layers at all — is ${{money.cap_bare}},
and each layer you add afterwards is a few tens of dollars of material and an
evening.

So the deferral is real and it is cheap. What you are buying with the four
decisions above is the right to spend that money in the order your life
actually allows, rather than in the order a building would prefer.

## The failure this prevents

I have watched somebody build a beautiful frame, run out of money, put a
tarpaulin over it for a winter, and lose the frame.

Not because the tarpaulin was a bad idea. A tarpaulin is a perfectly good
temporary skin. They lost it because there was nowhere for a real skin to
*attach* when the money came back — no inserts, no strap points, nothing but
a frame that had been designed on the assumption that the skin would be
figured out later by somebody with more time.

Later is not a plan. Later is a thing you design for.

## Where this leaves us

Three chapters in, the argument of this book is complete:

* the frame is allowed to be messy, which is what lets a split log be a
  structural member;
* the skin and the floor are not, because water and comfort do not forgive;
* and the two layers that are not allowed to be messy are the two you can
  postpone, upgrade and swap — if you spend four decisions on it in week one.

The rest of the book is how. It starts where the material does, with a tree.
