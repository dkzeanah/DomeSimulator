# 5. Why I Built the Ugly One

<!-- concept: master/ms_franken_why -->

Everything in the last chapter assumes care. Measured deductions, a jig proved
before it is trusted, a cut list worked out to the thousandth of an inch.

The first dome that taught me anything was the opposite experiment, and I
built it on purpose.

I took whatever the chainsaw had made that week. I skipped the compound cuts
entirely. Where two members met at a seam I folded a flat piece of steel into
a bracket instead of mitring anything. I let nothing land where it was
supposed to, and then I went to see what the geometry would forgive.

![Take whatever the chainsaw made, and see what the geometry forgives.](plate-ugly-one.png)

Here is why that was the right test.

A careful hubless dome replaces the hub with **precision**. Every strut is cut
exactly so that it lands exactly where five or six others land, and the hub
that would have hidden the difference is no longer there to hide it.

The ugly one replaced the hub with **tolerance**. Nothing landed exactly, and
the triangles absorbed it, the way Chapter {{ch.lumpy}} describes.

That difference is the whole experiment. If triangulation only works for
careful people with good timber and a workshop, it is a luxury: a hobby for
people who already own the tools. If it works for salvage and a folded
bracket, it is a way of housing people. The ugly one stood. So the rest of
this book is about how to do on purpose, and well, what that frame did by
accident.

## Who is behind this

<!-- concept: hype/whoami -->

For anyone wondering who is behind this unreasonable quantity of triangles: a
Navy veteran, a programmer and an avionics bench technician, now using the G.I.
Bill to study toward aerospace engineering. The longer version is at the back of
the book. The short version is that a dome, to me, is a home written the way I
would write software -- modular, reusable, built from parts with clean
interfaces so one can change without disturbing the rest -- and this book is
that idea in timber.

## Build one. Then build the next one faster.

<!-- concept: master/ms_close -->

So that is the argument, before any of it is proved.

A shape that encloses the most room for the least material, and so spends less
on skin and gives it back every winter. Two lengths of wood and one flat board.
A method that forgave a chainsaw. A price with a receipt on every line, and a
parts list that does not grow when the house does.

Every number in this book came out of code you can run, and the parts that are
borrowed are named out loud. A sphere encloses the most room with the least
material whether you are a developer or a widow. The geometry does not check
your credit.

Build one. Then build the next one faster.
