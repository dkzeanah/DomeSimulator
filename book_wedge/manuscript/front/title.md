# Forty Hours

The goal is a house built so efficiently that one person can go from standing
timber to a standing shell inside a single working week.

Not a kit. Not a shell delivered on a lorry. A tree, a chainsaw, a froe, and
{{hr.week}} hours.

That is not an aspiration bolted onto the method afterwards. It is where the
number came from, and here is the arithmetic.

## The clock

**Splitting.** One session at the log — {{hr.session}} hours with a meagre
chainsaw — produces {{hr.split_session}} wedges. The frame needs
{{dome.members}}, so every member in the building is
**{{hr.split}} hours** at the log.

**Everything else.** Taken end to end, one member costs about
{{hr.minutes}} minutes from tree to standing: split, crosscut, drilled,
joined, and up in the shell. That is an average over the fast work and the
slow, not a stopwatch on one operation.

    {{dome.members}} members x {{hr.minutes}} minutes  =  {{hr.straight}} hours

Splitting is {{hr.split_share}} per cent of that, which is why Chapter
{{ch.nine}} says the rip is the process worth improving first.

**Getting it wrong.** Add {{hr.redundancy}} per cent for the stick that
splits off the radius, the panel laid down in the wrong order, and the hour
spent looking for the drill.

    {{hr.straight}} hours + {{hr.redundancy}}%  =  {{hr.total}} hours

Which is a {{hr.week}} hour week, with a little in hand.

## The honest part

You will not do it in five days.

This is hard physical work. Splitting a log is not typing; you get perhaps
four good hours at it before the quality of your swing goes, and the quality
of your swing is what keeps a split on the radius. Nobody does forty hours of
this linearly, and anybody who tells you they did was counting the hours and
not the days.

It comes in **spurts**. A weekend at the log. An evening on the bench. A day
when three people turn up and the shell goes from nothing to closed.

That does not weaken the number — it is the reason the number is worth
having. Forty hours of work is a thing you can *schedule* against a life:
five Saturdays, or two weeks of evenings, or one very determined week off. A
building measured in months is a building you never start, because months
cannot be fitted into a year that already has things in it.

So: the forty hours is a **budget of effort**, not a calendar. The point of
counting it is that it is small enough to count.

## And the rest of the book

The shell is a week. The skin and the floor — Chapters {{ch.skin}} and
{{ch.floor}}, the two parts of this building that decide whether it is still
standing in thirty years — are not, and this book spends more pages on them
than on the frame for exactly that reason.

The frame is the part you can do in a week. It is also the part you are
allowed to get wrong.
