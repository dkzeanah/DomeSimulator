# 21. Why a Network Beats a Park

An RV park rents you a slot for a week. This rents you a foundation for a
decade.

That is the whole difference, and everything below follows from it. The
arrangement only works if it is better for the landowner *and* for the person
who arrives with a building, so this chapter works it out twice.

## First, why it is not a caravan site

An RV park sells **nights**. Its costs are turnover: booking, cleaning,
hookup, disconnect, a pitch standing empty out of season, and a tenant who is
leaving from the moment they arrive and behaves accordingly.

A pad sells **years**. It is built once, connected once, metered once, and
occupied by somebody who owns the building standing on it. The revenue per
square foot is lower. The cost per occupied month is very much lower. The
difference is turnover, and turnover is most of what makes short-stay
accommodation a job rather than an income.

The tenant's own building is also the deposit. Nobody abandons a dome.

## What the tenant actually gets

**No land purchase and no mortgage.** The most expensive item in almost every
housing decision is removed, not reduced.

**A building that moves.** Not "portable" in the sense of a caravan — this is
a real timber shell — but demountable. It comes apart into panels, a column, a
floor and a cap, and it goes onto a trailer. The thing you cannot take is the
pad, and the pad is the cheap part.

**Flexibility in how long.** A season or a decade, decided as you go. There is
no twelve-month lease to break because the thing tying you to the place is a
rent on a platform, not a claim on a home.

**Flexibility in where.** This is the part that has no equivalent. If the work
moves, or the climate turns, or the neighbours do — you take the house. In
every other arrangement on that table, moving means losing either a deposit or
a building.

**A floor that is better than the pitch.** An RV park gives you a slab and a
hookup. A pad in this arrangement is a prepared, levelled, serviced platform
sized for the building that is going on it, and the dome's own floor clamps to
its own column.

## Because those two lines are not the same kind of thing

The short-let line is given every night of the year full, which nobody
achieves. Halve the occupancy and it halves.

It also requires the host to own a *building* — to build it, insure it,
furnish it, clean it three times a month forever, hold a reserve against
damage, renovate it every year, and answer the phone when the heating fails at
eleven at night. That is not an investment. It is a small business, and the
income in the table is the pay for running it.

The pad is an annuity. ${{net.pad_upfront}} once, ${{net.pad_net}} a year net,
and the work is reading a meter and sending an invoice. The tenant owns the
building, so the tenant fixes the building.

The honest framing is: **a short let earns more per pound of effort at the
start and a pad earns more per hour of attention forever.** Which of those you
want is a question about your life, not about your land, and this book is not
going to pretend the money answers it.

## What the host is really selling

Not land. A prepared, serviced, level place to put a building — which is the
expensive, skilled, permit-heavy part of building anywhere.

Doing that once and having it used for thirty years by a succession of owners
is a far better use of the effort than doing it once per owner. The pad is
also worth something while it is empty, which bare ground is not: it is an
improvement with a rent attached to it.

## Why a network and not a park

One host with one pad is a transaction. The thing that makes it a network is
that the pads are **the same**.

A dome built to this book's geometry fits any pad built to this book's
specification, which means a tenant can move between them without modifying
anything, and a host can take any tenant without knowing in advance which dome
is coming. Chapter {{ch.pad}}'s clean split — the host owns what is attached to
the ground, the tenant owns what can be carried — is what makes that
interchangeability possible, because nothing straddles the line and so nothing
has to be negotiated.

That is the same argument as Chapter {{ch.nine}}, applied to land instead of
labour: a short, fixed interface is what lets a thing scale.

## What would have to be true

This chapter is a design possibility, not a tested arrangement. Four things
would have to hold, and none of them is proven:

**Zoning would have to permit it.** A demountable dwelling on a leased pad is
not a category most jurisdictions have. Some will treat it as an RV, some as a
permanent dwelling, some as neither. This is the single largest risk and it is
entirely local.

**The rates would have to survive contact.** ${{net.pad_rent}} a month for a
serviced pad is a declared assumption against local RV and storage rates, not
a price anybody has charged.

**Enough domes would have to exist.** A network of pads with nothing to stand
on them is a field of concrete. The first hosts carry that risk, which is why
the model's test build is three pads and not thirty.

**Somebody would have to underwrite the first year.** A host who builds a pad
on the strength of a book deserves better than hope, and the park model
carries a twelve-month manufacturer guarantee for exactly that reason.

State those four plainly and the argument is still a good one. Leave them out
and it is a brochure.
