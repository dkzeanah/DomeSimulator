# 19. The Floor Is the Other Thing to Get Right

You will spend more hours in contact with the floor than with any other part
of this building, and it is the only part in permanent contact with the
ground.

Both of those are reasons to spend money here rather than on the frame.

## What "right" means

**Dry.** The ground is wet. It is wet under a slab, under a deck, and under
gravel, and it will move moisture upward into anything that touches it unless
something stops it. A capillary break — gravel, a membrane, an air gap — is
not optional and it is cheap at the time and impossible afterwards.

**Warm underfoot.** A cold floor makes a warm room feel cold, because you are
touching it and you are not touching the air. This is worth more comfort per
dollar than the same money spent on the walls.

**Flat and stiff.** You notice a floor that flexes every single day.

**Level, to a few millimetres.** Not because the dome needs it — the dome
would stand on a slope — but because everything you put in the dome needs it.

The gravel base at ${{money.gravel}} is not a floor. It is a prepared ground
that a floor can go on. It is in the list because it is what a lot of people
actually start with, and it is honest to say that it is a starting point
rather than a cheap answer.

## The thing that surprises people

Half a pour is not cheaper than a whole one.

The concrete ring with a timber middle costs *more* than the full slab. The
saving on concrete is smaller than the cost of the timber deck that replaces
it, and the ring's formwork is fiddlier per cubic yard than a slab's.

![Meeting the ground: piers, a ring beam, or a slab.](plate-meeting-the-ground.png)

This is in the book because the intuition is so strong and so wrong, and
because the model prints the number that does not flatter the argument.

## The floor that clamps

![The mast through the column, and the floor that clamps to it, sitting on the base ring where a floor goes.](mast-and-floor.png)

There are two ways to build a floor in a dome.

**Framed into the base ring.** Joists between the ring segments, decking over.
Simple, strong, and permanent: it is now part of the structure and removing it
is a demolition.

**A hub, spokes and decking.** A steel hub in the middle, radial spokes out to
the base ring, timber over the top. It is a component. It comes out, it goes
back, it can be replaced with a better one in ten years, and it can be left
behind when the dome moves.

Chapter {{ch.hotswap}} argues for the second on general principles — the
floor is one of the two layers that matters, so keep it changeable. It also
turns out to be what makes Chapter {{ch.floating}} possible, which was not the
reason for doing it.

The hub wants something in the middle to clamp to. In this design that is the
utility column, which is already there, already centred, and already the one
thing running floor to apex.

## Under-floor is storage

A deck on piers has a void under it. That void is dry, it is out of the way,
and it is the cheapest cubic footage in the building.

Design for access to it from inside — a hatch, not a crawl from outside — and
it is the difference between a small building that feels cramped and one that
does not.

## What this chapter will not tell you

Frost depth, soil bearing, seismic anchorage.

Those are local, they are the reason foundations are the part of a building
most tightly bound by code, and this book does not know where you are. The
five constructions above are priced take-offs of a design; what depth your
piers go to is a question for somebody who has stood on your ground.
