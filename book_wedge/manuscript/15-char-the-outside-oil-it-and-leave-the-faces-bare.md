# 15. Char the Outside, Oil It, and Leave the Faces Bare

Every wedge has three faces, and only one of them wants a finish.

The two sawn faces are a joint. One of them butts into the side of the next
member in the panel (Chapter {{ch.pinwheel}}); both of them meet their
neighbours' faces across a seam; and they are the walls of the channel the
seam's air runs down (Chapter {{ch.seam_module}}). Anything you put on them
changes the fit of the key and slows the one thing the channel is there to do,
which is carry water out of the wood.

The third face is the round one: the bark face, turned out toward the weather.
That is the face that stands in the sun and rain for the weeks the frame is up
before its cap goes on, the face that lives in the vented gap under the cap
afterwards, and the face a frame without a cap shows the world. It gets the
finish.

![Round face out, point in: the only face the weather ever sees.](plate-bark-face.png)

The whole frame has {{wood.bark_m2}} square metres of bark face and
{{wood.sawn_m2}} of sawn face. The finish in this chapter goes on the smaller
number.

## Charring the round face

Charring wood is old. The Japanese name for it, yakisugi, means burnt cedar,
and the idea is simple: a layer of char on the surface has nothing left in it
for fungus or insects to eat, sheds water, and does not break down in sunlight
the way bare wood does. It is also a finish that costs a tank of gas.

1. **Char before you build**, with the wedges on bearers on bare ground, well
   away from the stack, the chips and the sawdust. Never char a standing frame.
2. **Use a propane torch with a wide head**, and work along the bark face in
   one steady pass, holding the flame until the surface cracks into a fine
   alligator pattern. Stop there; a deep char just crumbles away.
3. **Keep the flame off the sawn faces and the ends.** Stop short of the two
   sawn edges so they stay crisp. The key, the butt joint and the bolts all
   bear on those faces.
4. **Brush it.** A stiff brush along the grain takes off the loose soot and
   leaves the hard char. Brush more for a lighter, textured face; less for a
   darker one.
5. **Let it cool and blow it off** before any oil goes near it.

A single pass of about {{wood.char_mm}} millimetres of char takes
{{wood.char_loss}} percent off each member's cross-section, all of it from the
bark face, which is the part of the section furthest from the seam. That is the
number that does not help, and it is small: the frame's strength argument in
Chapter {{ch.lumpy}} does not rest on the last few millimetres of the bark side.

**Char is not fireproofing.** A charred surface is slower to catch than a bare
one, and that is all it is. Do not count it in anything to do with fire.

## Oiling it

Linseed oil is pressed from flax seed, and it does not dry by evaporating: it
takes up oxygen from the air and sets into a tough film inside the surface of
the wood. Over char, it binds the char so it stops dusting off on your hands,
and it deepens the colour.

- **Raw or boiled.** Raw linseed soaks in deeper and sets slowly, over days.
  Boiled linseed has driers added so it sets in a day or so. Over char, either
  works; raw is the traditional choice and the more forgiving.
- **Thin coats, wiped off.** Brush or rag it on, let it soak for twenty
  minutes, then wipe off everything that has not gone in. A thick coat that sits
  on the surface stays tacky for weeks and collects dust.
- **{{wood.coats}} coats**, a day or more apart.

How much the frame takes, at a declared {{wood.oil_rate}} square metres a litre
on rough or charred wood:

    bark faces only, {{wood.coats}} coats      {{wood.oil_l}} litres
    every face, {{wood.coats}} coats           {{wood.oil_all}} litres

Oiling everything would take more than three times the oil to finish surfaces
that should not be finished at all.

**What oil will not do.** Linseed is a food for mildew in wood that stays damp.
On a bark face that dries between wettings, that does not matter. On a sawn
face inside a seam, where the whole point of the channel is to move moisture,
a film of oil slows the drying and feeds the mould. Leave the faces bare until
the frame is dry, and then leave them bare anyway.

## Oily rags catch fire on their own

**A rag soaked in linseed oil can set itself alight.** The same reaction that
sets the oil gives off heat, and a crumpled rag holds that heat in until it
reaches the point where cloth burns. It happens hours later, in a bin, with
nobody there.

Lay used rags out flat, one layer thick, outdoors, until they are stiff. Or
drop them in a metal can full of water with a lid on. Never put them in a pile,
a bag or a bin with anything else, and never leave them in the shop or in the
frame.
