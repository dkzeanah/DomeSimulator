# End geometry

What happens at the end of a member is more important than how long the member
is.

A length that is out by a sixteenth makes a slightly loose joint. An end angle
that is out by a degree makes a joint that does not sit down, and a panel that
will not lie flat, and forty panels that will not agree with each other.

WHAT THE END ACTUALLY HAS TO DO

In the pinwheel joint, each member does two different things at its two ends.

At one end it butts into the side of its neighbour. That end needs a flat face
cut square across the stick, at the angle the neighbour presents.

At the other end it receives a butt from a different member. That end needs a
flat face on its side for the neighbour to land on.

So every stick has a butting end and a receiving face, and they are not the
same operation.

THE PART THAT IS EASIER THAN IT SOUNDS

Here is the good news, and it is the reason this method exists.

Every one of those cuts is a plain angle. No compound mitre. No bevel and
mitre at the same time. No holding a workpiece at two angles at once while a
blade comes at it.

You are making flat cuts across a stick at a stated angle, which is the thing
a mitre saw does well and a person can check with a protractor.

That is the trade the wedge method makes. It uses more wood -- eighty percent
more than a dome that shares its struts -- and in return every cut is a cut
you can make accurately at two in the afternoon on your fourth day.

THE FENCES

The jig holds the stick with the cut faces referenced off fences rather than
off measurements.

A fence is a physical stop. You put the stick against it and the cut is right,
or the stick does not fit against it and you know immediately. That is a much
better failure mode than a measurement, which can be wrong quietly.

Every cut in this method should be referenced off a fence. If you find
yourself measuring and marking a cut angle with a bevel gauge, you are doing
it the way the method is trying to save you from.
