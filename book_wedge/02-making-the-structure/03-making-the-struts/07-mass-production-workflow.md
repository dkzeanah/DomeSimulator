# Mass-production workflow

Forty panels is a production run, not a project. Treat it like one and it goes
quickly. Treat it like forty separate builds and it does not.

THE ORDER THAT WORKS

Do every operation on every piece before moving to the next operation.

Split all the wedges. Then cut all the A members to rough length. Then all the
Bs. Then do every butting end. Then every receiving face. Then assemble.

This feels slower than building a panel at a time. It is substantially faster,
for three reasons.

You set the jig once per operation instead of forty times. Setup is the
expensive part of any cut; doing it once for sixty sticks rather than once per
panel removes almost all of it.

Your hands learn. The tenth identical operation is faster than the first and
the fiftieth is faster again. That only happens if you do them in a run.

Errors show up in batches. If a fence is wrong you find it on the third stick
of an operation, not on the third panel -- and you have three to redo instead
of a panel to take apart.

THE STATIONS

Set up the shop as stations rather than as a bench.

* the splitting station, where logs become sectors
* the length station, where sectors become members
* the joint station, where members get their ends
* the assembly station, where members become panels
* the stack, where finished panels live

Material moves one way through those. Nothing goes backwards. If you find
yourself carrying a stick back to a previous station, something is wrong with
the sequence and it is worth stopping to fix.

WHERE THE TIME ACTUALLY GOES

Not where you expect.

The cuts are quick. Splitting is quick once you have done a few. What takes
the time is handling -- picking things up, putting them down, finding the one
you want, deciding which pile it goes in.

So the highest-value thing in the whole shop is not a better saw. It is a
place to put things down that keeps them sorted.

Two racks, labelled, within reach of the saw, will save you more hours than
any tool you could buy.

THE REALISTIC SCHEDULE

The reference build assumes roughly 135 hours of labour for the frame,
including splitting the timber.

That is not a weekend. It is three or four solid weeks of evenings, or two
people for a week and a half. Anybody who tells you a dome frame goes up in a
weekend is talking about a kit with the parts already cut.
