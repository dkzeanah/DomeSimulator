# Ripping

Ripping is cutting along the grain. Everything that turns a log into a wedge
is a rip.

THE DIFFERENCE THAT MATTERS

A crosscut goes across the fibres and is over quickly. A rip goes along them,
and the fibres want to steer the blade.

That means rips wander. A rip that starts true can finish an eighth of an inch
off, and in green timber with tension in it, more.

Everything in this chapter is a way of fighting that.

BLADE

Use a rip blade -- fewer teeth, bigger gullets, aggressive hook. A crosscut
blade will do it and will burn, bind and wander.

Sharp matters more than anything else. A dull blade in a rip cut is how
kickback happens, and green softwood dulls a blade faster than dry hardwood
because of the resin.

FEED RATE

Slower than feels right at first, then steady.

The mistake is varying the feed. A rip cut that speeds up and slows down
produces a wandering line, because the blade deflects differently under
different load. Pick a rate you can hold for the whole cut and hold it.

TENSION

Green logs have internal stress. As you cut into one it can close on the
blade, or spring open, and both are dangerous.

Use a riving knife or a wedge in the kerf behind the blade. Always. Not for
the first few cuts until you see how the log behaves -- from the first cut,
because the one that closes is the one you did not expect.

If a cut starts to pinch, stop, back out, and wedge it open before continuing.

THE ORDER

Cut the log in half first, then quarters, then eighths.

Halving a round log is the hardest cut because there is no flat face to
reference. Once you have a flat, every subsequent cut has something to sit on,
and the whole job gets easier as it goes.
