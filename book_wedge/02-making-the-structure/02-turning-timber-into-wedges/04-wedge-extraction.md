# Wedge extraction

Extraction is getting a usable member out of a sector.

The sector is 45 degrees of a tapering cone. The member needs to be a
consistent cross-section over its length. Those are not the same thing, and
the difference is the work.

THE TAPER PROBLEM

A sector from a log that is twelve inches at the butt and eleven at the top is
wider at one end.

For a six-foot member that difference is about half an inch across the width.
Small enough to live with in a frame; too big to ignore when you are trying to
make forty identical panels.

Two ways to handle it.

TRIM TO THE SMALL END

Take every sector down to the cross-section of its narrow end.

Consistent members, predictable panels, and you throw away the difference.
Simplest and most wasteful.

USE IT ORIENTED

Keep the taper and decide, once, which way it faces.

Every member has its fat end toward the vertex, say, and its thin end toward
the middle of the edge. Every panel is then identically tapered and the panels
still match each other, because they are all wrong in the same direction.

More wood kept, more discipline required, and completely dependent on doing it
the same way 120 times.

The reference build trims to the small end, because the first dome is not the
place to be clever. Once you have built one, the oriented method is worth the
saving.

WHAT COMES OFF

The head end. Where the member meets a vertex, material has to come off to
make room for the joint.

That offcut is not scrap -- it is the right size for blocking, for the jig
itself, and for the keys that fill the seams. Keep a box of it.

THE RULE

Extract every member the same way, in the same order, referencing the same
faces.

A sector is an irregular object and there are four different ways to set it on
a bench. Three of them are wrong for your cut and all four look reasonable.
Mark the reference face on every sector as it comes off the splitting station
and always put that face down.
