# Log division

Splitting a log into eight is the operation the whole method rests on.

Eight sectors of 45 degrees each. Those 45 degrees are what the dome's
dihedral angles come out of, which is covered in the geometry chapter and is
worth reading before you cut.

WHY EIGHT

Not because the geometry demands it.

Eight is what a person can do accurately. Halving is easy -- you find the
centre and cut through it. Halving again is easy. Halving a third time gives
you eight, and each cut references a flat face that already exists.

Six or twelve means cutting angles that are not repeated halvings, and that
means measuring rather than referencing.

The reference build uses eight because of that, not because 45 degrees is
special. If you can hold twelve accurately, twelve works and gives you thinner
sticks.

FINDING THE CENTRE

Not the centre of the bark. The centre of the wood.

A tree is not round and the pith is not in the middle. Find the pith at both
ends, mark it, and run your first cut through both marks. That is the cut that
makes the other seven work.

Get this one wrong and every sector is a different size, and you will not find
out until you are assembling.

THE SEQUENCE

* find the pith at both ends, mark it
* cut through both marks: two halves
* each half, cut through the pith again: four quarters
* each quarter, cut through the pith again: eight sectors

Every cut after the first has a flat face to run against a fence, which is why
this gets easier rather than harder.

WHAT YOU GET

Eight sectors, each a 45-degree wedge in cross-section, each with two sawn
faces meeting at the pith and one curved bark face on the outside.

The sawn faces are the ones that matter. They are what meets the neighbouring
member at a seam, and the angle between them is the 45 degrees the geometry
uses.

The bark face is the outside of the dome. Leave it or take it off -- the
method does not care structurally, but bark holds moisture and insects, so
most people take it off.
