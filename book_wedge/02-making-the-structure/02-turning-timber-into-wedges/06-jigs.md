# Jigs

A jig is a fixture that holds a workpiece in a known position so a cut lands
in the right place without being measured.

In this method, the jig is the method. One flat jig turns 120 irregular
split-log sectors into forty identical panels, and without it the whole
approach falls apart.

WHAT IT DOES

It holds three members in the relationship they will have in a finished
triangle, and presents each joint to the saw at the right angle.

You do not measure the joint. You put the stick against the fences, and the
joint comes out right because the fences are in the right places.

WHY THAT MATTERS SO MUCH HERE

The members are not rectangular. They are wedges, with a curved face and two
sawn faces at 45 degrees, and no two are exactly alike because they came out
of a real tree.

You cannot reference an irregular object off itself. You have to reference it
off something known, and the jig is that something.

THE FENCES

The jig in the tool has coloured fences and they do different jobs:

* the rail sets the stick's point-axis across the panel
* the red fence is the sawn face that must land on the seam side
* the green fence is the sawn face the next butt lands on
* the magenta plate is the head-end flush-cut fence
* the cyan plate is the receiving face the next member's butt meets
* the clamp holds the stick down on rail and fences

Six references, and every one of them is a physical stop rather than a number.

BUILD THE JIG FIRST

Before any member is cut to final size.

The jig is made out of offcut from the splitting, so it costs nothing but
time, and it is the thing every other operation depends on. A jig that is a
degree out makes 120 members a degree out.

Build it, check it, and check it against the tool's own drawings rather than
against your expectations.

THE TEST

Make one panel. If all three corners close and the triangle sits flat on the
floor, the jig is right.

If they do not, the jig is wrong, and it is wrong now -- before it has made
119 more.
