# Labeling

Label everything, as it is made, with something that survives.

This is the least interesting section in the book and skipping it will cost
you a day.

WHAT NEEDS A LABEL

Every member: which family it is, A or B.

Every member: which face is the receiving face. This is not visible once the
stick is out of the jig, and fitting a member with its receiving face on the
wrong side mirrors the panel.

Every panel: which triangle it is, AAA or BAB, and which way is up. A BAB
panel fitted upside down looks nearly right.

Every sector, before it becomes a member: which face is the reference face,
and which end was the butt of the tree.

HOW TO MARK

One line for A, two lines for B. Not the letters -- they look alike upside
down in bad light at the bottom of a pile, and you will be reading them from
across the shop.

Use a lumber crayon or a keel, not pencil. Pencil disappears off rough sawn
timber the first time it rains or gets handled.

Mark on the end grain where possible. It is the part you see when the stick is
in a stack.

WHEN TO MARK

As the piece comes off the saw. Not at the end of the batch.

"I will know which pile is which" is true for about four minutes.

THE PANEL LABEL

Once a panel is assembled, write its number on it -- and write it on the
inside face, because the outside is going to be covered.

The tool numbers the panels and the fabrication package prints the list. Use
those numbers rather than inventing your own, so that when something does not
fit you can describe it to somebody in terms they can look up.

THE COST OF NOT DOING THIS

A pile of 120 unlabelled split-log members all look broadly similar, and
telling a 72.000 inch chord from a 63.670 inch one by eye is harder than it
sounds when both sticks are tapered, irregular, and lying at an angle.

You will end up measuring all of them. That is the day it costs.
