# Plates

A plate is a flat piece of metal spanning a joint, fastened to both members.

The wedge method does not need them. This section is about when you might want
them anyway.

WHY THE METHOD DOES NOT NEED THEM

The pinwheel joint carries its load through wood in compression. A plate does
not improve a compression joint -- the wood is already doing the job, and the
plate is along for the ride.

That is the whole point of the method. Hub domes need plates or castings
because their joints are in shear and tension. Pinwheel joints are not.

WHEN TO ADD ONE ANYWAY

Three situations.

Repair. If a member has split at a joint, a plate across it is a reasonable
way to bring it back into service without taking the dome apart.

Uplift. A dome is light and wind gets under it. The base ring joints -- where
the frame meets the floor -- are the ones that see uplift rather than
compression, and those are worth plating or strapping.

Somewhere a load arrives that the geometry did not plan for. A hoist point. A
heavy door frame. A partition carrying a floor above. The frame will take
those loads locally if you give it something to take them with.

WHAT PLATES COST YOU

Visibility. A plate is visible from inside, forever, and a dome interior is
mostly visible.

Thermal bridging, if the plate passes through the insulated envelope.

And a small honesty problem: a dome with plates everywhere is a dome that has
stopped being a wedge dome and started being a bracket dome with wedge-shaped
struts. If you find yourself plating every joint, the method is not working
and the reason is worth finding rather than covering.
