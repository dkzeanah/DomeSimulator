# Dimensional lumber

You can build this dome out of bought lumber. It works, it is faster, and it
costs more.

WHAT IT MEANS FOR THE METHOD

A 2x6 is not a wedge. It is the same width on both faces, so it does not do
the thing the method depends on -- the changing width that lets neighbouring
triangles turn relative to each other.

So if you build from dimensional lumber you have two choices.

Rip the wedge yourself. Take a 2x6 and rip it at an angle so one face is
narrower than the other. You now have a wedge with the profile the geometry
wants, and you have thrown away the offcut.

Or give up the wedge and handle the dihedral at the joint instead, with
shims, bevels or plates. That is no longer the wedge method; it is a
conventional dome built out of conventional lumber, and there are better books
about that.

THE COST

At the time of writing, a 2x6x12 at a big-box store is about fourteen dollars,
and it rips and crosscuts into four 2x3x6 struts. That is three dollars fifty
a stick.

A shared-strut dome needs 65 of them. This method needs 120.

So the frame is about four hundred and twenty dollars in wood if you buy it,
against roughly two hundred and thirty for the same dome built the
conventional way.

That is the honest number and it is not enormous either way. Wood is not what
makes a dome expensive.

WHEN BOUGHT LUMBER IS THE RIGHT ANSWER

When you do not have trees. When you are in a hurry. When the dome is small
and the difference is forty dollars. When you want to build one to learn the
method before you commit a woodlot to it.

There is no purity requirement here. The method works with a bought wedge; it
just does not get the thing that makes it interesting.
