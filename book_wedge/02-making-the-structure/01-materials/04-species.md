# Species

The method does not care much which tree. The building does.

WHAT MATTERS, IN ORDER

Splitting behaviour. Some species split cleanly and predictably along the
grain; some tear, wander, or spiral. This is the property that decides whether
the method is pleasant or miserable, and it is not on any span table.

Rot resistance, if any part of the frame is near the ground or exposed.

Strength, which matters less than people expect, because a triangulated dome
does not load its members heavily.

Weight, which matters when you are carrying 120 of them.

WHAT SPLITS WELL

Straight-grained softwoods. Pine, spruce, fir, cedar. These are the species the
method was developed around and they split predictably with a froe or on a
saw.

WHAT DOES NOT

Anything with interlocked or spiral grain. Elm is famously difficult. Some
eucalyptus. Any tree that grew on a windy slope and twisted as it went.

You can tell a lot by looking at the bark: if the bark runs in a spiral up the
trunk, the grain does too, and your sectors will come out twisted.

THE ROT QUESTION

Cedar, larch, black locust and osage orange are durable. Pine and spruce are
not.

For a frame that is off the ground, dry, and inside an envelope, durability
barely matters -- the reference build uses pine and expects it to last as long
as the roof over it.

For anything in contact with the ground or outside the envelope, use a durable
species or do not use wood.

THE ADVICE

Use what is growing where you are.

A local species you can get for free and can split reliably beats an ideal
species you have to buy and transport. The method is tolerant, and the frame
is not the part of this building that will fail.
