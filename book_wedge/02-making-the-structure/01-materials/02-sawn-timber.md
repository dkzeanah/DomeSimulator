# Sawn timber

Sawn timber -- squared stock from a local mill rather than a big-box store --
sits between dimensional lumber and logs, and for a lot of people it is the
practical middle.

WHAT YOU GET

Bigger sections than a lumber yard carries. Rough sawn rather than planed,
which means the stated size is the actual size, not the dressed size.

Usually green, or air-dried rather than kiln-dried.

Usually local species, which may or may not be what the span tables assume.

And usually cheaper per board foot than dressed lumber, sometimes
substantially.

WHY IT SUITS THIS METHOD

A rough-sawn 6x6 or 8x8 rips into wedges very conveniently, and a mill will
often sell you the slabwood and offcuts that a dressed-lumber supplier does
not have.

The dimensional stability is worse than kiln-dried and that matters less here
than it would in a stud wall. The frame is triangulated and the joints are
compression joints; a member that moves a sixteenth is not a problem.

WHAT TO ASK THE MILL

Moisture content, or at least when it was cut.

Whether they will cut to a given section rather than a standard one. Most
small mills will, and having your wedge profile cut at the mill removes the
hardest operation from your shop.

Whether they have anything that has been sitting. Timber that has been in the
stack for a year is drier and cheaper than fresh, and a mill usually has some.

THE OPTION MOST PEOPLE MISS

Ask them to rip the wedges.

A mill has a big enough saw to split a log into eight accurately in a fraction
of the time it takes you, and they charge by the hour. For the price of a
morning of mill time you can skip the entire splitting chapter.

That is not cheating. The method is about what the wedge does in the building,
not about who cut it.
