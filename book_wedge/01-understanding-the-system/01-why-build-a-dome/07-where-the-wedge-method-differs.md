# Where the wedge method differs

The wedge method changes where the dome's three-dimensional geometry is handled. A conventional timber dome often starts with rectangular struts and then uses hubs, plates, brackets, compound end cuts, or other connection hardware to make those rectangular members meet correctly in space. The wedge method moves part of that problem into the member itself.

Instead of treating every strut as a rectangular stick with parallel inside and outside faces, the timber is shaped so the inside edge is narrower than the outside edge. The member becomes part of the angular transition between neighboring triangular faces. That is the main difference.

The dome still depends on correct geodesic geometry. The struts still have to be the correct lengths. The vertices still have to occur in the correct locations.

The triangles still have to assemble into the intended spherical pattern. The wedge does not replace any of that.

It changes how the physical timber occupies the geometry.

GEOMETRY

A geodesic frame can be thought of as a network of theoretical straight lines joining vertices.

Those lines are the chord geometry.

A conventional rectangular strut is then placed along one of those lines. Its cross-section does not naturally change from the interior side of the dome to the exterior side.

That means the joint has to handle the difference in direction between the triangular face on one side and the triangular face on the other. A wedge-shaped member gives us another way to handle that transition. Viewed in cross-section, the member has an interior width and an exterior width.

The two side faces are therefore not parallel.

That creates an included wedge angle.

If the difference between the outside width and inside width is:

$$ \Delta w = w_{outside}-w_{inside} $$

and the distance between the interior and exterior reference faces is \(t\), then a symmetrical wedge can be described by:

$$ \alpha = 2\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\Delta w}{2t}\right) $$

where:

* \(\alpha\) is the total included wedge angle,
* \(w_{outside}\) is the outside width,
* \(w_{inside}\) is the inside width,
* and \(t\) is the thickness between the inside and outside reference faces.

This equation describes the shape of the wedge. It does not tell us automatically what wedge angle a particular dome needs. That angle depends on the actual dome geometry, the reference line used through the timber, the relationship between neighboring faces, and the connection method.

The important part is that the timber now contains a controlled angular relationship in its own cross-section.

RECTANGULAR MEMBER VERSUS WEDGE MEMBER

Take two neighboring triangular faces. If their common edge is made from a rectangular timber, the member has approximately the same width at the inside and outside of the frame. The two triangular faces still need to turn relative to one another.

That change may be handled with a gap, bevel, plate, hub, bracket, angled fastener arrangement, or some combination of those. Now replace that rectangular member with a wedge. The inside edge is narrower.

The outside edge is wider. The widening cross-section gives the adjoining faces more separation toward the exterior while allowing them to remain closer together toward the interior. Instead of asking the connection to create the entire face-to-face transition, some of that geometry is already built into the timber.

That is the central construction idea behind the wedge method. The member is not only spanning from one vertex to another.

Its cross-section is also doing geometric work.

THE WEDGE IS NOT A HUB

It would be easy to describe the system simply as hubless construction, but that description can be misleading. Removing a traditional central hub does not remove the need for a connection. The members still have to be held together.

The vertex still has to stay in the correct location. Loads still have to pass from one member into the others. The difference is that a large specialized hub does not necessarily have to establish the primary angular relationship between the surrounding faces.

Depending on the version of the system, the remaining connection could use:

* structural screws,
* through-bolts,
* washers,
* internal plates,
* external plates,
* straps,
* brackets,
* direct timber contact,
* or combinations of these.

The wedge method therefore redistributes the geometry rather than eliminating connection design. Some of the work normally done by a hub is moved into the timber.

THE MEMBER BECOMES A REPEATED GEOMETRIC PART

This changes the fabrication problem. In a traditional hub system, the strut can often remain a relatively simple component while the specialized hub handles more of the spatial geometry. In the wedge method, the strut becomes a more specialized component.

That means its manufacturing process matters. If one wedge profile is correct, we want to reproduce that profile instead of laying out every part independently.

A practical production process should eventually define:

* the starting stock,
* the reference face,
* the fence or jig position,
* the workholding method,
* the order of cuts,
* the final length reference,
* the drilling reference,
* the labeling method,
* and the inspection method.

The goal is to stop treating each wedge as a custom carving. It should become a manufactured part. If twenty members are supposed to be identical, the twentieth member should come from the same references and setup as the first.

That is where jigs, fences, stops, templates, and gauges become more useful than repeated measuring.

FABRICATION ERRORS

The wedge creates another set of dimensions that have to remain controlled. A rectangular strut can be the correct length and still have minor width variation without completely changing its basic geometry. With a wedge, the width relationship is part of the intended shape.

If the outside width changes while the inside width stays the same, the wedge angle changes. If the timber is flipped during assembly, the taper works in the opposite direction. If the wrong face becomes the reference during a cutting operation, the same mistake can be reproduced across an entire batch.

The common errors therefore need to be kept separate:

* incorrect strut length,
* incorrect inside width,
* incorrect outside width,
* incorrect wedge angle,
* reversed wedge orientation,
* incorrect end geometry,
* incorrect fastener-hole position,
* incorrect member family,
* and incorrect labeling.

These errors do not all produce the same result. A wrong strut length changes the vertex position. A wrong wedge profile changes how the surrounding faces meet.

A reversed wedge puts the wide side toward the interior instead of the exterior. A misplaced bolt hole can change the effective connection point even when the overall piece of timber is the correct length. This means quality control eventually has to check more than one measurement.

BUILD NOTE

The actual reference-build fabrication procedure should be recorded from the shop rather than invented from the geometry.

For each wedge family, that record should include:

* nominal starting stock,
* actual measured stock dimensions,
* tool used,
* fence or guide setup,
* workholding,
* first cut,
* second cut if required,
* reference face after each cut,
* final length operation,
* drilling operation,
* labeling,
* target dimensions,
* measured finished dimensions,
* and variation across multiple completed parts.

Until those measurements exist, the book can explain how the operation should be organized without claiming a production tolerance that has not been measured. The same rule applies to saw settings. A drawing can show the intended wedge.

The builder-facing procedure needs the actual setup used to reproduce it.

DIMENSIONAL LUMBER

One version of the wedge method starts with ordinary rectangular lumber. The wedge profile is ripped from that rectangular blank. The cutting layout matters because the offcut may represent a large portion of the original board.

That does not automatically mean the offcut is waste. Depending on the final wedge geometry, another useful member may be recovered from it.

The same material might produce:

* another wedge,
* a smaller structural member,
* blocking,
* panel cleats,
* spacers,
* trim,
* or another repeated component.

This makes material yield part of the system. The fastest way to produce one wedge is not necessarily the most efficient way to process all the lumber required for a dome. The better question is how many useful finished parts can be recovered from each starting board while maintaining repeatable reference surfaces.

LOGS AND LARGER TIMBER

The wedge method also creates another possibility when the starting material is a log or large timber. A log is already radial in cross-section. Instead of converting the entire log into rectangular boards first, the log can potentially be divided longitudinally into rough wedge-like sectors.

Those sectors can then be processed into controlled structural members. This does not mean that a raw radial slice from a tree is automatically a finished dome strut.

The original log may have:

* taper,
* sweep,
* knots,
* checks,
* changing diameter,
* moisture variation,
* irregular grain,
* and irregular exterior surfaces.

The rough wedge still needs controlled reference surfaces and verified dimensions.

But the material layout can change substantially.

Instead of:

$$ \text{log} \rightarrow \text{rectangular lumber} \rightarrow \text{wedge} $$

the process can potentially become:

$$ \text{log} \rightarrow \text{rough wedge sectors} \rightarrow \text{finished wedges} $$ That is more than a different connection detail. It changes how the structural material can be recovered from the original timber.

CONNECTION GEOMETRY STILL MATTERS

Shaping the member can reduce how much specialized three-dimensional geometry has to be concentrated at the vertex. It does not make the connection trivial. The connection still has to answer practical questions.

Where is the theoretical vertex? Where is the fastener center? Which timber surfaces actually touch?

Are the wedges clamped directly together? Is there a controlled gap? Does a plate bridge several members?

Can the connection be tightened after erection? Can a damaged member be replaced? Does an exterior panel attach across the same joint?

How does the connection affect the physical cut length compared with the theoretical chord?

Those questions connect the wedge profile to the actual building system.

The two problems therefore have to be developed together:

$$ \text{member geometry} $$ and $$ \text{connection geometry} $$

A good wedge profile attached through a poorly defined connection is not a complete system.

Neither is a good connection attached to incorrectly manufactured wedges.

REPEATABILITY IS THE LARGER IDEA

The wedge is the obvious visual difference, but repeatability is the more important fabrication idea behind the method. A dome may contain a large number of pieces while containing only a relatively small number of unique part families.

Once those families are defined, each one can eventually have:

* a profile,
* a length,
* a drilling pattern,
* a label,
* a manufacturing fixture,
* an inspection gauge,
* and a known location in the finished dome.

That changes the question during assembly.

Instead of asking:

"How do I make this piece fit here?"

we should be asking:

"Which defined part belongs here, and was that part made correctly?" That difference matters. Custom fitting allows one error to be hidden by another adjustment.

Repeatable fabrication makes the error easier to find. If a correctly labeled part does not fit where the geometry says it belongs, something is wrong with the part, the assembly position, the connection, or the underlying model. That gives us a system that can be checked instead of continuously improvised.

DESIGN VARIATION

The wedge concept can also extend into panelized construction. A wedge member can form the perimeter of a triangular panel. The outside face of the wedge can support exterior sheathing while the inside edge defines the interior side of the frame.

Neighboring wedge-framed panels can then meet along their prepared edges. That creates the possibility of prefabricating larger triangular modules instead of assembling every individual strut on site. Other versions could keep the wedge frame permanent while making the exterior panel removable.

Still others could combine the wedge with metal reinforcement or laminated timber. These are variations of the same underlying idea: make the member participate in the dome geometry instead of requiring every spatial relationship to be created entirely by separate hardware.

SAFETY / ENGINEERING

Changing the member shape does not prove structural capacity.

The wedge affects the physical cross-section of the timber and may also affect:

* fastener placement,
* available edge distance,
* bearing surfaces,
* connection geometry,
* bending orientation,
* and the amount of wood remaining at different parts of the section.

Those effects have to be evaluated for the actual material, joint, and loading condition. A wedge that fits the geometry correctly is not automatically a structurally adequate wedge. The same applies to log-derived members.

Material efficiency does not override timber quality, grading, defects, moisture, connection requirements, or structural analysis. The geometry tells us what shape we are trying to build. Engineering determines whether the resulting parts and connections can carry the required loads.

WHERE THE METHOD DIFFERS

The wedge method differs from conventional timber geodesic construction in several connected ways. It makes the shape of the timber part of the three-dimensional dome geometry. It can reduce the amount of specialized geometry concentrated in a traditional hub.

It turns cross-sectional accuracy into part of the fabrication process. It can be produced from rectangular lumber, larger sawn timber, or potentially timber recovered directly from logs. It can form the edge geometry of prefabricated triangular panels.

Most importantly, it gives us a system that can be broken into defined and repeatable manufacturing operations. That is the point where the wedge stops being only a shape.

It becomes a building method.
