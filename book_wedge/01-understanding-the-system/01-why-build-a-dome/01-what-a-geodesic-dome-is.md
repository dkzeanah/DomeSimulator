# What a geodesic dome is

A geodesic dome is a rounded structure built from a network of straight members.

That is the first thing to understand about it.

The finished structure may look curved, but the individual pieces making up the frame do not have to be curved. Instead, straight members are connected into a repeating network of triangles. As more of those triangles are joined together at slightly different angles, the overall frame begins to follow the shape of a sphere.

In practical terms, we are using a collection of flat triangular faces to approximate a curved surface.

A simple way to picture this is to imagine replacing the smooth surface of a ball with a large number of flat panels. A ball made from only a few large flat faces would look obviously angular. Divide those faces into smaller pieces and the shape begins to look progressively rounder.

A geodesic dome works on the same basic idea.

The frame is not one continuous curved beam. It is a collection of straight structural members whose lengths and connection geometry place them at the correct points in space.

This matters to a builder because straight pieces are much easier to produce repeatedly than large curved ones.

A saw can cut another member to the same length.

A jig can hold another piece in the same position.

A stop can reproduce a cut.

A template can reproduce a panel.

The finished building may have a complicated three-dimensional shape, while much of the actual fabrication is reduced to making the same small group of parts over and over.

GEOMETRY

A complete sphere can be described using points, straight lines between those points, and the triangular faces created by those lines. A dome uses only part of that spherical structure.

The exact portion of the sphere used depends on the design.

Some domes are approximately hemispherical. Others stop above or below the halfway point. A dome may also be placed on a vertical wall, a raised base, or another supporting structure.

For that reason, saying that two structures are both "geodesic domes" does not mean they automatically use the same strut lengths, number of triangles, base shape, or connection angles.

The underlying geometry matters.

The frequency of the dome also matters. Frequency describes how far the original triangular faces used to develop the sphere have been subdivided. A higher-frequency dome generally contains more members and smaller triangular faces.

The full frequency calculations and the exact strut families used in this book are covered later. For now, the important point is that the rounded shape is produced by a calculated arrangement of straight pieces rather than by bending the frame into a curve.

The reference structure used throughout this book is a 20-foot-diameter 2V dome.

That description tells us two useful things.

The 20-foot dimension tells us the intended diameter of the reference structure.

The 2V designation tells us the frequency of the geodesic subdivision.

It does not, by itself, tell us every dimension needed to build the dome. The exact sphere geometry, truncation, base configuration, strut lengths, and joint geometry still have to be defined before a reliable cut list can be produced.

That distinction is important throughout this book.

A drawing that looks like a 2V dome is not enough information to start cutting lumber.

The geometry has to be established first.

THE WEDGE METHOD

The wedge method does not change the basic definition of a geodesic dome.

We still have straight members forming triangular faces and those faces joining together to create a rounded three-dimensional structure.

What changes is the shape of the structural member and the way we use that shape at the joints.

A conventional wood dome can begin with rectangular lumber. The builder then has to deal with the fact that neighboring triangular faces do not all lie in the same plane. Their edges meet at angles.

Different dome systems handle that problem in different ways. They may use metal hubs, flattened pipe ends, plates, brackets, compound cuts, flexible connections, or other hardware.

The wedge method approaches the same three-dimensional problem by making the cross-section of the timber itself part of the system.

Instead of every member remaining rectangular from its inside face to its outside face, the member is formed as a wedge.

The narrow portion is oriented toward the interior of the dome and the wider portion toward the exterior.

When those members are assembled into the frame, the wedge shape becomes part of how neighboring faces transition around the curved surface.

That does not mean the wedge automatically determines every connection angle.

It also does not mean that any wedge-shaped piece of lumber can be assembled into a dome.

The strut lengths, triangle geometry, vertex arrangement, member orientation, and connection details still have to agree with one another.

The useful idea is that the shape of the timber can participate in the three-dimensional geometry instead of being treated only as a straight rectangular stick that needs separate hardware to resolve every change in direction.

From a builder's point of view, this gives us two geometries to think about.

The first is the geometry of the dome itself: where every vertex and strut belongs in space.

The second is the geometry of the individual wedge member: its width, thickness, taper, orientation, end treatment, and connection to the neighboring pieces.

The rest of the method comes from making those two geometries work together.

This is also why the wedge method can have several variations.

The starting material could be dimensional lumber.

It could be larger sawn timber.

It could be material recovered from a log by dividing the log longitudinally into usable wedge-shaped sections.

A wedge could be solid, laminated, reinforced, or incorporated into a larger panel system.

Those are different fabrication strategies built around the same basic idea.

DESIGN VARIATION

The frame should also be thought of separately from the outer shell.

A geodesic frame defines the structural geometry. What is attached to that frame can vary.

A dome might use plywood triangles, fiberglass panels, sheet material, transparent sections, insulation assemblies, removable panels, or another enclosure system.

That distinction becomes useful later because changing the enclosure does not necessarily require changing the entire geometric system underneath it.

The same is true for floors, foundations, doors, windows, and utility routing. These systems have to interact with the dome, but they are not what makes the structure geodesic.

At its simplest, the dome can be understood as a three-dimensional framework of calculated straight members.

Everything else is built around that framework.

For this book, that gives us a useful order of operations.

First we establish the dome geometry.

Then we determine what the wedge-shaped member has to do inside that geometry.

Then we work out how to manufacture those members repeatedly.

After that, we can deal with the connections, assembly sequence, openings, shell, floor, foundation, utilities, and the different ways the same basic system can be modified.

The dome may look complicated when viewed as one complete object.

For fabrication, we will break it back down into individual parts.

That is where the system becomes manageable.
