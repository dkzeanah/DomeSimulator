# Hubless or reduced-hardware construction

The wedge method can reduce how much specialized hardware is needed at a dome vertex. That does not mean the structure has no connection system. It means the timber itself is doing more of the geometric work, so the connection does not necessarily have to do all of it.

That is the difference between saying "hubless" and saying "no hardware." A hubless dome can still use bolts, screws, plates, washers, straps, or other fasteners. The useful distinction is that we are no longer depending on one large central hub to establish the entire three-dimensional relationship between the members.

THE TRADITIONAL HUB PROBLEM

In a conventional strut-and-hub dome, several straight members meet at one vertex. Those members arrive from different directions. A hub sits between them and provides the physical geometry that allows those directions to coexist.

That hub may have:

* arms,
* tabs,
* holes,
* plates,
* sockets,
* welded features,
* or other geometry.

The more work the hub has to do, the more specialized the hub becomes. That is not automatically a problem. A well-designed hub can make assembly much easier.

But it also means the dome depends on a manufactured component that may be harder to make than the struts themselves. The wedge method changes that balance. By shaping the timber to participate in the face-to-face geometry, the connector may be allowed to become simpler.

MOVING GEOMETRY INTO THE MEMBER

The wedge gives each strut a controlled inward-to-outward taper. That taper helps define how neighboring triangular faces spread apart. If the member itself is already carrying part of that relationship, then the connector does not have to create the whole angle from scratch.

Conceptually, the system changes from:

$$ \text{rectangular strut}+\text{geometry-heavy hub} $$

to:

$$ \text{geometry-shaped strut}+\text{simpler connection} $$ The exact amount of simplification depends on the final joint. It may be substantial.

It may be modest. The important point is that the geometry can be distributed between the timber and the connection instead of concentrated almost entirely in the hub.

DIRECT MEMBER-TO-MEMBER CONNECTION

One possible form of reduced-hardware construction is direct timber-to-timber connection. In that approach, the wedge members meet physically at the vertex and are held together with fasteners.

The connection could use some combination of:

* structural screws,
* through-bolts,
* washers,
* dowels,
* pins,
* or local reinforcement.

The main advantage is obvious. There is no large fabricated hub occupying the center of the joint. But direct contact also creates new design questions.

Which timber surfaces touch? Do the ends meet fully? Do they stop short of the theoretical vertex?

Is there a controlled gap? Do several members bear against one another? Does the fastener clamp the members together or only locate them?

Can the connection be assembled without forcing the surrounding geometry? A direct timber joint can look simple while still containing several geometric decisions.

PLATE-ASSISTED CONNECTIONS

Another approach uses plates without using a traditional central hub. A flat or formed plate can connect several wedge members while allowing the wedge profiles to establish much of the face geometry.

The plate might exist:

* on the interior,
* on the exterior,
* on both sides,
* between members,
* or locally around a vertex.

That creates a hybrid system. The timber carries part of the geometry. The plate provides restraint, load transfer, alignment, or reinforcement.

This is different from a hub whose arms or sockets establish the full strut direction. The plate may simply connect members that are already shaped to occupy the required geometry. That can make plate fabrication simpler.

It may also make replacement easier because the plate does not necessarily have to be a highly specialized three-dimensional part.

BOLTS

Through-bolts are attractive because the connection can be tightened and disassembled. A bolt also gives us a clear geometric reference. If the design uses bolt centers as the structural datum, then the distance between bolt centers can become the practical reference length between connections.

That can simplify dimensional control.

But the bolt does not solve the whole joint by itself.

We still need to define:

* bolt diameter,
* hole diameter,
* hole location,
* washer size,
* plate geometry if used,
* timber thickness around the hole,
* edge distance,
* member overlap,
* and tightening method.

If several members share one bolt, the stack-up also matters. The physical thickness of every member and washer changes the joint. If each member uses its own fastener, then those individual fastener locations still have to agree with the theoretical vertex geometry.

STRUCTURAL SCREWS

Structural screws offer a different assembly method. They can sometimes avoid the need to drill a complete through-hole across several members. They may also be installed from one side.

That can make field assembly easier in certain joint layouts. But a screw-driven joint still has to be deliberately designed. The screw path matters.

The angle matters. The member thickness around the screw matters. The available edge distance matters.

The receiving timber matters. A screw placed wherever there appears to be enough wood is not a repeatable connection detail. If structural screws become part of the reference build, their position should eventually be jigged or otherwise referenced just like a bolt hole.

HARDWARE REDUCTION SHOULD BE COUNTED

If the goal is reduced hardware, that reduction should eventually be measured instead of described vaguely.

For a reference dome, we can count:

* number of hubs avoided,
* number of plates,
* number of bolts,
* number of screws,
* number of washers,
* number of unique hardware types,
* number of drilled operations,
* and number of fabricated metal parts.

That gives us a real comparison. A system that eliminates forty hubs but replaces them with hundreds of unique steel pieces has not necessarily simplified manufacturing. A system that replaces a complex hub with one repeated plate and one repeated fastener pattern may have.

The useful metric is not:

"Does this system contain hubs?"

The better questions are:

How many unique parts are required? How difficult are those parts to make? How many operations are required?

How repeatable is the connection? How easy is it to assemble and inspect?

That gives us a more realistic definition of simplification.

ASSEMBLY WITHOUT A CENTRAL HUB

Removing a central hub changes the erection process. A hub can act as a convenient collection point. Several struts can be loosely attached to one component and then positioned as the frame grows.

Without that hub, the wedge members may need to be held directly against one another while the connection is made.

That can require:

* temporary clamps,
* assembly jigs,
* temporary screws,
* straps,
* supports,
* or preassembled triangular modules.

A connection that uses less permanent hardware may therefore require more temporary workholding during erection. That is not necessarily a disadvantage. It just needs to be included in the method.

The permanent joint and the assembly fixture solve different problems.

THREE-MEMBER PREASSEMBLY

One way to reduce difficulty at the full dome vertex is to assemble triangles first. Three wedge members can be placed in a flat or controlled fixture and connected into a triangular module. That gives the builder a larger, already-defined unit.

Those triangles can then be brought together at the dome. This moves some of the assembly work away from the final vertex. Instead of positioning every loose strut independently in space, the builder handles repeated modules.

Whether that is better depends on:

* triangle size,
* weight,
* connection access,
* lifting method,
* and the design of the shared edges.

The wedge method can support either individual-strut erection or panel/module assembly. The joint design should be developed with the intended erection method in mind.

FIVE- AND SIX-MEMBER VERTICES

The challenge becomes more obvious at a five-member or six-member vertex.

Several wedge members approach the same region from different directions.

If there is no central hub, the members may:

* meet directly,
* overlap,
* terminate around a shared fastener,
* connect through plates,
* connect in pairs,
* or use another repeated arrangement.

The final geometry has to keep the intended vertex from becoming a random pile of timber ends.

A clean connection should define where every member belongs.

That can be done with:

* drilling jigs,
* vertex plates,
* temporary assembly fixtures,
* indexed holes,
* machined end features,
* or another repeatable locating system.

The less geometry the permanent hardware provides, the more important the fabrication accuracy of the timber becomes.

That is one of the central tradeoffs of the wedge method.

SIMPLER HARDWARE REQUIRES BETTER PARTS

A large hub can absorb some variability. A member may rotate slightly within a connection. A tab may flex.

A bolt hole may provide some installation clearance. When the timber itself is expected to establish more of the geometry, the timber has to be more consistent. That means reduced hardware and increased fabrication precision can go together.

If the wedge profile is wrong, the connection has less ability to hide that mistake. If the length is wrong, the vertex moves. If the end geometry is wrong, several members may no longer meet cleanly.

If a hole location is wrong, a simple plate may no longer fit.

The manufacturing process therefore becomes part of the connection system.

BUILD NOTE

For the reference build, the connection-development record should include more than photographs of the finished joint.

It should document:

* member IDs entering the vertex,
* member orientation,
* contact surfaces,
* connection hardware,
* hole pattern,
* plate dimensions if used,
* assembly order,
* temporary workholding,
* tool access,
* tightening sequence,
* disassembly method,
* and any interference observed during assembly.

That information is what turns a concept into a repeatable joint. A connection that works once after several pieces are forced into place is not yet a production method.

REPLACEABILITY

Reduced-hardware construction can also be designed around replacement. If the structure uses mechanical fasteners instead of permanent adhesive, an individual strut may potentially be removed later.

That could help with:

* damaged-member replacement,
* modification,
* panel access,
* transportation,
* or experimental configurations.

But replaceability has to be designed into the assembly sequence. A member can be bolted in place and still be impossible to remove after neighboring members trap it. A plate can be removable and still be inaccessible after the shell is installed.

So the question is not simply:

"Can the fastener be removed?"

It is:

"Can the entire part be removed from the assembled structure without damaging the surrounding system?" That becomes a useful design criterion if modularity is one of the goals.

DESIGN VARIATION

Several connection levels can exist within the same wedge system. A minimal connection might use direct timber contact and mechanical fasteners. A reinforced version might add small plates.

A heavy-duty version might add larger plates or brackets while still keeping the wedge geometry. A removable prototype might use bolts where a permanent version uses another connection. A panelized version might connect complete triangular modules instead of loose struts.

These are all variations of the same system. The presence of hardware does not make the method stop being a wedge method. The defining feature is that the wedge member still participates in establishing the geometry.

SAFETY / ENGINEERING

The connection is one of the most critical structural parts of the dome. Forces carried by the struts have to pass through the vertices. A reduced-hardware joint therefore cannot be evaluated only by counting how few pieces it uses.

The actual connection may depend on:

* timber bearing,
* bolt shear,
* bolt bending,
* screw withdrawal,
* screw shear,
* plate bending,
* washer bearing,
* hole spacing,
* edge distance,
* group effects,
* splitting,
* moisture movement,
* and the direction of the force relative to the grain.

Those requirements depend on the actual material, fastener, geometry, and loading. This book should not claim that fewer connectors means a stronger or weaker joint without the engineering to support that conclusion. A simple connection is useful only if it is also adequate for the job.

HUBLESS DOES NOT MEAN CONNECTIONLESS

This point is worth keeping explicit. A geodesic dome cannot be made from a group of correctly shaped pieces that merely touch one another. The members still have to act together as a structure.

The wedge can reduce the amount of connection geometry that must be manufactured separately.

It cannot remove the need to transfer forces between members.

So when this book uses the term "hubless," it should mean:

**no conventional centralized geometry-defining hub**

rather than:

**no connection hardware of any kind.** That definition leaves room for bolts, screws, plates, and other practical connection methods while keeping the distinction from traditional strut-and-hub construction clear.

REFERENCE BUILD

For the 20-foot 2V reference dome, the primary connection should eventually be frozen as one repeatable vertex system.

The process should define:

1. where the theoretical vertex lies,
2. where each wedge member terminates,
3. which surfaces make contact,
4. where the fasteners are located,
5. what hardware is required,
6. how the joint is temporarily held during assembly,
7. what order the pieces are installed,
8. how the connection is tightened,
9. how it is inspected,
10. and whether the joint can later be disassembled.

Once that joint exists, the hardware can be counted and compared against a traditional hub system. Until then, reduced-hardware construction should be treated as a design objective rather than a claim that the dome has already eliminated a particular percentage of connectors. The wedge gives us the opportunity to simplify the joint.

The finished connection determines whether that opportunity actually turns into a better construction system.
