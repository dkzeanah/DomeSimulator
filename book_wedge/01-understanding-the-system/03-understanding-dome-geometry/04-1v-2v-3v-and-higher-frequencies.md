# 1V, 2V, 3V, and higher frequencies

Here is what each one actually gives you, in the terms that matter on a build
site rather than in a geometry textbook.

1V

Twenty triangles in a full sphere, ten in a hemisphere. All of them identical.

This is the only frequency where every single strut is the same length, and
that is a genuinely large advantage. One cut list. One jig setting. No A and
B members to keep separated.

It is also barely a dome. The faces are large and flat, the shape is
noticeably faceted, and the span you can cover before the members get absurdly
long is small. Fine for a play structure, a chicken coop, a garden cover.

2V

Forty triangles in the hemisphere the reference build uses. Two strut lengths,
called A and B.

This is the first frequency that looks like a dome and behaves like one, and
it is the last frequency where the parts count is small enough to keep in your
head. Sixty-five edges, twenty-six vertices, 120 physical members once every
triangle owns its own three sticks.

The two lengths are the price of admission. You will cut sixty of one and
sixty of the other and you will mix them up at least once.

3V AND UP

Three strut lengths at 3V, more above that. Rounder, stronger for its weight,
better at spanning distance.

Also: substantially more sticks, more joints, more sorting, and a much higher
cost to any systematic error. If your jig is out by a degree at 2V you will
notice at the last panel. At 4V you will notice at the last twelve panels, and
you will have already cut them.

WHAT THIS BOOK ASSUMES

Everything from here on assumes 2V unless it says otherwise.

Not because 2V is the best frequency. Because it is the frequency where the
wedge method can be explained completely, built by one person, and checked
without a computer -- and because everything that is true about a wedge at 2V
is still true at 3V, with more of it.

Learn the method at 2V. Go up a frequency when the span demands it, not
because higher numbers sound better.
