# Why Wedges

A log is round, and almost everything we build is square. The sawmill settles
that argument in favour of the square: it saws rectangles out of the circle
and sends the curved outside away as slabs, edgings and sawdust. Splitting
settles it the other way. It accepts the circle and cuts it into sectors, so
that nothing is taken off the outside at all.

[[figure:log|A {{sq.log}} inch log split {{cut.splits}} ways through the pith. Each cut runs straight through the centre, so every wedge is the same shape and nobody measures an angle.]]

## The log does the geometry

Halve a circle through its centre, halve each half, halve each quarter, and
you have eight sectors of exactly {{sq.theta}} degrees each, whether or not
you own a protractor. The method needs a straight cut through the centre and
nothing else. That is why a chainsaw and a steady hand can produce matching
members by the hundred: the accuracy is in the log, not in the person.

## Every face goes through the heart

Wood shrinks as it dries, and it shrinks more around the growth rings than
across them: from green to oven-dry, {{wood.shrink_t}} per cent around the rings
against {{wood.shrink_r}} per cent across them, for the pine these books use. A sawn board cut away from the centre has faces that cut
the rings at every angle, so it cups, bows and twists as it dries. A wedge's two
flat faces both run through the pith, square across the rings. It shrinks
mostly in its width, evenly, and stays straight. Of all the ways to cut a log,
the radial cut moves least.

## A wedge carries its wood where it counts

A wedge is deep, pith to bark, and narrow at the point. Stood on its back, it
puts most of its wood far from the middle, where a beam needs it. In a
triangulated frame -- a dome, a truss, a zome -- the members are pushed and
pulled along their length rather than bent, and the shape of the stick hardly
matters. That is the whole argument of the dome books: *triangles do not care
what shape your stick is*.

:::box why "The short version"
A split keeps more of the log, moves less as it dries, needs no measured angle,
and comes off one small chainsaw. The cost is that you get wedges, not boards.
The rest of this book is about when that is a cost at all.
:::

:::box do "See it for yourself"
Draw a circle on paper and cut it out. Fold it in half, in half again, and once
more, and unfold it: eight matching sectors. Now draw the largest rectangle
you can inside a second circle and cut it out. Lay the four curved scraps next
to your eight sectors. Which way kept more of the paper?
:::
