# Split Finer, Get Boards

The skewed pair keeps cos θ of itself when it is planed square to its long
face. The smaller the wedge's point angle θ, the closer that is to all of it:
a thin wedge pairs into something very close to a board. Split finer, and the
pairs come out flatter, wider and less wasteful to plane.

[[figure:tradeoff|Pairs from the reference log, split six, eight, twelve and sixteen ways, each with the board it planes to. The finer the split, the flatter the pair.]]

## The saw pushes back

There is a price. Every extra split is another cut across the log, and every
cut takes a kerf. Split {{cut.splits}} ways and the saw takes {{sq.n8_kerf}} per
cent of the round; split twenty-four ways and it takes {{sq.n24_kerf}} per cent,
and what is left keeps only {{sq.n24_kept}} per cent as planed boards. Somewhere
in between, the two effects balance.

    {{sq.t_tradeoff}}

On the reference log the balance falls at **{{sq.best_n}} ways**, where pairs
plane to boards {{sq.n16_w}} by {{sq.n16_t}} inches and keep {{sq.best_kept}} per
cent of the log. Twelve ways is nearly as good -- {{sq.n12_kept}} per cent, as
members {{sq.n12_w}} by {{sq.n12_t}} inches -- with fewer cuts.

## Choosing your split

* **Four ways** for posts: quarters back to back, square without planing.
* **Eight ways** for frames: wedges as they are, and squares where needed.
* **Twelve or sixteen ways** for boards: pairs that plane to near-dimensional
  stock with the least waste.

You do not have to split a whole log one way. Quarter the butt for posts, split
the middle eight ways for frame members, and take the top sixteen ways for
boards. The log tells you which: big and straight, square it; small, split it
fine.

:::box watch "Watch out"
Splits that are not a power of two cannot be made by halving. Twelve ways means
marking the end grain with a template and cutting to the marks, and a cut that
wanders off the centre gives you wedges that will not pair. Mark both ends, and
keep the bar on the line.
:::
