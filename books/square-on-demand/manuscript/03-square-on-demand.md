# Square on Demand

Take two wedges. Turn one end for end, so its point lies against the other's
back, and lay them together on a flat face -- slant to slant. What you are
holding looks like a two-by-four that has been pushed sideways: four flat faces,
two long and two short, with corners of {{sq.acute}} and {{sq.obtuse}} degrees
instead of ninety.

[[figure:slant|Two eighths, slant to slant. The pair is a parallelogram, {{sq.pair_long}} by {{sq.pair_short}} inches with corners of {{sq.acute}} and {{sq.obtuse}} degrees. The dashed square is what it planes to.]]

## The skew planes to a square

Here is the part that gives this book its name. The largest rectangle you can
plane out of that skewed pair, cut square to its long faces, is *R* cos θ wide
and *R* sin θ thick, where θ is the angle at the wedge's point. For an eighth,
θ is {{sq.theta}} degrees, and the cosine and the sine of {{sq.theta}} degrees are
the same number, {{sq.cos45}}. So the rectangle is a square.

On the reference log, after the kerf and a planing allowance of {{sq.plane}}
inch a face, two eighths plane to a member **{{sq.square}} inches square**.
That is enough for {{sq.square_4x4}} dressed four-by-four, or
{{sq.square_2x4}} dressed two-by-fours resawn from it.

## What it costs

Squaring is not free. The planed square keeps {{sq.square_kept_pair}} per cent
of the pair, and {{sq.square_kept}} per cent of the two sectors of round log
it came from. Split eighths keep {{sq.split_pct}} per cent of that same log as
they come off the saw. So a square is something you pay for out of the
wedge's advantage, and you should buy it only where you need it: a post that
has to take a door frame, a sill that sits on a level foundation, a plate a
wall stands on.

That is the method in one line. **Split everything; square on demand.**

:::box do "Make a square from two wedges"
1. Choose two wedges from the same section of the same log, so they match.
2. Plane or flush-cut the backs flat if they are to be glued; leave the radial
   faces as sawn if they mate well.
3. Turn one end for end and lay them slant to slant. Clamp, and either glue the
   joint or bolt through it at a spacing your engineer gives you.
4. Mark the square on both ends with a carpenter's square, square to one long
   face, and plane down to the marks.
:::

:::box watch "Watch out"
A glued pair is only as straight as its two wedges. Pair wedges from the same
section, dry them together, and glue them dry. A bolted pair can be taken apart
and used as wedges again; a glued one cannot.
:::
