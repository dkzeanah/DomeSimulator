# What This Book Is

A round log split through its centre does not give you boards. It gives you
wedges: long sticks whose two flat faces meet at the pith, with the bark on
their backs. The dome books in this project build a whole house out of them,
because a triangulated frame does not care what shape its sticks are.

This book asks the next question. What else can you build with wedge-shaped
lumber, and what do you do when a job really does want a square stick?

The answer is in the title. You do not have to choose between wedges and
boards when you fell the tree. Keep the wood as wedges, which is how a log
gives up the most of itself, and make a square member only where a square
member is needed: two wedges put together the right way round, planed true,
on demand. Everywhere else, use the wedge as it is, and use its angles, which
turn out to be useful in their own right.

Everything here is worked from one log: the project's reference trunk,
{{sq.log}} inches across, split {{cut.splits}} ways. Every number is computed
from the geometry, by the same software that solves the domes, so you can
change the log and get a new book. The few figures that are not computed --
the saw's kerf, the planing allowance, the standard sizes of dressed lumber --
are declared in a table at the back, with where each one comes from.

:::box why "Who this is for"
Owner-builders with a chainsaw and a stand of timber. Veterans' build crews and
the villages they serve. Teachers who want geometry that ends in something
you can stand inside. And anybody who has watched a mill turn a good log into
a pile of slabs and wondered whether there was another way.
:::

:::box watch "Before anything else"
This is a book of methods, not engineering ratings. A member made from paired
wedges is not graded lumber, and nothing in these pages tells you what load it
will carry. Anything that holds up people, snow or wind is checked by a
licensed engineer before it is built. The saw work is adult work, done in full
protective gear, and never alone.
:::
