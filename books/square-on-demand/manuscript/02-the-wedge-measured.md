# The Wedge, Measured

Before pairing wedges or shimming them, know the one you have.

A wedge from a log of radius *R*, split *n* ways, has:

* two flat faces, each *R* long, meeting at the pith at {{sq.theta}} degrees for
  an eighth;
* a curved back. Trimmed flat, the back is a chord {{sq.chord}} inches wide on
  the reference log, and the section becomes an isosceles triangle;
* a depth, pith to the middle of the back, of {{sq.depth}} inches;
* two corners at the back of {{sq.beta}} degrees each.

For the reference log that triangle is {{sq.tri_area}} square inches. The
sector it came from was {{sq.sector_area}}; trimming the back flat costs
{{sq.back_loss}} square inches, {{sq.back_loss_pct}} per cent of the wedge. Leave
the back round where nothing has to sit on it, and you keep that wood too.

## The saw takes its share

A chainsaw cut is not a line. The saw removes a strip {{sq.kerf}} inches wide,
and splitting {{cut.splits}} ways takes four cuts across the whole diameter.
On the reference log that is {{sq.n8_kerf}} per cent of the round. Every wedge
loses half a kerf off each flat face, which moves its point outward a little;
the triangle that is left is the same shape, slightly smaller. Every figure
from here on has the kerf taken out.

## Bigger logs, bigger wedges

The proportions never change: an eighth is always {{sq.theta}} degrees at the
point and {{sq.beta}} at the back. Only the size does.

size, put together and planed square.

:::box why "Why the angles never change"
An eighth of a circle is an eighth of a circle at any size. The point angle is
three hundred and sixty degrees divided by the number of splits; the back
angles are what is left of a hundred and eighty, shared equally. Change the log
and every length scales together, but the shape is fixed by how many ways you
split it.
:::
