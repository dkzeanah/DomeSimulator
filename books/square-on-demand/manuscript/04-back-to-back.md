# Back to Back

There is a second way to put two wedges together: on their backs, the trimmed
bark faces glued or bolted face to face, points out.

[[figure:back|Quarters back to back are a true square with nothing to plane. Eighths back to back make a diamond.]]

## Quarters make a square

Split a log only four ways and each quarter is a right-angled triangle once its
back is trimmed. Two right-angled triangles laid together on their long sides
are a square. There is nothing to plane except the saw marks: two quarters of
the reference log make a member **{{sq.quarter_square}} inches square**, planed,
and keep {{sq.quarter_kept}} per cent of the round they came from.

That is the heaviest square the method makes, and it is the one for posts. A
quarter is also the easiest split there is: two cuts across the log, at right
angles, through the pith.

## Eighths make a diamond

Two eighths back to back make a diamond -- a rhombus whose sharp corners are
{{sq.theta}} degrees. It is a poor source of boards: the largest board inside it
is {{sq.diamond_w}} by {{sq.diamond_t}} inches, {{sq.diamond_kept}} per cent of the
wood. But a diamond is the right shape for a different job: a ridge, a rail with
both faces shedding water, a spline driven into a V-groove, or a corner post for
an octagon.

:::box why "Why the quarters come out square"
A quarter's point is a right angle, and its two flat faces are the same length.
Put two of them together along the side opposite the point and you have four
equal sides and four right angles. The eighth's point is half a right angle, so
the same move gives four equal sides and two sharp corners: a diamond.
:::
