# The Seam Is a Channel

Square lumber laid side by side closes up. Wedges laid side by side on their
backs do not: their sloping faces open a V between them, the full length of the
member.

[[figure:seam|Two wedges on their backs, a kerf's width apart. The V between them runs the whole length, and it can be a channel instead of a gap.]]

In the dome books that gap is the most useful thing in the building. Capped
outside, the seam between panels becomes a duct: it carries rain off the shell
to the tank, wiring round the room, and air to wherever it is needed, without a
single gutter or conduit added. The same is true of any wall or floor laid up
from wedges.

## What fits

* **Water.** A capped V seam is a gutter that cannot overflow sideways, sloped
  by the building itself.
* **Wire.** A seam is a cable run that is never buried in a wall: lift the cap
  and the wiring is there.
* **Air.** A seam can breathe a wall, carrying damp air out of it.

## What does not

A seam is narrow. It is not a duct for a furnace, and it is not a drain for
anything with solids in it. Big services go in the building's utility core, not
its seams.

:::box why "Why the gap is the same everywhere"
Every wedge from one split has the same face angle. Two of them side by side
open a V of exactly the wedge's point angle, every time, so every seam in a
wall has the same section, and one cap, one key and one fitting do all of them.
:::
