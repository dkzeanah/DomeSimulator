# Triangle types

There are two kinds of triangle in a 2V hemisphere and you can tell them apart
across the room.

AAA TRIANGLES

Three A members. All three sides 72.000 inches. Equilateral -- genuinely
equilateral, not approximately.

There are ten of them, and they live at the top of the dome, in and around the
ring at the apex.

These are the easy ones. Every side is the same, every corner is sixty
degrees, and if one of them comes out wrong you will see it immediately
because it will not be symmetrical.

BAB TRIANGLES

One A and two Bs. Two sides of 63.670 inches and one of 72.000.

There are thirty of them, and they make up everything below the top: the
middle band, the base ring, and every triangle that touches the ground.

These are isosceles, not equilateral. They have a long edge and two short
ones, and the long edge always points the same way relative to the dome --
which is the thing to check when you are assembling, because a BAB fitted
upside down looks nearly right and is not.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR PANELS

If you are building panel by panel -- and in the wedge method you are -- you
are building exactly two kinds of panel.

Ten of one. Thirty of the other.

That is a much better position than it sounds. It means one jig setting for
the AAA panels and one for the BAB panels, and it means the fortieth panel is
the same job as the first.

It also means your total perimeter of panel edge is two numbers rather than
forty. On the reference build an AAA triangle has 216.00 inches of perimeter
and a BAB has 199.34 inches. Ten of the first and thirty of the second is
every edge in the building, and you will want that number when you buy gasket,
tape, or anything else sold by the foot.

THE COUNT, IN ONE PLACE

* 40 triangles
* 10 AAA, 30 BAB
* 26 vertices
* 65 geodesic edges
* 55 of those edges are interior seams, where two triangles meet
* 10 are the base ring, where nothing meets them

Those numbers do not change with size. A nineteen-foot 2V hemisphere and a
forty-foot one have exactly the same counts. Only the lengths move.
