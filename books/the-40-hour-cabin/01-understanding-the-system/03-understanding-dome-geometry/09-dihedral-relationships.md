# Dihedral relationships

This is the section the rest of the book leans on, so it is worth reading
slowly.

A dihedral angle is the angle between two flat faces that share an edge. Open
a book halfway and the angle between the two covers is a dihedral angle.

On a dome, every interior seam is a dihedral. Two triangles meet along a line
and they are not coplanar -- they fold, because that folding is what makes the
flat triangles approximate a curved sphere.

HOW MUCH THEY FOLD

On the reference build:

* an A seam folds 18.029 degrees
* a B seam folds 22.459 degrees

That is the angle the two panels turn through, relative to each other, at
their shared edge.

Fifty-five interior seams in the hemisphere: twenty-five A and thirty B. The
other ten edges are the base ring and they do not fold, because there is
nothing on the other side of them.

WHY A WEDGE METHOD CARES MORE THAN A STRUT METHOD

In a strut-and-hub dome the dihedral is handled at the hub. The hub is a
casting or a plate with the angles built into it, and the struts just arrive.

In the wedge method there is no hub. The dihedral has to be handled by the
timber, and it is handled by the fact that a wedge is wider on one face than
the other.

That is the entire mechanism. A wedge, seen end-on, is a sector of a log -- a
triangle in section. Put two of them side by side along a seam and the
difference in width between their inner and outer faces makes the two panels
turn relative to each other. The wood does the folding.

THE NUMBER THAT MAKES THIS WORK

Split a log into eight, and each sector is 45 degrees.

Now look at what the dome asks for:

* A seam: folds 18.029 degrees, leaves 26.971 degrees
* B seam: folds 22.459 degrees, leaves 22.541 degrees

Add each pair. Both come to exactly 45.

That is not a coincidence and it is not tuned. The raw 45-degree sector is
being split between the fold the dome needs and the gap left over, and the two
always add to the sector angle because that is what a sector is.

WHAT THE LEFTOVER IS

The leftover -- 26.971 degrees on an A seam, 22.541 on a B -- is a gap. Two
sawn faces meeting at a dihedral angle do not close flush. There is a
wedge-shaped void along every one of those fifty-five seams.

Every other way of building a dome treats that as a defect and machines it
out.

This book does not. That gap is the key, it has a chapter of its own, and it
turns out to be the most useful thing in the building.
