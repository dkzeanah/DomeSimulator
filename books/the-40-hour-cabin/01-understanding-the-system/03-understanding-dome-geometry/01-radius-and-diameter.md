# Radius and diameter

Everything in a geodesic dome is measured from one point that is not there.

The centre of the sphere. On the reference build it sits on the floor, in the
middle of the room, and nothing is bolted to it. But every vertex in the frame
is exactly the same distance from it, and that distance is the only number the
whole design really has.

On the reference build that number is 116.4984 inches. Call it a radius of
nine feet eight and a half.

The dome is a hemisphere, so the diameter across the floor is twice that:
19.416 feet. Nineteen feet five. And because it is a hemisphere and not a
taller or shorter slice, the height is the same as the radius: 9.708 feet.

Those three numbers are not three decisions. They are one decision, written
three ways.

WHERE THE RADIUS ACTUALLY COMES FROM

Here is the part that surprises people, and it is worth getting straight
before anything else.

You do not choose the radius.

You choose the longest stick you are willing to cut, and the radius falls out
of it.

On the reference build the longest member is six feet. Seventy-two inches.
That is not a geometric choice at all -- it is a decision about what fits on a
truck, what one person can carry, and what comes out of the log you have. From
that, the geometry gives you a radius of 116.4984 inches, and from the radius
you get the diameter, the height, the floor area, and every other stick in the
building.

Six feet in, nineteen feet five out.

If you want a bigger dome you do not scale the radius. You pick a longer stick
and let the radius follow. That is the direction the arithmetic runs, and
people who try to run it the other way end up with a beautiful radius and a
member length nobody can cut.

WHAT THE RADIUS DOES NOT TELL YOU

The radius is the distance to the vertices. It is not the distance to the
skin, and it is not the distance to the inside face of the wall.

A member on this dome is six inches deep. It sits with its outer face near the
sphere and its inner face six inches closer to the middle. So the room inside
is smaller than the sphere by roughly the depth of the wall, everywhere.

That sounds obvious written down. It stops being obvious at two in the
afternoon when you are trying to work out whether a piece of furniture fits,
and you measure from a drawing of the sphere rather than from the inside of
the wood.

Measure the room. Not the maths.
