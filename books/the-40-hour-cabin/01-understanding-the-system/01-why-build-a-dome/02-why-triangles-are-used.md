# Why triangles are used

Triangles are used because three connected sides can define a shape without needing another member to keep that shape from folding sideways. That makes them useful anywhere we want a frame to hold a predictable geometry. Take four straight pieces and connect them into a rectangle.

If the corners are allowed to rotate, the rectangle can lean sideways and become a parallelogram without changing the length of any of the four sides. Nothing has to stretch. Nothing has to get shorter.

The joints only have to rotate. Now add a diagonal brace across that rectangle. The diagonal divides it into two triangles.

At that point, changing the shape requires one or more of the member lengths to change, a connection to slip, or a member to bend. That is the basic reason triangular framing appears throughout a geodesic dome.

GEOMETRY

A triangle is determined by its geometry in a way that a four-sided frame is not. If we know the lengths of all three sides of a valid triangle, its angles are also determined.

For sides \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\), the angle \(C\) opposite side \(c\) can be found with the law of cosines:

$$ c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos(C) $$

Rearranging it gives:

$$ C = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{a^2+b^2-c^2}{2ab}\right) $$

The other two angles can be calculated the same way.

This matters in a dome because the strut lengths are not just pieces we cut to fill space. Their lengths establish the triangular faces.

Once those lengths are correct, the triangle has a defined shape. The next problem is then how that triangle meets the triangles around it in three-dimensional space. That second problem is where the dome's curvature appears.

The individual triangle can be treated as a flat geometric face. The neighboring triangle is also a flat face. The angle between those two faces turns the collection of flat triangles into a rounded structure.

A dome is therefore not made by bending each triangle. The curvature comes from changing the orientation of one triangle relative to the next.

For the wedge method, that distinction is especially useful.

We can first think about the geometry inside one triangular face:

* What are its three side lengths?
* Which strut family forms each side?
* What are its three internal angles?
* Is it equilateral, isosceles, or scalene?

Then we deal with the three-dimensional relationship between that face and the faces beside it.

The wedge-shaped member is part of solving that second problem.

TRIANGLES AS REPEATABLE BUILDING UNITS

From a fabrication standpoint, triangles also give us manageable units. Instead of trying to position every member of the dome independently in open space, we can think in terms of repeated triangular relationships. If a dome uses two triangle types, for example, we do not have to invent forty different shapes just because the finished dome contains forty triangular faces.

We identify the repeating geometry and make those parts consistently. This is one of the places where geodesic construction begins to make more sense from a builder's point of view. The finished frame is complicated.

The list of unique parts may not be. A group of accurately repeated strut lengths can produce a much larger three-dimensional form when those struts are assembled in the correct pattern. The triangle becomes the bridge between those two scales.

At the fabrication scale, we are cutting individual pieces. At the assembly scale, those pieces become triangles.

At the building scale, the triangles become the dome.

WHY THIS MATTERS TO THE WEDGE METHOD

In a conventional rectangular timber frame, the member is usually treated as having the same basic cross-section from one end to the other. The connection hardware or the end cuts then have to deal with how one face of the structure changes direction relative to another. The wedge method gives us another variable to work with.

The member itself has a narrow side and a wide side. When the narrow side is oriented toward the interior and the wider side toward the exterior, the cross-section can participate in the transition between neighboring triangular faces. The triangle is still defined by its strut geometry.

The wedge does not replace that geometry. It changes how we can physically realize that geometry in timber. This means there are several different angles in the system that should not be confused with one another.

There are the internal angles inside a triangular face. There are the angles created where struts meet at a vertex. There are the angles between neighboring triangular faces.

There are also the angles created by the wedge-shaped cross-section of the timber itself. They are related, but they are not interchangeable. A common mistake would be to calculate one of those angles and assume it is automatically the saw setting needed for another part of the joint.

We will keep them separate throughout the book.

REFERENCE BUILD

The 20-foot-diameter 2V reference dome will contain repeating triangular faces made from the strut families established by its exact geodesic geometry. Once those strut lengths are formally defined, we can calculate the internal angles of each triangle directly from their side lengths.

For a triangular face with verified side lengths \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\), the calculation will be:

$$ C = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{a^2+b^2-c^2}{2ab}\right) $$

That gives the flat internal angle of the triangle. It does not yet give us the angle between that triangle and the neighboring face.

That relationship is handled separately when we get into the three-dimensional dome geometry and the wedge itself. This separation is important because it keeps the fabrication logic clean. First determine the correct strut lengths.

Then verify the triangle. Then determine how the triangles meet. Then determine what the wedge member has to do at those connections.

If we mix those steps together, it becomes much easier to cut the right number at the wrong angle.

For now, the main point is simple:

Triangles let us create repeatable, fixed geometric units from straight members.

The dome comes from arranging those units correctly in three-dimensional space.
