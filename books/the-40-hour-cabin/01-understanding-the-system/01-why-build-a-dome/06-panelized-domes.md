# Panelized domes

A geodesic dome does not have to be built as a loose frame first and skinned later. Another approach is to build the dome from panels. A panelized dome treats each triangular face, or sometimes groups of faces, as a prefabricated building unit.

Instead of assembling only struts and hubs, the builder assembles complete sections that already contain some combination of frame, sheathing, insulation, weather barrier, interior finish, or connection hardware. That changes the entire workflow. A strut-built dome starts with lines.

A panelized dome starts with surfaces.

DESIGN VARIATION

The simplest triangular panel might be nothing more than a sheet cut to the shape of one geodesic face.

A more developed panel might contain:

* structural edge members,
* an exterior skin,
* insulation,
* an interior skin,
* gasket material,
* attachment hardware,
* window openings,
* or service penetrations.

At that point the triangle is no longer just geometry. It becomes a prefabricated wall or roof section. The same basic geodesic layout is still underneath everything.

The triangle dimensions still come from the dome geometry. The difference is that more construction work happens before the panel reaches the dome. That can be useful when repeatability matters.

If twenty panels share the same geometry, the builder can make one accurate jig and use it repeatedly. If several panel types exist, each type can have its own fixture, cut list, and assembly process.

The manufacturing problem becomes:

Make Panel A correctly. Make Panel B correctly. Make however many of each type the dome requires.

Then assemble those panels in the correct pattern.

PANEL GEOMETRY STILL COMES FIRST

A panel cannot be designed correctly until the triangular face underneath it is known.

For each panel type we eventually need to know:

* the three edge lengths,
* the three internal angles,
* the orientation of the panel in the dome,
* the angle between that panel and its neighbors,
* the connection method along each edge,
* and how the panel thickness relates to the intended dome surface.

This is especially important when the panel is thick. A flat sheet has an inside face and an outside face that are close together. A thick insulated panel may move the exterior skin noticeably farther from the theoretical geodesic chord.

If neighboring panels are simply made from the same two-dimensional triangle and pushed together, the exterior edges may not meet the way the builder expects. The edge geometry has to account for thickness. That can mean bevels, wedges, gaskets, external covers, internal battens, or another joint system.

This is the same three-dimensional problem seen in strut construction.

We have just moved it into the panel edge.

FRAME-FIRST PANELS

One panelized method is to build a triangular timber frame first. The three edge members define the panel. Sheathing is then attached to that frame.

The frame may be built from rectangular lumber, wedge-shaped lumber, laminated members, or another section. This approach gives the panel its own structural border before it reaches the dome. In a wedge-method system, the wedge can become part of that panel edge.

Instead of using a rectangular perimeter and then cutting a separate bevel into the panel connection, the timber edge itself can help establish the transition between neighboring faces.

That creates a useful manufacturing possibility:

The wedge strut can act as both a structural member and a panel-edge geometry component. The panel skin can then be attached to the outside of the triangular frame. When two complete panels are brought together, their wedge-shaped edge members meet at the dome joint.

The connection system then has to clamp, screw, bolt, plate, or otherwise join those edges in a controlled way.

SKIN-FIRST PANELS

Another method starts with the sheet material. A triangular panel is cut first and framing is attached behind it. This may be practical for thin sheathing or lightweight assemblies where the skin itself provides the easiest reference surface during fabrication.

The advantage is that the visible panel shape can be controlled directly from a template or CNC layout. The disadvantage is that the rear framing still has to agree with the three-dimensional joint geometry. A flat triangle is easy to cut.

Making many thick flat triangles close into a dome without gaps is the harder part. That is why panel edge design matters as much as panel face dimensions.

PANEL JOINTS

When panels meet, several things have to happen at once.

The joint has to:

* locate the panels,
* hold them together,
* deal with the angle between faces,
* control water entry,
* control air leakage where required,
* allow whatever installation tolerance the system needs,
* and transfer structural forces if the panel is part of the load-bearing system.

One connection may not need to do every job by itself. For example, a structural fastener might hold the panels together while a separate gasket handles weather sealing. An exterior cap might cover the seam.

An interior batten might reinforce it. A removable design might use bolts or clamps instead of permanent adhesive. The important part is to stop treating "the panel joint" as one vague detail.

It is several problems occupying the same edge.

PANELIZATION AND REPEATABILITY

Panel construction makes the most sense when we take advantage of repetition. If every panel is built differently in the field, much of the benefit disappears.

The useful approach is to identify the repeating panel families early.

For each family we can eventually create:

* a panel ID,
* a drawing,
* a cut list,
* a jig,
* an edge orientation,
* an assembly sequence,
* and a location map for the finished dome.

That turns the dome into something closer to a manufactured kit. The site work becomes assembly of previously defined parts instead of fabrication of every part in place. This does not automatically make the project faster.

The fabrication time still exists. It has simply moved into a more controlled stage of the build. The advantage is that repeatable work can be organized around fixtures, templates, stops, and inspection.

A bad first panel can be corrected before twenty more are made from the same setup. A good setup can then be repeated without re-solving the geometry each time.

OPENINGS AND SPECIAL PANELS

Doors and windows interrupt the repeating triangle system. A panelized dome gives us one way to handle that interruption. Instead of modifying random pieces during erection, special panels can be designed before assembly.

For example:

* one triangular panel could contain a window,
* several neighboring triangles could be replaced by a reinforced door module,
* one panel could contain a ventilation opening,
* or a utility panel could contain controlled service penetrations.

These special pieces should be treated as separate panel types. They may look similar to the normal panels from outside, but structurally and functionally they are not the same. That means they need their own drawings and connection details.

A hole cut into a completed generic panel is not automatically equivalent to a panel designed around that opening.

REMOVABLE PANELS

DESIGN VARIATION

Panelization also makes removable sections possible. If the joint system is mechanical rather than permanently bonded, an individual panel could potentially be removed for repair, replacement, modification, or access.

That could be useful for:

* replacing damaged exterior sections,
* changing transparent and opaque panels,
* adding future openings,
* accessing utilities,
* or experimenting with different enclosure systems on the same structural frame.

This idea becomes more useful when panel dimensions and connections are standardized. If every panel is custom-fit during construction, replacing one later may require rebuilding that exact piece by hand. If the panel system is repeatable, replacement becomes a manufacturing problem rather than a reconstruction problem.

That does not mean every structural panel should be casually removable. If the panel participates in the load path, removing it may change the behavior of the structure. The connection between structural frame and removable shell therefore needs to be clearly defined.

SEPARATING FRAME FROM SHELL

One useful design decision is whether the panel is part of the structure or only part of the enclosure. Those are two different systems. In one version, the dome has a complete timber frame.

Panels attach to that frame but are not intended to be the primary structural system. In another version, the panel and its perimeter framing act together as a structural module. In another, the skin itself may participate in transferring forces between the edges.

Those systems may look similar after the dome is finished. They should not be assumed to behave the same way. For the wedge method, separating the frame and shell can keep the basic structural concept easier to understand.

The wedge members establish the geodesic frame. The panel system then attaches to that frame. Later variations can explore whether the panel can take on additional structural work.

SAFETY / ENGINEERING

A panel that carries structural load needs more than correct triangle dimensions.

Its behavior can depend on:

* panel material,
* thickness,
* edge framing,
* fastener spacing,
* connection design,
* openings,
* moisture,
* buckling,
* wind pressure,
* suction,
* and the way forces enter and leave the panel.

For that reason, this book does not treat a panel as structural simply because it fits into the geodesic pattern. A panel can be geometrically correct and still be structurally inadequate. Geometry tells us where the piece belongs.

Engineering determines what that piece can safely carry.

REFERENCE BUILD

For the 20-foot 2V reference dome, panelization will eventually be tied directly to the verified triangle families. Once those triangle types are fixed, each repeated face can be assigned a panel type.

A basic workflow will be:

1. identify the verified triangle families,
2. assign a panel ID to each family,
3. determine the edge geometry,
4. determine how panel thickness relates to the theoretical dome surface,
5. design the joint between neighboring panels,
6. define normal and special panels,
7. create fabrication jigs or templates,
8. label the panels,
9. assemble them according to a dome map.

That gives us a panel schedule instead of a pile of triangles. The important idea is that panelization does not replace the dome geometry.

It packages the geometry into larger, repeatable building units.
