# Tolerances

A tolerance is how wrong a part is allowed to be before it stops working.

Stating them is worth doing because without a stated tolerance every part is
either perfect or scrap, and neither is true.

WHAT THIS BUILD CAN LIVE WITH

Member length: plus or minus a sixteenth of an inch. The joints take up under
the fastener and a sixteenth vanishes.

End angles: within half a degree. This is the tight one. An angle error does
not take up -- it holds the joint open and it accumulates round a ring.

Cross-section: a quarter inch of variation is fine. The members are split from
real trees and they are not going to be identical. The frame does not care.

Panel flatness: a triangle that rocks by more than a sixteenth on a flat floor
is telling you an end angle is out.

WHY ANGLE IS TIGHTER THAN LENGTH

Because length errors are random and angle errors are systematic.

If your stop block is right and your technique is variable, lengths scatter
either side of the target and they cancel around the dome.

If your fence is set half a degree off, every single angle is off by the same
half degree in the same direction, and ten of them round a base ring is five
degrees of accumulated error with nowhere to go.

Length errors average out. Angle errors add up. That is the whole reason for
the different tolerance.

MEASURE THE RIGHT THING

Do not measure a member's length with a tape against the curved bark face.

Measure between the reference faces -- the sawn faces the jig uses. Those are
the surfaces the geometry actually uses, and the bark face is decoration.

WHEN TO SCRAP A PIECE

If it is long, it is not scrap. Recut it.

If it is short, it may still be a B member, or blocking, or jig stock.

If the angle is wrong, recut the end. If both ends are wrong, it is blocking.

Very little of this actually becomes waste, which is the compensation for
using eighty percent more wood than a shared-strut dome.
