# Structural screws

A structural screw is not a longer wood screw. It is a different product with
a published capacity.

WHAT MAKES ONE STRUCTURAL

A stated, tested load rating. That is the difference that matters. A
structural screw comes with a number you can design against; a hardware-store
screw comes with a box.

They also have a hardened shank, a thread designed for withdrawal rather than
just driving, and usually a head that will not cam out under real torque.

Most of them do not need a pilot hole in dimensional lumber. In green
split timber near an end, drill one anyway.

WHERE TO USE THEM

At the pinwheel joints. All 120 of them.

This is the load path of the entire building and it is not the place to save
four pounds. The joints are the only thing holding the dome's shape, and if
one fails the load goes to its neighbours, which are already carrying their
own.

WHERE NOT TO BOTHER

Everywhere else.

Battens, blocking, trim, temporary bracing, panel fixings -- ordinary
exterior-rated screws are fine and considerably cheaper. Using structural
screws for everything is a way of spending money without making the building
better.

THE HONEST LIMIT

This book cannot tell you what size or how many.

That depends on your timber species, your moisture content, your snow load,
your wind exposure and your local code, and anyone who gives you a number
without knowing those is guessing. What this book can tell you is that the
joint is a butt in compression with fasteners holding it closed, and that is
the information an engineer needs to size the fastener for you.

If the dome is a shed, use good structural screws at every joint and sleep
fine. If people are going to live in it, get the fastener schedule stamped.
