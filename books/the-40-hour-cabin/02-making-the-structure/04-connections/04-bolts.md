# Bolts

A bolt goes all the way through and has a nut on the other side. That is the
difference from a screw, and it changes what the joint can do.

WHEN A BOLT IS BETTER

When you want to take it apart and put it back together more than once.

A screw into wood loses holding power every time you remove and refit it --
the threads cut a slightly bigger hole each time. A bolt does not care. The
bolt bears on the hole and the nut does the clamping, and you can undo it a
hundred times.

That matters for a dome you intend to move, and it matters enormously for the
first dome you build, which you will take apart at least once.

WHEN A BOLT IS WORSE

When you cannot reach the back of it.

Every bolt needs access to both sides. In a finished dome with panels in, a
lot of joints have one accessible side and one buried. A bolt there is a bolt
you cannot ever tighten.

Plan the bolted joints before the panels go on, not after.

THE THREADED INSERT

There is a middle option and it is the one the reference build is heading
toward.

Set a threaded insert into the receiving member -- a metal sleeve with an
internal thread, driven into a drilled hole. Then a bolt from the butting
member threads into that.

The insert takes the thread so the wood never does. You get the repeatability
of a bolt without needing access to the far side, and you can undo and remake
the joint indefinitely without chewing out the hole.

Two per joint, 120 joints, 240 inserts.

BUY THE BOLTS LONG

If you think you might ever build a bigger dome, buy the bolts for the bigger
one now.

A bolt cut thirty-five percent longer than this dome needs will still reach
through the thicker members of the next two sizes up. The extra length costs
pennies per bolt and saves re-buying 240 fasteners later.

That is the cheapest forward decision in the whole build and almost nobody
makes it.
