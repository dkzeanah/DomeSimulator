# Washers

A washer spreads a point load into an area load. In softwood, that is not a
refinement -- it is the difference between a joint that holds and a fastener
that slowly buries itself.

WHY IT MATTERS MORE HERE

Split timber has exposed grain on its sawn faces, and a bolt head bearing
directly on end grain or near-end grain will crush into it under load and
under time.

Once it has crushed in, the joint is loose. Retightening crushes it further.

A wide washer under every bolt head and every nut puts that same load over
several square inches of wood instead of the area of a bolt head.

USE THEM WIDER THAN YOU THINK

The standard washer that comes taped to the bolt is sized for steel.

For timber, go wider. A washer twice the diameter of the standard one costs a
few cents more and quadruples the bearing area.

WHERE THEY GO

Under every nut and under every bolt head. Both ends. Not just the nut side,
which is the side people remember.

BEVELLED WASHERS

If the surface the washer sits on is not square to the bolt -- and on a wedge
it often is not, because the faces are sawn at the sector angle -- a flat
washer bears on one edge only.

A bevelled washer, or a small squared-off pad let into the face, fixes that.
On the reference build most joints are close enough to square that a flat
washer with a large enough diameter is fine. Check yours rather than assuming;
put a washer on and look at whether it sits flat.

THE ONE-SENTENCE VERSION

If you find yourself retightening a bolt more than once, the wood under the
washer has crushed, and a bigger washer would have prevented it.
