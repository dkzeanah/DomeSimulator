# Moisture

Green wood is wet wood, and it will get drier after you build with it.

That is not a reason to avoid it. It is a reason to know what it will do.

WHAT HAPPENS AS IT DRIES

Wood shrinks across the grain and barely at all along it.

For a member, that means it gets narrower and shallower and does not get
shorter. Chord lengths stay put. Cross-sections do not.

So a joint that was tight when built gets slightly loose after a season. That
is the single most predictable thing about building with green timber and it
has one answer: come back and tighten everything after the first summer.

HOW MUCH

Softwood shrinks roughly four to eight percent across the grain going from
green to dry, depending on species and direction.

On a member six inches deep that is a quarter to a half inch. Not nothing.

BUILDING GREEN ANYWAY

People have built with green timber for a very long time and the technique is
well understood: use joints that can be tightened, expect to tighten them, and
do not build anything that depends on a dimension staying exactly put.

The pinwheel joint is well suited to this. It is a compression joint held
closed by a fastener, and tightening the fastener takes up the shrinkage.

A joint relying on a precise fit -- a mortice, a dovetail, a machined key --
is not well suited, and that is worth remembering when you are tempted to get
clever at a seam.

WHAT NOT TO DO

Do not seal green timber. Paint, varnish or a tight plastic wrap on wet wood
traps the moisture and you get rot instead of drying.

Do not build it into an unventilated cavity while wet.

Do not assume it is dry because the outside feels dry. A six-inch member is
wet in the middle for a long time.

THE SCHEDULE THAT WORKS

Split green, because green splits far more easily than dry.

Then sticker the sectors -- stacked with spacers, under cover, with air all
round -- for as long as you can stand. Three months makes a real difference.
Six is better.

Then cut to final size, because a member cut dry stays the size you cut it.
