# Logs

This is what the method is really for.

A log is not a worse version of lumber. It is a different material with a
different economics, and the wedge method exists because squaring it is
wasteful.

THE NUMBER THAT MATTERS

Split a trunk into wedges and you keep about 88 percent of it -- 391 board
feet out of a tree that has 442.

Mill the same trunk into 2x4s and you keep about 45 percent. Two hundred
board feet.

Nearly twice the building, out of the same tree.

That is the whole argument, and it is not subtle. Squaring a round log means
throwing the round part away. The wedge method does not square anything.

WHY IT WORKS OUT THAT WAY

A rectangle inscribed in a circle leaves four corners of waste. Do it once per
board and the waste adds up to more than half the log.

A sector of a circle wastes only the saw kerf. Eight sectors out of a round
log is eight sectors, minus eight thin cuts.

WHAT A LOG COSTS

If it is standing on your land, it costs your time.

If you are buying, a log is sold by the ton or by the board foot and is
substantially cheaper than sawn timber, because you are buying it before
anybody has done anything to it.

THE CATCH, AND IT IS A REAL ONE

Green logs are heavy, they are awkward, and they will do things sawn timber
does not.

They move as they dry. They can have tension in them that makes a rip cut
close on the blade. They have bark, which holds water and insects. They are
never quite straight and never quite round.

The chapter on turning timber into wedges is longer than it would be for
lumber for exactly these reasons.

WHAT THE REFERENCE BUILD ASSUMES

A trunk about twelve inches across, split into eight, yielding sectors that
give a member roughly four and a half inches wide and six inches deep.

Roughly one and a half trees for the whole frame.

Twelve inches is not arbitrary: it is what gives a sector of the right size
for a six-foot member on this dome. A smaller trunk gives a thinner wall and a
bigger one gives you wood to trim off.
