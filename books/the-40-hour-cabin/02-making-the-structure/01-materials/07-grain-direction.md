# Grain direction

Wood is much stronger along the grain than across it, and every decision in
this chapter follows from that.

IN A MEMBER

The grain runs along the length. That is automatic when you split, because the
split follows the grain -- which is one of the quiet advantages of splitting
over sawing.

A sawn board can have grain running out the side of it, where the saw cut
across a curving grain line. That board is weak and it will break where the
grain runs out. A split member cannot have that, because the split went where
the grain went.

That is worth saying plainly: a split member is stronger for its size than a
sawn one, because splitting cannot cut across the fibres and sawing can.

AT A JOINT

The butting end presents end grain. The receiving face presents side grain.

Compression on end grain is fine -- wood is very strong that way. Compression
on side grain is weaker but adequate at these loads, and the contact area is
large.

A fastener into end grain holds poorly. A fastener into side grain holds well.

So: wherever possible, drive fasteners into the side of a member rather than
into its end. The pinwheel joint arranges this naturally, which is another of
its quiet advantages.

SPLITTING NEAR AN END

The end of a member is where the grain is free to separate, and it is where
every fastener goes.

Pilot every hole. In green timber, near an end, without a pilot, you will
split the piece -- reliably, not occasionally.

THE ORIENTATION QUESTION

A wedge has a curved bark face, two sawn faces, and a point. Which way it goes
in the wall is a real decision and it has its own section in the geometry
chapter.

For grain purposes it does not matter much: the fibres run along the member
whichever way you rotate it. What changes is which face takes the fastener and
which face meets the seam, and those are covered where the geometry is.
