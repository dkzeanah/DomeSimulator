# Error checking

Errors in a dome do not stay where you put them.

A dome is a closed shape. Every panel has to agree with its neighbours all the
way round, and when you get back to where you started, the last panel either
fits or it does not. There is no slack to absorb a mistake and nowhere to hide
it.

That makes the last panel an extremely good, extremely late test.

FINDING IT EARLIER

Check the sticks. Every tenth member against your master, as described
earlier.

Check the panels. Every panel against the first good one. They should stack.

Check the rings. As you assemble, the base ring should close on itself and sit
level. If the tenth base vertex does not land where the geometry says, stop.
Do not force it. Something upstream is wrong and forcing it will push the
error into the next ring.

THE ERRORS THAT ADD UP AND THE ONES THAT DO NOT

Random errors mostly cancel. If half your sticks are a sixteenth long and half
are a sixteenth short, the dome closes and nobody notices.

Systematic errors do not cancel. If every stick is a sixteenth long because
your fence is a sixteenth out, then by the time you have gone round the base
ring you are ten sixteenths out -- more than half an inch -- and the last
panel will tell you about it.

So the question to ask when something does not fit is not "how big is this
error". It is "is this error in all of them".

Measure three sticks from different points in the run. If all three are long
by the same amount, you have a systematic error and a fence to adjust. If they
disagree randomly, you have a technique problem and a different fix.

WHEN TO STOP AND WHEN TO CARRY ON

Stop if a ring will not close, if a panel rocks, or if the same measurement
comes out differently twice.

Carry on if a joint has a small gap that closes when the fastener pulls up, or
if a member is a sixteenth under. Wood moves, joints take up, and a dome that
is built to a sixteenth is built better than most houses.

The skill is telling those two apart, and it comes from having checked enough
things that you know what normal looks like.
