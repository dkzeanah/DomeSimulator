# A and B members

Sixty A members and sixty B members. Eight and a third inches of chord between
them.

That difference is big enough to see and small enough to get away from you in
a pile.

KEEP THEM APART FROM THE FIRST CUT

Not after. From the first cut.

Two stacks, two locations, and mark every stick as it comes off the saw. Not
later, not when you have a few. As it comes off.

A pencil mark on the end grain takes one second and survives the whole build.
An unmarked stick in the wrong pile costs you a panel.

WHY THIS IS WORSE THAN IT SOUNDS

If these were just two lengths, mixing them up would be an annoyance. You
would offer up a stick, find it too short, and reach for another.

But in the wedge method the two families sit at different angles. An A seam
folds 18.029 degrees; a B seam folds 22.459. The member is not just a length,
it is a length at an angle, and a B member fitted where an A belongs is wrong
in a way that partially fits.

That is the dangerous kind of wrong. It goes in. It looks close. The panel
comes out slightly off and you do not know why, and by the time you find it
you have built six more.

THE MARKING SCHEME

Use something you cannot misread at a glance:

* A members get one mark
* B members get two

Not "A" and "B" in pencil, which look similar upside down in poor light at the
bottom of a pile. One line or two lines. You can count that from across the
shop.

Mark the receiving face too, while you are at it, and mark it differently.
Which face receives a butt is not obvious once the stick is out of the jig,
and getting it backwards mirrors the panel.

THE COUNT

Sixty of each. If you have cut sixty-one of one and fifty-nine of the other,
something went in the wrong pile and you want to know now.

Count them before you start assembling. Counting 120 sticks takes two minutes.
Finding the miscount at panel thirty-eight takes an afternoon.
