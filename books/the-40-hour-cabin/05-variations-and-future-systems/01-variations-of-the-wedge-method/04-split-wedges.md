# Split wedges

Splitting is different from sawing, and the difference is the oldest argument
in woodworking.

SPLIT VERSUS SAWN

A saw cuts through the fibres wherever the blade goes. A split follows them.

Split stock is therefore stronger along its length, because no fibre is cut
short -- which is why axe handles and riven oak gates are split, and why a
sawn one of the same dimensions breaks first.

It is also faster and needs no power.

WHAT IT COSTS

Accuracy. A split follows the grain, and the grain in a real tree is not
perfectly straight. A split wedge is approximately the shape you wanted, and
the amount of approximately depends on the tree.

Kerf, though: none. The reference build's model loses
51.2 board feet per log to a
0.25-inch kerf -- out of 442 solid --
and splitting loses none of it.

THE METHOD THAT ACTUALLY WORKS

Split radially, into 8 pieces, then clean the two faces that
matter with a saw or a plane.

That gets the strength of split stock and the accuracy of sawn faces on the
two surfaces that have to mate. The curved back is left as it comes, because
nothing bears on it.

That is the reference build's assumption and it is the reason the numbers
work: it is a split log with two tidied faces, not a machined beam.

WHERE IT FAILS

Twisted grain. Some trees spiral, and a spiral log splits into spiral wedges.
Test one section before committing sixty feet.

Big knots. A split runs into a knot and goes round it, or stops.

Species. Straight-grained softwoods split beautifully. Interlocked tropical
hardwoods do not split at all.
