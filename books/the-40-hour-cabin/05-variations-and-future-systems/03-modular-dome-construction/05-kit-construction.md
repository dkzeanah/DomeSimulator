# Kit construction

A kit is what this method becomes when somebody else does the cutting, and it
is a different product with a different customer.

WHAT IS IN ONE

120 members, cut and sorted. 40 panels in two
shapes. The utility column, assembled and tested. Fixings, gaskets, a shell,
and a set of drawings.

WHAT IT COSTS TO INCLUDE THE FRAME

The frame is the part a buyer with trees does not need.

That is the whole harvest argument: a milled log yields about 45 percent of
itself as usable lumber, and split into wedges it yields about 88. A kit
buyer is paying for somebody else's 45 percent.

So a kit is either expensive or it is not a wedge kit -- and a kit that ships
milled rectangular stock has abandoned the method's central claim while
keeping its shape.

THE HONEST SPLIT

Two products, not one.

For somebody with land and trees: everything except the frame. The column,
the panels, the shell, the fixings and the drawings, and a weekend with a
chainsaw for the rest.

For somebody with money and no trees: the whole thing, at a price that
reflects the timber, and no pretence that it is the cheap version.

Trying to sell one product to both customers is how a good idea gets a
reputation for over-promising.

WHAT A KIT IS GENUINELY GOOD AT

The column. It is fiddly, it wants
6.5 hours on a bench, and it is the same object every
time. That is exactly what should be made by somebody who has made forty.

Panels, gaskets and fixings, for the same reason.

The frame is the part that should stay local. The rest is the part that
should not.
