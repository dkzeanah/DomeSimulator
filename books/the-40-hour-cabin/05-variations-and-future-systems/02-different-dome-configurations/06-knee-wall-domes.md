# Knee-wall domes

A knee wall is a short vertical wall between the foundation and the dome, and
it is the single most effective change you can make to how a dome feels
inside.

WHAT IT FIXES

The lean. Every complaint about living in a dome is a complaint about the
perimeter -- furniture that will not go against a wall, a door you duck
through, a room whose usable area is smaller than its floor.

Raise the shell three feet on a vertical wall and all of those improve at
once. The wall is vertical where furniture is, the door is a normal door, and
the usable floor grows by more than the wall's own area.

WHAT IT COSTS

The wall itself, which is conventional construction and cheap.

Some of the shape's efficiency: more envelope for the same floor, so the
41 percent saving shrinks.

And the base ring stops being the dome's ring. The dome now lands on a wall,
and the wall has to resolve the outward thrust the ring used to take in hoop
stress. That means a top plate acting as a ring beam, properly joined at
every splice, and a tie across the floor or a foundation stiff enough to
resist spreading.

That last point is the one that gets missed. A dome on a knee wall is pushing
that wall outward all the time, and the wall is not a dome and cannot resolve
it by itself.

HOW HIGH

Two to four feet is the useful range.

Under two, it does not change the feel enough to be worth the complication.
Over four, the building stops reading as a dome and starts reading as a round
house with a domed roof -- which is a perfectly good building and a different
one.

THE HONEST RECOMMENDATION

If somebody is going to live in it full time, and they have not lived in a
dome before, a three-foot knee wall is probably the right answer and this
book's reference build does not have one.
