# 3V

A 3V divides each icosahedral face into nine, giving 180 triangles on the
sphere.

WHAT IT BUYS

Roundness. A 3V reads as a sphere rather than as a polyhedron, and the
difference at any size above a shed is obvious.

Smaller panels, which means sheet goods start to fit, which quietly solves
the 2V's sheet problem.

Better structural behaviour. More, smaller triangles distribute load more
evenly and rely less on any single member.

And a proper base. A 3V can be cut at several heights, including one that
gives a near-vertical lower wall -- which is the thing people most often want
from a dome and cannot get from a 2V.

WHAT IT COSTS

Three strut lengths, and the member count roughly triples from the reference
build's 120. That is three piles to keep straight and
three times as many joints to make.

More seams, more gaskets, more sealing, more of every per-joint cost.

And at this scale the wedge method starts to strain: a hundred and twenty
members is a weekend of sorting and four hundred is a different kind of job.

WHERE THE LINE IS

Roughly: up to about 20 feet, 2V. From about 25 feet upward, 3V. Between
them it is a judgement about whether you would rather cut more pieces or live
in a more faceted room.

The reference build sits at 19.4 feet, just under the line, which is
not a coincidence.

THE WEDGE-SPECIFIC CATCH

A higher frequency means shorter members, and shorter members mean the
pinwheel overlap is a larger fraction of each one.

At the reference build's size an A member is
70.2 inches of stock for a
72-inch chord. Halve the chord and the overlap does not
halve with it, so the ratio gets worse.

The pinwheel already carries a 1.80x stock penalty against a mitred frame,
covered by a 1.95x harvest advantage. As the members get shorter that
penalty grows and at some frequency the harvest stops covering it.

Nobody has worked out where. It is one of the open questions this book would
rather name than paper over.
