# Ring foundation

A ring foundation is concrete where the load lands and lumber where it does
not, and on a dome that turns out to be most of the floor.

THE REASON IT SUITS THIS BUILDING

A 2V dome delivers its whole weight to ten points on a circle.

Not to a perimeter wall, not to the middle -- ten points, evenly spaced,
where the base vertices sit. Everything inside that circle is carrying
furniture and people, which is a fraction of the load and a job timber does
perfectly well.

So pouring the middle is pouring concrete that nothing stands on.

WHAT IT COSTS

$3,039 for the ring and the framed middle, on 82 boards
of lumber.

That is more than the slab's $2,468 -- and that is worth saying plainly
rather than hiding, because the intuition is that half a pour must be cheaper
than a whole one. It is not. The saving on concrete is smaller than the cost
of the timber deck that replaces it, and the ring's formwork is fiddlier per
cubic yard than a slab's.

WHAT YOU ACTUALLY BUY

A floor you can get under.

That is the thing. Services run in the void, they stay reachable, and a
mistake is an afternoon rather than a demolition saw. On a slab every pipe
is a decision you made once.

It is also the honest way to build on ground that is not flat, because the
ring can step and the deck inside it stays level.

WHO ELSE BUILDS THIS WAY

Grain bins and yurt platforms, both of which are round buildings that put
their load on a perimeter. It is not an invention; it is the standard answer
to this shape, arrived at by people who were not thinking about domes.

WHEN TO CHOOSE IT

When you want permanence at the load points and access everywhere else, and
when the ground is uneven enough that a slab would need a lot of fill.

Not for the money. It does not save any.
