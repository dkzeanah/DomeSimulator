# Composite panels

A composite panel is two skins with a core between them, and the core is doing
most of the work.

WHY THEY SUIT A DOME

A dome panel is a triangle spanning about five feet, carrying wind and snow
and its own weight, and it is not carrying the building -- the frame does
that.

That is exactly the load case a composite is good at: stiff across a span,
light, and not required to be strong in the way a structural member is.

THE COMMON CORES

Foam. Light, insulating, cheap, and it will not take a fastener. Anything
screwed to it needs a hard insert.

Honeycomb. Stiffer for its weight than foam, more expensive, and it does not
insulate.

Timber. A ply skin either side of a timber frame is the oldest composite there
is and the one you can make in a shed.

THE JOIN PROBLEM

Composites are excellent panels and awkward edges.

A foam-cored panel has an exposed core all the way round, which has to be
capped, sealed or rebated. On a triangle that is three edges per panel and 120
edges for the dome.

That edge detail is where composite panels get expensive, and it is the reason
people who cost a composite skin off the panel price alone are always
surprised.

WHERE THEY EARN IT

Where you want insulation and structure in one operation and you are not
planning to take the panel off again.

The reference build does not use them, because the reference build's whole
argument is a shell that comes off and layers that stack underneath. A
composite panel decides its own insulation value at the factory and cannot
change its mind afterwards.

That is the trade: a composite is better on the day it goes on, and it is the
last decision you get to make about that bay.
