# Window frames

Windows are easier than doors, because nothing walks through them and they can
follow the shell's geometry instead of fighting it.

THE TRIANGLE WINDOW

The simplest window in a wedge dome is a glazed panel in a bay.

The bay is already a frame -- three members with a lip cut into them by the
shape of the wedge. Put a triangular pane in instead of a solid panel and you
have a window with no new framing at all.

That is the option the reference build uses, and it is why the fit-out
catalogue can turn a dome into a studio by changing which bays are glazed.

WHAT IT COSTS YOU

Triangular glass is a special order and it is more expensive per square foot
than rectangular.

It does not open. A triangular opening light is possible and it is not a
standard product.

And a triangle high in the shell is a skylight whether you meant it or not --
it faces upward, it gets sun all day, and it leaks heat all night.

THE OPENING WINDOW

If you want a window that opens, put a square one in a square sub-frame inside
a triangular opening, the same way the door goes in a box.

You lose some glass area to the frame. You gain a normal window with normal
hardware that a normal person can replace in fifteen years.

WHERE TO PUT THEM

Low for view, high for light.

A dome's upper panels face the sky and will light the room brilliantly and
overheat it in summer. The lower panels face out and give you something to
look at.

Most people want more of the second than they expect and fewer of the first.

THE SEAL

Every window is a hole in the envelope with a perimeter, and on a curved shell
that perimeter is not in one plane.

That is the detail to get right, and the way to make it easy is the same as
everywhere else in this book: make the opening flat and local -- a sub-frame
-- and seal to the sub-frame rather than to the curve.
