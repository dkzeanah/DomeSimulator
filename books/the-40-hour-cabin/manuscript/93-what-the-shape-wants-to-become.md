# 93. What the Shape Wants to Become

You could invent a hundred products out of split wedges. It is better to invent
six, because six archetypes cover the ground and each one follows from something
the last chapters established rather than from a catalogue. Here they are, and the
three that need a picture have one.

![The separated-chord beam, the hollow column and the box, each drawn from the real split sections.](grammar-archetypes.png)

## The axial stick

The simplest. One sector, used where the load runs straight down its grain — a
truss web, a space-frame strut, a diagonal brace. Its weak bending axis never
gets asked a question, because the triangulated frame around it carries the
sideways loads. This is the home for the fine splits, the W16 and W32, whose weak
axis is feeble but whose job here is only to be straight and hold their length.

## The balanced dyad

Chapter {{ch.dyad}}'s two-piece primitive, used as a member in its own right:
symmetric, taper cancelled, two broad faces to connect through. Most of the
assemblies below are built out of dyads rather than single sectors.

## The separated-chord beam

The most promising beam in the whole idea, and not a new one — it is the topology
the radial-sawn research built and tested (Chapter {{ch.rating}} cites it).
Opposing sectors become the top and bottom chords; a light web — plywood,
lattice, or fine sectors on the diagonal — runs between them and carries the
shear. The point is to get the wood *far from the neutral axis* and then connect
it, which is Chapter {{ch.connect}}'s law made into a shape: the further apart the
chords and the better the web, the stiffer the beam, climbing with the square of
the spacing.

## The hollow column

Instead of bundling sectors solid at the middle, arrange them in a ring around a
void. For a given amount of wood, pushing it outward buys resistance to buckling,
and the hole in the middle is not waste — it is where the services go: wire,
pipe, a threaded tension rod, the hardware of a node. The column stops being a
sawn post and becomes something closer to a wooden tube.

## The box

Four sector groups closed into a hollow box or ring. This is the cure for the fine
splits' one real weakness — their tiny weak-axis stiffness — because a closed
section is stiff in every direction and in torsion too. A W32 that is useless as a
lone joist is excellent as one wall of a box, where the box, not the blade, is the
member. This is recursive reinforcement correcting the primitive's weakness rather
than fighting it.

## The rib shell

The fine blades again, but many of them, connected by a skin and cross-ribs into a
curved surface: a vaulted roof, a ribbed wall, a gridshell. Here the depth of the
blade is the rib's depth, pointed the way the surface curves, and the skin
distributes load between ribs the way sheathing does between studs — as far as its
own connection allows, which Chapter {{ch.rating}} is careful not to overstate.

## One sorting rule under all of them

Notice what the six archetypes quietly ask of the pile of wood. Long, straight,
clear members want to be chords and long beams. Shorter or slightly crooked ones
want to be space-frame struts, where a joint every few feet is no sin. Fine or
defect-limited pieces want to be webs, ribs and blocking. The structure is, in
effect, a sorting machine for whatever the tree turns out to be — which is the
opposite of asking every tree to produce the same rectangle.
