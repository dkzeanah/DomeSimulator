# 95. Standardize the Node, Not the Tree

Here is the design decision that turns the idea from a clever way to saw a log
into a building system, and it is the opposite of the instinct most people have.

The instinct is to machine every wedge until it is identical — plane the faces,
trim the point, bring every member to a dimension — so the pieces are
interchangeable like studs. That throws away the whole advantage. A natural
wedge is variable on purpose: it is cheap because nothing was removed, and it
carries its tree's own grain and taper, which Chapter {{ch.dyad}} just showed are
usable information.

So do not standardize the member. **Standardize the interface.** Let the stick
stay variable, and make the five places it connects repeatable.

## Five fittings

* **The end shoe.** A metal, plywood or hardwood fitting that captures a
  sector near its end and turns an irregular point into a repeatable connector.
  Then beams, trusses and columns connect shoe to shoe, not arbitrary wood face
  to arbitrary wood face — which is a large simplification, because the shoe is
  identical even when the wood inside it is not.
* **The side saddle.** The two sawn radial faces of a sector are flat and
  square to each other — the same two reference faces every jig in this book
  locates on (Chapter {{ch.jig}}). A saddle grips both of them and spreads load
  over a length of the member instead of concentrating it at the fragile point.
* **The shear spline.** The piece that runs between a pair of chords and carries
  the longitudinal shear — the thing that, by Chapter {{ch.connect}}'s law, is
  the difference between a bundle and a beam. Plywood webs, solid diagonals, fine
  sectors on the bias, bolted plates: all candidates, none interchangeable until
  tested, because each has a different load-slip stiffness.
* **The bundle collar.** A band at intervals along a column or a multi-sector
  chord that holds the cross-section in its arrangement — restrains it from
  spreading, maintains the symmetry, gives bracing and floors something to
  attach to — without machining the whole member to do it.
* **The node hub.** For a space frame: a fitting several members plug into, so
  the tree-derived pieces are simply axial struts between controlled points. This
  is the easiest path to using the fine splits, because a triangulated frame asks
  nothing of a member's weak bending axis.

## Where the connector goes

Two faces, not the point. The point is the member's weakest wood (Chapter
{{ch.pith}}) and its narrowest spot — a fine W64 is barely an inch across its
bark face, which is no place for a bolt to behave the way it does in a
two-by-four. The two broad radial faces are where there is side grain, width and
room. Put the fittings there, and the dyad's two outward faces stop being a
geometric curiosity and become the system's standard connecting surface.

Standardize the node, and the tree is allowed to be a tree.
