# 21. Forty Frames, Lifted Whole

<!-- concept: wedge/panels -->
<!-- concept: why/panels -->
<!-- concept: wedge/assemble -->
<!-- concept: build/hubless_intro -->

The shell of this dome is not assembled stick by stick in the air. It is
{{dome.panels}} separate triangular frames, each one finished on the ground and
lifted into place whole: three members each, closed into their own triangle,
none of them shared with a neighbour. {{dome.panels}} frames of three is
{{dome.members}} members.

That changes where the precise work happens. Each triangle is built flat, on a
bench, at waist height, where a square end and a screw are easy (Chapter
{{ch.jig}}). What happens up a ladder is bolting finished frames to each other.
Nobody is holding three sticks at an angle in the wind. There is not one
connector between two triangles that has to be bought, machined or waited for --
every joint between panels is a lap between two flat sides.

![Forty frames, built flat, lifted whole.](plate-forty-frames.png)

## A panel that cannot fall through

<!-- concept: why/keystone -->
<!-- concept: byod/panel_lip -->

Here is the property that turns a frame into a chassis.

A wedge gets wider toward its point. So the opening in the middle of a closed
triangle is not a plain hole; its sides slope, because each member's faces lean.
Which way they lean depends on which way the members point. Turn them so the
opening is wider on one side than the other, and a panel dropped in from that
side **seats on the taper and is physically stopped**. It is a keystone.

Which means a panel is not cladding nailed onto a frame. It is a module in a
socket. You want an air conditioner: it goes in a panel. You want a window, a
vent, a solar collector, a hatch: each is a panel that drops into the same
seat. This is the frame-level reason for the argument of Chapter {{ch.hotswap}}.

![The opening tapers; the panel seats.](plate-keystone.png)

## Four rotations, four buildings

<!-- concept: why/features -->

Stiffness was one reason to choose which way the wedge faces (Chapter
{{ch.member}}). Turning it changes the building in other ways too, measured off
the same cross-section:

- **Point in** (this book's reference): the panel is caught from the inside, so
  it goes in from inside and cannot fall out; and the services run on the warm
  side.
- **Point out**: the panel is caught from outside, and the channel's services sit
  toward the weather.
- **Turned sideways**, either way: the opening's sides go parallel. No lip, so
  panels need fixings, but the sawn faces come much closer to flat, which is what
  a sheet of sheathing wants to land on.

Same stick, same wood. The choice is which building you are making.

## What holds it together

<!-- concept: why/bracket -->
<!-- concept: why/joinery -->

The first frame of this project was held together with folded steel V brackets:
a strip bent to the corner angle, seated inside the vee between two struts, with
long legs screwed down each one. A V bracket does not care what section it is
holding, which is exactly why the salvage dome of Chapter {{ch.ugly}} could use
whatever the chainsaw made.

The wedge frame does not need it, because every member is the same shape as
every other one, so the joint gets to be simpler:

- **Inside a triangle**, the members are glued on their mating faces and screwed
  in from the far side of the neighbour, so the fastening pulls the joint closed
  rather than hanging off it.
- **Between triangles**, panels bolt to each other edge to edge, and the bolt
  passes through the seam key that was going in anyway (Chapter
  {{ch.pinwheel}}).

No brackets to order, no hubs to weld, no single part the whole build waits on.
