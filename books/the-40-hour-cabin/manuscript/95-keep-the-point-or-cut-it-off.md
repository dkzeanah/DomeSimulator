# 95. Keep the Point, or Cut It Off

Every full sector runs all the way to the pith, which means its pointed apex is
made of the youngest wood in the tree — the juvenile core laid down in the first
years, before the trunk settled into its mature habit. That wood is different, and
not in a flattering direction.

## The point is the worst wood, in the worst place

Two things meet at the apex, and they compound.

The first is quality. Juvenile wood is weaker and less stiff than the mature wood
further out; cited work on loblolly pine found it could cost up to about
{{grammar.juvenile_pct}} per cent of the tested properties, and left the piece
more sensitive to how the rings sit relative to the load. The second is geometry.
Chapter {{ch.families}} showed that the apex is the *extreme fibre* of a sector
bent the strong way — the point is the furthest wood from the neutral axis, the
place that sees the most stress. So the member is asked to work its hardest
exactly where its wood is weakest, and the point is also where drying checks open
and where a knife-edge is easy to damage in handling.

## Consider cutting it off

The move that follows is to stop insisting on the whole sector. Instead of a full
triangle running to a point, take an *annular* sector: truncate the apex to a
small flat, and lose the pith.

Cutting the point off can remove the checked heart and much of the juvenile core,
create a second flat datum to build from, blunt the fragile edge, make bundling
easier, and — when sectors are reassembled into a ring — leave a deliberate
service void down the middle.

:::box against
**This is a hypothesis, not a free win.** Removing the point changes the area,
the second moment and the section modulus — the very numbers Chapter
{{ch.families}} is built on — and where to make the cut is a question for testing,
not a figure to assign by eye. A structural system does not need to use the last
cubic inch of the tree. It needs the most useful structure per tree felled and
hour worked, and those are not the same optimisation.
:::

## Keep the member's story with it

A commodity stud is anonymous; a wedge does not have to be. Because its position
in the tree is useful — the mate is on the far side, the taper reverses, the
defects sort by length — every member is worth a short record: which tree, which
section up the trunk, which sector index and its mate, its length and its two
end diameters, its moisture, and a note of any knot or check. That record is what
lets the software pair "07 of 32" with "23" and reverse one of them, and it is
what turns a defect from waste into a sorting decision — long and clear to the
chords, short and knotty to the triangulated frames, the rest to blocking and
fibre. The tree stops being a pile of interchangeable sticks and becomes a graded
inventory that still remembers where it came from.
