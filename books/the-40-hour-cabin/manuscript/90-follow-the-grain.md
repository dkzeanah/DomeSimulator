# 90. Follow the Grain

A two-by-four pretends to be one material. It is sawn out of the log at
whatever angle the grid of the mill happens to land, so by the time it is on the
rack the saw has thrown away the information about which way its fibres run and
which way its rings curve. You buy it as if it were the same stuff in every
direction. It is not.

Wood is three different materials along three different axes, and it is worth
naming them, because the whole case for a radial split is that the split keeps
all three on the member instead of erasing them.

## Three axes

* **L, longitudinal** — along the tree, the way the grain runs. This is the
  strong, stiff direction, the one that holds up a mast and a rafter.
* **R, radial** — pith to bark, straight out from the centre. On a split
  sector this is the member's *depth*.
* **T, tangential** — around the rings. On a sector this is the member's
  *width*, across the bark face.

A sawn sector arrives with those three axes still legible: the grain runs the
length of it, the depth is the radius, the width is a slice of the
circumference. Nothing has been rotated into an anonymous rectangle. So a
drawing can say what it means — *load this along L, let its depth carry the
bending in R* — instead of hoping the stick happens to be oriented well.

## Load along the tree

The default job of a wedge should be the one its grain is best at: tension or
compression straight down its own length. That is what makes the natural first
homes for these members the ones where the load is axial — truss chords and
diagonals, posts and ties, arch ribs, the struts of a space frame. The dome is
already this: its members spend their lives mostly holding their own length
against the shell's push.

Loading a wedge across the grain — squeezing it in R or T — is a different and
weaker proposition, and a joint that bears on end grain is less predictable than
one that bears on a side face. The grammar's rule of thumb falls out of that:
put the connectors on the two flat radial faces, where there is side grain and
room, not on the pith point.

## Area is inventory, not a grade

Chapter {{ch.member}} compared a sector to a stud "by area," and
Chapter {{ch.families}} showed why that single number is not enough. Here is the
replacement, and it is three numbers, not one:

* **area** — how much wood is there, which sets the axial load it can take;
* **I and S** — how that wood is distributed, which sets how it bends;
* **connection width** — whether there is enough face to land a fastener on
  without splitting the piece.

"One-point-four two-by-fours by area" is a statement about the first of those
and silent about the other two. It is an inventory line, not a structural grade.

## Three gates, across one tree

Those metrics become a decision when you ask them of a real trunk at a real size.
A split family has to pass three gates, and a wedge can clear one and fail
another: is there a stick's worth of wood in the sector at all; is the bark face
wide enough to land a connector on; and how much of the sector's share of the rim
