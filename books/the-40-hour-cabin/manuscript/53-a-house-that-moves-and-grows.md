# 53. A House That Moves, and Grows

<!-- concept: byod/why_owner -->

Why would anybody put their own money into a dome, when they could rent?

Because a small home you own can start simple and become more -- more
comfortable, larger, better equipped -- as your life changes, and every
dollar of that stays yours. The target for the most basic version of this
system is a ${{park.dome_target}} dome that leans on the pad for the
essentials. That is a design target, not a finished-home quote, and Chapter
{{ch.host}} has already shown what happens to targets when the arithmetic
gets to them. But it is the right shape of target: the smallest building that
is a home, on a pad that supplies what a small building cannot.

The durable money goes somewhere specific, and the rest of this chapter is
where.

## The rooms can change

<!-- concept: byod/rooms -->
<!-- concept: byod_deepseek/rooms -->

I live in a trailer. Four bedrooms, a living room, a den, an office, two
bathrooms, a kitchen and a dining area -- every allotment a house is supposed
to have. What changed was not the house. It was us. I was gone for six years
and came back. Three people became four. Eventually that same house will be
too much for one person, and none of its walls will have moved an inch.

A dome does not have that problem, because its walls are not holding anything
up. The shell carries the load; the partitions inside are furniture with
ambitions. Split a big bedroom to make an office. Three years later you
retire, stop needing the office, and open the bedroom back up. The services
stay where Chapter {{ch.pad_kit}} put them -- in the middle -- and the rooms
rearrange around that fixed point instead of around a plumber's quote.

## Semi-nomadic

<!-- concept: dome_park/move -->
<!-- concept: byod/move -->
<!-- concept: byod/crossover -->

This is the part a lease cannot do at any price.

Not a caravan you tow away on a whim. **A house that comes apart.** The
working estimate is {{tt_growth.setup_fast}} days at the quick end and
{{tt_growth.setup_slow}} for something large or heavily layered: disassemble,
transport, reassemble, connect. That is a real burst of effort and nobody
should pretend otherwise -- but it is a burst, not a condition. You spend it
when a move is worth it, and the rest of the time you are simply living in a
house.

Off one pad, onto another. A different park, a different city, a different
state. No lease broken, no deposit argued over, no walk-through, nothing left
behind -- and every improvement you ever made goes with you. Moving the dome
between pads is ${{tt_net.transport}} in the park model.

Chapter {{ch.network}} found the stay length where owning starts to win:
{{net.crossover_months}} months. Under that, rent; a short let is the right
answer and this book is not going to pretend otherwise. Over it, the gap
opens and keeps opening. That is the stay neither a hotel nor a twelve-month
lease was ever designed for: **too long to be a guest, too uncertain to sign
for a year.**

![Lift, drive, set down, plug in.](plate-semi-nomadic.png)

:::box against
**A dome is worth less the day after you buy it.** The park model takes a
{{tt_net.haircut_pct}} percent secondhand haircut on day one, because you may
build your dream home but only you value every choice in it at what you paid.
After a year on a pad, the house it prices is worth ${{tt_net.recovered_12}}.
That loss is in every owning figure in this Part, which is why they can be
trusted.
:::

## One hardware set, three sizes

<!-- concept: byod/hardware -->

Here is where the durable money should go. Take one design in the Dome
Creator and move a single slider -- the radius -- holding everything else
exactly where it was:

    {{park.w_hardware}}

From {{park.hw_small_ft}} feet across to {{park.hw_large_ft}}, the counts do
not move: {{park.hw_struts}} struts, {{park.hw_hubs}} hubs,
{{park.hw_panels}} panels. The hub bill does not move either --
${{park.hw_hub_usd}} at both ends. What grows is the stick and the skin,
because those are priced by length and by area. That is
{{park.hw_floor_x}} times the floor off the same list of parts.

<!-- concept: byod/growth -->

For the wedge frame in this book, the plan is the same idea at three steps:

    {{park.w_growth}}

Start with a six-foot member. Keep the connectors, collect the longer
sticks over time, and the next house is a rebuild with parts you already own
rather than a purchase.

That illustrates the opportunity. It does **not** prove that a connector
sized for the small house can hold up the large one. The hardware has to be
qualified for the whole range of sizes and loads first, and the skin, the
insulation and the pad all have to grow with the frame. Past the rated span,
change the hardware or the frequency.

## A house that gets warmer every winter

<!-- concept: byod_deepseek/r_value -->

I stood lookout outside the skin of a ship inside the Arctic Circle, for
hours at a time, over months. There is one way to stay warm out there: layers
on layers on layers, and then a wind-breaking, watertight suit over the whole
lot. You look like the Michelin man. You also stay alive.

A dome with a removable shell can work the same way. Lift the shell, add a
quilted layer -- about {{tt_quilt.shirts_per_layer}} recycled t-shirts' worth
-- and put the shell back over it. Which means the R-value stops being a
number somebody decided on the day the house was built:

    {{park.w_layers}}

The first layer pays for itself in months. The seventh takes years. That is
one over R, and it is exactly why deciding the whole R-value on day one is the
wrong move: add the layer that is worth adding this year, and the house gets
better at heating and at cooling for as long as you own it.

Two honest limits. Seven layers is R-{{tt_ladder.r7}} -- a good wall, not a
code-beating one. And the ladder prices conduction only. It does not price
the windbreak, and the windbreak is what makes layers work; that is the
shell's job, and Chapter {{ch.hats}} is about it. A real assembly also has to
answer for moisture and for fire, which a stack of quilts on a model does not.
