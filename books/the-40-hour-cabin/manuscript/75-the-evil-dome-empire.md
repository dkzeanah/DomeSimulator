What if a house was not really a finished product? What if it was a platform?

Build the geodesic skeleton once. Keep the bones. Then replace, repair,
upgrade, mutate, Frankenstein, and occasionally bolt questionable things onto
it for the next fifty years.

That is what fascinates me about geodesic construction. The dome is not just a
weird round house for hippies, Bond villains, mathematicians and people who own
suspicious amounts of polycarbonate. It could be an entirely different building
paradigm. Think of building a PC: keep the chassis, upgrade the components. And
apparently I have decided that this is now my problem to investigate.

## The world's poorest supervillain

<!-- concept: hype/combination -->
<!-- concept: hype/teardown -->
<!-- concept: hype/organs -->
<!-- concept: hype/supervillain -->

Programmer, plus electronics technician, plus fabricator, plus engineering
student, plus scavenger, plus Alabama hobo-redneck, plus aspiring science
madman. A perfectly stable combination.

I like programming things. I like wiring things. I like woodworking and
metalworking. I like taking things apart. I like finding machinery somebody
threw away and immediately thinking: excellent, the organs are still good.
Give me discarded motors, bearings, sheet metal, wiring harnesses, switches,
sensors, lumber, gears and random military surplus, and I become the world's
poorest supervillain.

That mentality is most of the Frankendome of the last chapter. When the
material is cheap, salvaged or already considered garbage, **failure becomes
affordable.** Drill the wrong hole? I do it often. Cut something too short?
Again? Excellent. Wire it wrong? Educational. Discover your revolutionary
invention is actually a terrible idea? Congratulations: you have conducted
science, and nobody has to hold a funeral for the four-hundred-dollar precision
component you just murdered.

Eventually you stop seeing junk. You see components temporarily trapped inside
the wrong machine. As a kid I loved Operation Junkyard. In hindsight I was
clearly receiving vocational training.

Which brings us to my completely reasonable long-term business plan: **the
Evil Dome Empire.** Chapter {{ch.why_me}} gave its three phases. Here is the
revenue model.

Need the Imperial Arctic Insulation Package? That will cost you. Fancy
greenhouse panels? Money, please. Solar-integrated exterior shell? Straight
into the treasury. Luxury window module? Your tribute has been accepted. It is
printers and ink cartridges, except the printer is your house. And eventually
I shall sit inside an enormous spherical fortress overlooking thousands of
domes, and rule benevolently, but firmly, over the Hoboclass.

Unfortunately, my flawless strategy has one catastrophic weakness. **The
Hoboclass owns power tools.**

I will unveil a magnificent four-thousand-dollar Imperial Replacement Wall
Module, and somebody will stare at it for twelve seconds and say: brother,
that is plywood, insulation and sheet metal. Three hours later their dome has
a salvaged RV window, half a solar panel, two cheap brackets, a door off a
chicken coop, and something they found beside the highway bolted to it.

My monopoly: destroyed. My empire: humiliated. My customer: now completely
self-sufficient. And standing proudly in front of me, the natural predator of
proprietary modular architecture: **Frankendome.**

## Now the serious part

<!-- concept: hype/asymmetry -->
<!-- concept: hype/friend -->

Everything up to here was a bit. This part is not, so I am going to drop the
voice and say it.

Institutional capital is buying single-family housing at a scale that is
genuinely difficult to picture. They have trillions of dollars and entire
departments. I have the leftover remains of one prototype and the cheapest
chainsaw in the store. I am not pretending that is a fair fight. I am telling
you it is the actual balance of forces, and I am doing it anyway.

![The actual balance of forces.](plate-balance-of-forces.png)

And here is the thing I want you to sit with. You know somebody who is never
going to afford a house. Not because they are lazy and not because they did
anything wrong. Because the arithmetic stopped working. You know exactly who
they are. You thought of them just now.

So the ask is small: put this in front of one person who can carry it further
than I can. Homesteaders, builders, developers, makers, preppers, people who
crowdfund, creators of every description -- reach is reach, and I am not in a
position to be picky. One share from somebody with an audience does more than a
month of me in a field with a chainsaw.

If it reaches somebody who wants to put weight behind it, it turns into tools:
a CNC router, a laser cutter (said in the correct voice), cameras so the
documentation stops being the bottleneck, and the power and space to run all
of it. None of that is a salary. Every bit of it turns straight back into
parts, and into footage of parts. I am not going to beg for it and I am not
going to dance for it. I am going to keep building either way. It would simply
happen faster with tooling.

## Who it is for

<!-- concept: hype/shop -->
<!-- concept: hype/instrument -->
<!-- concept: hype/forwho -->

And honestly, that is the point. I do not just want to build domes. I want to
document the geometry, the fabrication, the structure, the energy systems, the
modular panels, the failures, the experiments, the software, the electronics
and the weird prototypes well enough that somebody else can take the idea and
run somewhere I would never have thought to go.

A dome could become a house. A workshop. A greenhouse. A cabin. A disaster
shelter. An off-grid power station. A modular research lab. A timber-frame
monster made from trees you milled yourself, because apparently buying lumber
normally was not entertaining enough.

And you should not need a hundred-thousand-dollar shop to experiment. Maybe you
have a circular saw, a drill, some reclaimed lumber, YouTube, and questionable
confidence. Plus the most important engineering instrument ever created: *I
wonder if this will work.*

That is who this is for. Normal people. DIY builders, homesteaders,
programmers, fabricators, tinkerers, people learning as they go. People whose
workshop looks like a tornado hit a hardware store. People who look at a pile
of scrap and accidentally start designing infrastructure.

I want to find out how cheap, modular, repairable, scalable and adaptable
geodesic construction can actually become. If conventional construction has
no interest in that, it is fine: we will build weird triangular houses in the
woods until somebody notices.

The mission remains simple. Spread geodesic domes through civilisation like a
highly efficient modular plague. Establish the Evil Dome Empire. Rule the
Hoboclass. Accidentally teach the Hoboclass to manufacture all my products
themselves. Destroy my own recurring revenue. Continue anyway.

Because in the end: Frankendome always wins. Follow the experiments -- every
one of them, including the ones that fail, is published as it is made.
