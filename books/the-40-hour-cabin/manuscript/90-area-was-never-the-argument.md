# 90. Area Was Never the Argument

Chapter {{ch.member}} ended on a crossover: below a certain trunk diameter a
split sector is the weaker member, and above it the stronger. That chapter was
defending the wedge against the two-by-four. This one stops defending it,
because the comparison it was making — one sector against one stud, at one
size — was only ever half the picture.

Hold the wood still. Take exactly one dressed two-by-four's worth of
cross-section, {{grammar.stud_area}} square inches, and ask what *shape* that
wood is in when the same log is split into eighths, then sixteenths, then
thirty-seconds, then sixty-fourths. That is the one figure in this part I want
you to look at before you read its caption.

![The same two-by-four's worth of wood, split four ways. The finer the split, the deeper and narrower the blade.](grammar-families.png)

## Four shapes, not four sizes

They are not four sizes of one stick. The eighth is a stubby wedge. The
sixty-fourth, holding the identical amount of wood, is a blade ten inches deep
and an inch across. And the thing that matters about a beam is not how much
wood it has — that decides how much it can carry straight down its own length —
but how that wood is arranged around the axis it bends about.

The two numbers that measure the arrangement are the second moment of area and
the section modulus. Here they are for each family, every figure read off the
same cross-section routine the dome is solved with, so a sixty-fourth in this

## The blade sharpens as you split

Read the two right-hand columns down the page. Strong-axis stiffness climbs the
whole way — a W32 of one stud's wood has {{grammar.w32_strong}} times the stud's
strong-axis second moment, and a W64 has {{grammar.w64_strong}} times — because
the same wood is being carried further and further from the bending axis.

Weak-axis stiffness does the opposite. It collapses. That same W32 has only
{{grammar.w32_weak}} of a stud's second moment across its narrow direction.

A fine wedge, in other words, is a structural blade: superb in one direction,
feeble in the one at ninety degrees to it.

:::box against
**This is the number that bites.** A fine sector used on its own, as a little
joist, is weaker sideways than the stud you were trying to replace, and it will
buckle out of plane long before its deep direction is anywhere near its limit.
The weak axis does not go away because the strong axis is impressive.
:::

## Which is exactly how to use it

The collapse is a defect only if you ask a blade to be a joist. Ask it to be
what its shape wants — a flange, a rib, a lamella, a chord — and the weakness
is somebody else's job: a neighbour, a web, a skin, a tie, standing the blade
up in the direction it is strong and holding the direction it is not.

That is the turn this whole part takes. The dome already does it, without
saying so: every interior seam carries two sectors, point to point, and the
panel on each side braces the one across the gap. The chapters that follow are
what happens when you take that same member off the dome and let it be a beam, a
column, a frame — on purpose, and everywhere.

Area was the inventory. The shape is the argument.
