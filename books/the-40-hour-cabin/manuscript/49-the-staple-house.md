# 49. The Staple House
<!-- concept: cabin_staple/open -->
<!-- concept: cabin_staple/first -->

Ask the man who builds these domes what one is, and he will not reach for a
word like *shelter* or *dwelling*. He reaches for the counter at a burger
chain.

> The dome is a McDouble. Cheap, fast, reliable, effective, economic,
> consistent, known everywhere. It is the substance of the whole menu -- the
> staple. An eighteen-year-old does not need his first home to be a
> five-course meal. A filling, honest, everyday meal gets him through the day
> and lets him save for the upper rungs. Without that staple in the housing
> market, most people never get in at all, and the thousand small cuts belong
> to somebody who is not you. With it, it regulates the whole machine. It is
> the baseline.

That is the whole idea of this book in one paragraph, and it is worth taking
apart, because every word in it is a claim that can be checked.

## What a staple is
<!-- concept: cabin_staple/staple -->

A staple is not the best thing on the menu. It is the thing that is *always
there*, always the same, priced so that nobody has to think twice, and known
so well that nobody has to read the ingredients to trust it. It is not
exciting. It does not need to be. Its job is to make sure that everybody who
walks in the door walks out fed.

A housing market without one works the other way round. The cheapest new
thing on it is still a project: a lot to find, a plan to draw, a loan to
qualify for, a builder to wait for, a list of trades that each send their own
bill. The first rung of the ladder is a long way off the ground, and a great
many people never reach it.

The dome in this book is built to be the staple: one body, built the same way
every time, from a parts list short enough to read in one sitting, with
every cut published and every price on one sheet. A whole family can learn it,
build it, keep it and pass it on. It is meant to be the first home, not the
last one -- the one that gets you through the day while you save for the rest.

## Why a baseline regulates everything
<!-- concept: cabin_staple/baseline -->

The second half of the owner's paragraph is the bolder claim: that a staple
*regulates the whole machine*. It is the claim this book can least prove, so
it is worth being exact about what it means.

A staple sets a floor that everything else is measured against. When there
is a ${{staple.dome_price}} building that is dry, serviced and movable, a
${{staple.mhs_same}} manufactured home of the same floor has to say what the
other ${{staple.mhs_gap}} buys, and a
${{staple.site_same}} site-built one has to say it again. The dome is
{{staple.dome_vs_mhs_pct}} per cent of the one and {{staple.dome_vs_site_pct}} per
cent of the other. Some of the difference buys real things -- a bigger
kitchen, a bedroom door, a bank that will lend on it. Some of it is
papercuts. A staple on the menu is how a buyer finds out which is which.

That is a hope, not a measurement. Nobody has yet built enough of these for
it to move any market, and this book will not pretend otherwise. What can be
said is smaller and certain: for one family, the staple turns *can we ever
get in?* into *what do we add next?*

## A family event, not a transaction
<!-- concept: cabin_staple/family -->

The other thing a staple is, at the counter, is *known*. Nobody needs a
professional to explain it. That is the part of the analogy this book cares
about most.

A house bought the usual way is a transaction between a buyer and a long
row of people the buyer never meets -- {{staple.hands}} sets of hands in every
stud before it reaches the yard, {{staple.building_lines}} separately priced
lines in the building, and a lender for thirty years. A dome built the way
this book describes is closer to a family event: felling, splitting, a jig on
the grass, forty panels, a weekend of lifting. The children learn the
triangle. Somebody learns the saw. The building that results is fully known
to the people who live in it -- every part on one list, every cut in one
book -- and it can be added to a panel at a time over a lifetime, instead of
paid for a month at a time over one.

That is what *transparent* means here. Not that housing is simple -- it
is not -- but that the first rung of it can be, if somebody builds the first
rung to be a staple instead of a project.

## The menu above the staple
<!-- concept: cabin_staple/close -->

A staple does not end the menu. It is what makes the menu possible. The rest
of this book is the menu above the dome: the panels, the fit-outs, the
network of pads, the bigger and smaller frames. Every one of them is
optional. The staple underneath is not.
