# 61. The Twelve, One at a Time

<!-- concept: world/world_open -->
<!-- concept: world_chatgpt/ms_tools -->
<!-- concept: world_chatgpt/ms_creator -->

The Dome Creator is one of the tools that came with this book: a walkable
site where you click the ground and an avatar walks there, click a panel and
swap it -- glass, shingle, solar, plastic sheeting -- and change the
frequency, the radius, the frame material or the foundation, while a bill of
materials updates as you go: cut lists, panel weights, costs, even the number
of trees to harvest. It reads the same geometry module as every other tool in
the project, so a strut length in one cannot disagree with the same strut in
another.

It ships with {{cat.count}} finished designs. This chapter is all of them,
each one rebuilt from the preset the tool loads and every figure read off that
model. Nothing here is a drawing of a dome. It is the dome.

The same engine makes a {{cat.d11_sqft}}-square-foot canopy dome and a
{{cat.d03_sqft}}-square-foot lodge, and the only things that changed between
them are a frequency, a radius and a material list. The equipment count is the
part most catalogues cannot show at all: every bench and tank in these
buildings has a weight, a price and a power draw in the model, and the wiring
was routed to reach it.

## The timber four

<!-- concept: world/show_00 -->
<!-- concept: all_domes/show_00 -->

**{{cat.d00_name}}** -- {{cat.d00_freq}}V, {{cat.d00_sqft}} sq ft,
${{cat.d00_usd}}. The one the tool opens on, and the one every comparison in
the next chapter starts from. Dimensional lumber on metal brackets, plywood
between the struts, a concrete slab under it, and a working shop on the floor:
an assembly bay, wood and metal benches, storage and an office corner. If you
want to know what a dome costs as a *building* rather than as an idea, this is
the honest starting point.

<!-- concept: world/show_01 -->
<!-- concept: all_domes/show_01 -->

**{{cat.d01_name}}** -- {{cat.d01_freq}}V, {{cat.d01_sqft}} sq ft,
${{cat.d01_usd}}. Raise the frequency to four and the shell gets smoother,
rounder and far more complicated: {{cat.d01_struts}} struts instead of
{{cat.d00_struts}}, in more length classes. Every panel is glass on a wood
deck. It shows what smoothness costs -- not in dollars first, but in part
variety, which is the thing that actually slows a build down.

![The glass studio loft.](plate-glass-loft.png)

<!-- concept: world/show_02 -->
<!-- concept: all_domes/show_02 -->

**{{cat.d02_name}}** -- {{cat.d02_freq}}V, {{cat.d02_sqft}} sq ft,
${{cat.d02_usd}}. The one this book argues for: hubless doubled framing from
quarter wedges split straight out of logs, cedar shakes over plywood. Not one
hub connector in the building. Every triangle brings its own three members and
bolts to its neighbours, and the model reports the trees to harvest, four
wedges to a log.

<!-- concept: world/show_03 -->
<!-- concept: all_domes/show_03 -->

**{{cat.d03_name}}** -- {{cat.d03_freq}}V, {{cat.d03_sqft}} sq ft,
${{cat.d03_usd}}. The tree used whole. The radius is set so the longest
member lands just under twenty feet, which is what two people can actually
move and lift. {{cat.d03_struts}} round trunks with canvas between them,
gravel underneath: the largest floor in the catalogue by a wide margin, framed
from the fewest pieces, and at ${{cat.d03_per_sqft}} a square foot the
cheapest floor too.

## The other eight

<!-- concept: world/show_04 -->

**{{cat.d04_name}}** -- {{cat.d04_freq}}V, {{cat.d04_sqft}} sq ft,
${{cat.d04_usd}}. The workshop's exact skeleton, in aluminium and
polycarbonate, with grow racks and water storage inside. The geometry does not
change at all; only the material list does -- which is exactly the argument
of this whole Part. One frame, many buildings.

![The grow dome.](plate-grow-dome.png)

<!-- concept: world/show_05 -->

**{{cat.d05_name}}** -- {{cat.d05_freq}}V, {{cat.d05_sqft}} sq ft,
${{cat.d05_usd}}. The geometry grouped into hexagonal cells on a structural
steel frame with composite tiles, fitted out as a presentation space. Heavier
and dearer than the timber designs, and it looks like nothing else on the
site.

![The hex cell pavilion.](plate-hex-pavilion.png)

<!-- concept: world/show_06 -->
<!-- concept: all_domes/show_06 -->

**{{cat.d06_name}}** -- {{cat.d06_freq}}V, {{cat.d06_sqft}} sq ft,
${{cat.d06_usd}}. Short straight struts swapped for continuous curved steel
ribs and rings, with fabrication and site-power equipment inside, open
between the ribs. This is what the same geometry looks like when a steel shop
builds it instead of a carpenter -- and the dearest thing in the catalogue.

![The continuous steel arc hangar.](plate-arc-hangar.png)

<!-- concept: world/show_07 -->
<!-- concept: all_domes/show_07 -->

**{{cat.d07_name}}** -- {{cat.d07_freq}}V, {{cat.d07_sqft}} sq ft,
${{cat.d07_usd}}. Rebar is the cheapest structural steel there is, and it
bends, so the tool draws it as a dense lattice of meridians and rings rather
than sticks between hubs. The cheapest building in the catalogue, and the one
closest to what people actually put up in a back yard on a weekend.

![The rebar garden dome.](plate-rebar-garden.png)

<!-- concept: world/show_08 -->

**{{cat.d08_name}}** -- {{cat.d08_freq}}V, {{cat.d08_sqft}} sq ft,
${{cat.d08_usd}}. Here the frame is not the building. It is the formwork:
rebar and shuttering carry a poured concrete shell, with shoring, scaffold, a
mixer and a rebar bender on the floor. The dome is doing what concrete does
best -- standing in pure compression -- and at {{cat.d08_tonnes}} tonnes it is
by some distance the heaviest thing here.

![The concrete monocoque form.](plate-concrete-form.png)

<!-- concept: world/show_09 -->
<!-- concept: world/show_10 -->

**{{cat.d09_name}}** and **{{cat.d10_name}}** -- ${{cat.d09_usd}} and
${{cat.d10_usd}}. They exist because the renderer can do it: mirror tiles
that genuinely reflect the sky, the ground and the trunks around them, a
pavilion that disappears into a wood. The hexagonal one is the same
{{cat.d09_freq}}V shell as the workshop; the square one is
{{cat.d10_freq}}V on a steel frame, with more and smaller panels and a finer
reflection. Side by side they show exactly what frequency does to a faceted
surface: it stops looking like a machine and starts looking like a curve.

![The woodland hex mirror.](plate-hex-mirror.png)

<!-- concept: world/show_11 -->

**{{cat.d11_name}}** -- {{cat.d11_freq}}V, {{cat.d11_sqft}} sq ft,
${{cat.d11_usd}}. The smallest: a two-frequency dome on an elevated timber
platform with braces and ladder access, up in the canopy. Proof that the same
{{cat.d11_struts}}-stick frame works when the ground it stands on is not the
ground.

![The treehouse canopy dome.](plate-canopy-dome.png)
