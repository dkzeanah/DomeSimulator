# 93. Mirror the Wedge, Reverse the Taper

A single sector has a quiet asymmetry that the dome gets away with ignoring and
a beam does not. Its point and its bark face lie at different distances from the
axis it bends about, so it is lopsided in exactly the direction a beam cares
about. One sector is a *part*. The fix is to stop thinking in parts and start
thinking in a two-piece primitive.

![The dyad: a sector and its mate from the far side of the log, reversed fat-to-thin so the pair runs parallel.](grammar-dyad.png)

## Take the mate from the far side of the log

When you split a log into *N* sectors, number them as you go — 00, 01, 02, round
to the last. Then the natural partner for sector *i* is the one diametrically
opposite it:

    {{grammar.pairing}}

Opposite sectors saw the same weather, the same lean, the same pith. Whatever is
a little off about one — a shade of reaction wood on the south side, a drift in
the grain — tends to be mirrored in the other, so a joint between them averages
the two rather than stacking the quirks. Keeping the original index is the whole
trick: a member that still knows it is "07 of 32" can always find that its mate
is "23," and the software in Chapter {{ch.nodes}} can insist on the pairing
instead of hoping for it.

## Turn one end for end

A tree is a cone. Every member cut from it is a little fatter at one end than the
other, and if you lay two of them the same way round, the pair is fat at one end
and thin at the other — still tapered, just doubled.

So do not lay them the same way round. Reverse one end for end, so the fat end of
one meets the thin end of its mate. The two tapers cancel, the pair's outer edges
come back to parallel, and the lengthwise variation in the wood is averaged along
the member instead of piling up at one end. The dyad is that move, stated once so
the book and the fixtures cannot disagree about it: **opposite sector, reversed
fat-to-thin.**

## What the pair gives you

A dyad is worth more than two sticks. It is symmetric about the way it bends, so
it has no lopsided axis to design around. It presents two broad, flat faces — the
two sawn radial faces, pointing out — which is where Chapter {{ch.nodes}} wants
to put every connector. It gives two independent paths down the grain, so a flaw
in one is not a flaw in the structure. And it leaves a gap down its middle for
the thing that turns a pair of chords into one beam, which is the next chapter.

One wedge is a part. Two opposing wedges, one reversed, are a unit — and the unit
is where the grammar really starts.
