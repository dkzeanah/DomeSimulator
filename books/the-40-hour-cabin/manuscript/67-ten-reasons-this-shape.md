# 67. Ten Reasons This Shape

<!-- concept: kick/title -->

A house costs what somebody says it costs. This one costs what its parts
cost, and you can count the parts: {{tt_flat.struts}} sticks,
{{tt_flat.triangles}} triangles, {{tt_flat.processes}} operations, repeated.
That is the whole building.

The campaign films that came before this book argued for the shape in ten
points. Each one is a consequence of the geometry rather than a claim about
it, and each is worked below on the same reference dome:
{{kick.floor}} square feet of floor.

## Same floor, less building

<!-- concept: kick/problem -->
<!-- concept: kick/versus -->

Start with the shape everybody already builds: four walls, a roof, corners.
Give it {{kick.floor}} square feet of floor and you have to build, wrap, seal
and heat **{{kick.box_skin}} square feet** of outside. Every one of them costs
money twice -- once to put up, and again every winter for as long as anybody
lives there.

Put a dome beside it on exactly the same floor. It needs
**{{kick.dome_skin}}**. That is {{kick.skin_pct}} percent less to build, to
seal, to paint and to heat, forever, for the same room to stand in. Nobody
invented that. It is what the shape does.

![Same floor, less building.](plate-less-skin.png)

<!-- concept: kick/triangle -->

And it stands up for a reason Chapter {{ch.triangles}} spent a whole chapter
on. Push on a square and it leans: the corners hinge, and every rectangular
building on earth needs bracing to stop it. Push on a triangle and nothing
happens, because changing its shape means changing the length of a side, which
means breaking something. A dome is {{tt_flat.triangles}} triangles. There is nothing left to
brace.

<!-- concept: kick/wind -->

Wind is the same story. A flat wall catches everything thrown at it. A box
has a drag coefficient of about {{kick.drag_box}}; a dome, about
{{kick.drag_dome}} -- roughly {{kick.drag_pct}} percent less load out of the
shape alone, before anything is bolted down. The wind arrives, finds nothing
square to lean on, and goes over the top.

![Nothing for the wind to push on.](plate-wind.png)

## {{flat.ratio}} times the house, same parts list

<!-- concept: kick/flatrate -->
<!-- concept: kick/brackets -->

This is the part that makes it a business instead of a hobby. A
{{tt_flat.small_ft}}-foot dome has {{tt_flat.small_floor}} square feet of
floor. A {{tt_flat.large_ft}}-foot dome has {{tt_flat.large_floor}}. Both are
{{tt_flat.struts}} struts, {{tt_flat.brackets}} brackets,
{{tt_flat.screws}} screws and {{tt_flat.processes}} operations. The sticks get
longer and the parts list does not change at all. Learn to build one and you
have learned to build every size of it.

The bracket is part of why. Every joint in the campaign's frame is a strip of
flat steel with eight holes in it, folded once down the middle into a V: four
holes a side, four screws into each strut. That is the entire connector, and
it is why that frame needs no machine shop, no supplier and nobody's
permission to exist.

<!-- concept: kick/proof -->

And one already exists. It is not a rendering: it stands in a yard, framed
from four trees cut and milled by hand, with a hundred and twenty brackets
bent out of sheet metal on a bench because buying them was not an option, and
it has been up through half a year of weather without moving.

:::box against
**The first one took ten days, not {{clock.week}} hours.** It was milled, not split,
and it was the first -- every mistake in it was made for the first time. The
{{clock.week}}-hour clock in this book is a target worked from measured rates
(Chapter {{ch.raise}}), not a record anybody has set yet. The ten days are a
record. Both numbers are in the book for that reason.
:::

![One already exists.](plate-first-one.png)

## Fixing what a dome gets wrong

<!-- concept: kick2/pony -->

Now the honest complaint about domes, and the fix for it. A hemisphere has a
beautiful volume and almost none of it is against the edge, where the ceiling
comes down to meet the floor. Out of {{tt_pony.floor}} square feet you can
stand up in **{{tt_pony.usable_0}}**. The rest is crawl space with a view.

So put the whole shell on a short wall. On a three-foot stem wall the ceiling
profile lifts with it and usable floor goes to **{{tt_pony.usable_3}}** square
feet, for about ${{tt_pony.cost_3}} -- near ${{tt_pony.rate_3}} for every
square foot it buys back. It is the cheapest room in the building, and the
first thing worth building into every one of them.

![The pony wall.](plate-pony-wall.png)

<!-- concept: kick/hat -->
<!-- concept: kick2/brim -->
<!-- concept: kick2/water -->

Waterproofing is where the money goes, so here is the trick. Sort the {{tt_flat.triangles}}
triangles by height and the bottom twenty come to exactly half the surface.
Exactly -- a property of the shape, not a rounding. So laminate the top
twenty like a hat, {{kick.hat_sqft}} square feet of it, and leave the bottom
ring to the siding: half the resin, and resin is the expensive part. Chapter
{{ch.hats}} is the long version of this.

![Glass the top, skip the bottom.](plate-the-hat.png)

But a hat that stops flush with the wall is useless: the water runs down the
face and into every joint below it. So the brim stands proud, ribbed to keep it
stiff, and throws the rain clear of the bottom ring entirely. And once it
overhangs, it is a gutter. A {{tt_brim.overhang_in}}-inch brim gives
{{tt_brim.catch_sqft}} square feet of catchment, which on
{{tt_brim.rain_in}} inches of rain a year is **{{tt_brim.gallons_yr}}
gallons** -- about {{tt_brim.gallons_day}} a day, straight into a tank beside
the door. One downpipe. No pump. The roof shape does the collecting.

## What it costs to run

<!-- concept: kick2/energy -->
<!-- concept: kick2/paint -->

The number nobody puts on a listing. With the cavity insulated, this dome
takes about **${{kick.run_dome}} a year** to heat and cool -- not a month, a
year. The same floor in a square house costs ${{kick.run_box}}, because it has
{{kick.skin_gap}} more square feet of skin to lose heat through. That is
${{kick.run_saved}} a year never spent, on a building that was already cheaper
to put up.

![What it costs to run.](plate-run-cost.png)

And you can do better with paint. Radiative sky-cooling paint reflects nearly
all of the sunlight that hits it and emits strongly in the band the atmosphere
is transparent at, so the heat does not stop in the air -- it goes to space.
The shell runs roughly {{kick.paint_f}} degrees Fahrenheit cooler than a dark
one, below air temperature in full sun. The roof stops being a heat source and
becomes a heat sink.

:::box against
**On a well-insulated shell, the paint is worth about ${{kick.paint_usd}} a
year.** Very little of that heat was getting in anyway. On a thin wall it is
more like ${{kick.paint_thin_usd}}. You do it for the comfort and for the air
conditioner you do not have to buy, not for the bill.
:::

![A roof that refuses the sun.](plate-sky-paint.png)

## One skeleton, many products

<!-- concept: kick/lines -->
<!-- concept: kick/themes -->
<!-- concept: kick2/points -->

One skeleton is already four products: a home, a storage shed, a greenhouse,
a storm shelter you could put in a back yard. Four price points, four markets,
the same {{tt_flat.triangles}} triangles underneath every one. And the bones do not care what
goes on them -- a baseball, a basketball, a disco ball with a facet on every
panel. {{tt_flat.triangles}} flat faces, all replaceable, all pointing somewhere: a house that
can also be a sign, a venue or a greenhouse without changing a stick.

So the whole argument, in ten lines:

1. Less exterior for the same floor.
2. A parts list that does not grow with the house.
3. {{tt_flat.processes}} operations, endlessly repeated.
4. Rigid because of its shape, not its bracing.
5. Almost nothing for the wind to push on.
6. It costs what the parts cost.
7. A stem wall buys back the wasted rim, cheaply.
8. Less skin means less to heat.
9. A painted roof that refuses the sun.
10. A brim that collects the rain it was already throwing clear.

Every one of them is a consequence of the shape.
