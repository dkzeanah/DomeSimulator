# 40. Closing the Shell

*Forty triangles of something over forty triangles of nothing*

## Closing the Shell

The standing frame is a skeleton, and this chapter is its skin:
{{frame.panels}} triangles of something, over {{frame.panels}}
triangles of nothing, each panel closed so the weather stays on
the outside.

The options are few and the book ranks them the way it ranks
everything — by cost and behaviour, computed, not by feel. Sheet
goods dominate, because they are cheap, local and flat, and the
chapter's first two sections are the craft of sheathing forty
triangles from rectangular stock — and of saying plainly that no
panel of this dome fits a four-by-eight sheet. The
table prices the whole skin in sheets. The last section runs the
alternatives — fabric, shingle, living roof — because each one
exists for a reason and each one asks the frame for something
different, and the builder who knows all four picks the right one
instead of the first one.

## Sheathing a triangle

The craft problem the rectangle-building world never has: every
panel of a dome is a triangle, and sheet goods come in rectangles.

The answer is the panel drawing, again. Chapter
{{ch.panel_drawings}} printed the two panel shapes flat and full
size; the sheathing job is those shapes, cut in sheet, one per
panel, with the seam allowance carried around the edge. The
procedure that keeps the waste honest:

**One template, not forty measurements.** Trace each panel shape
once onto scrap, cut it, and use it as the pattern for its whole
class — the {{dm.equi_count}} of one shape, the {{dm.iso_count}} of
the other. The template is the sheathing's jig: the shape made
physical, so the saw never re-derives an angle it already cut
correctly once.

**Cut inside the line, then trim to fit.** Sheet cut to the
template's outside is always slightly big; sheet cut inside it can
be trimmed with a knife in place. The panel gets its skin offered
up, marked against its own frame, and trimmed to the frame's
actual edges — locate first, cut second, the same maxim the jig
chapters ran, applied to skin.

**Seam allowance stays outside the panel.** The skin's edge
belongs to the *seam*, not to the panel: the gasket's share, the
flashings, the overlap of the next panel's skin — all of it lives
beyond the frame's own edge, and a skin cut flush to the frame is
a skin that leaves the seam naked. Cut generous, trim at the seam,
and the {{frame.seams}} seams get the overlap the weather chapter
demands.

**Screw to the frame, not through it.** The skin fastens into the
members' faces — the wide flat of the wedge — at the spacing the
sheet's own spec asks. The skin is not structural; it is the
panel's face, and its screws' only job is to keep it flat against
the frame while the seams do the weather work.

## No panel fits a four-by-eight sheet

The second craft problem, and it is pure geometry: a dome's panels
are bigger than the sheet goods the rest of the building world
reaches for first.

No panel of this dome fits a four-by-eight sheet, and the book says
so plainly because the claim is easy to make and wrong. The
equilateral panel is {{cut.master_long_in}} inches on a side — about
62.4 inches tall — so it overhangs the four-foot width of a sheet
before any seam allowance is added, and the isosceles panel is
nearly as tall. Neither shape nests two-up in a sheet, either.

The honest version is that the panels are not cut from sheet goods
at all. They are framed from the members themselves — the split-log
sticks of Chapter {{ch.split}} — and sheet goods appear only later,
as the skin. The skin triangles inherit the same limit: a skin panel
the size of a frame panel does not fit a four-by-eight sheet singly,
so the skin is cut from larger stock or pieced from more than one
sheet, with the seam allowance carrying the joins. Where the piecing
does not reach — the odd counts, the rim panels with their
different edge — the waste goes to the offcut pile and the pile
goes to blocking, the same honest destination as every other
offcut in the book.

## Sheet count

The table is the skin's bill: shell area and sheets, computed
from the solved geometry, not estimated from the drawing.

The numbers are the answer to the question the chapter opened —
what it costs, in sheets, to close {{frame.panels}} triangles. The
area is the dome's own surface, the sheet count is what cutting
those triangles from stock actually requires, and the difference
between the two is the waste the previous section promised to keep
honest. The figure is
worth reading twice, because it is the first number in the book
that is *bought* rather than *cut* — everything until now came out
of the trees, and the skin is where the fortnight's ledger first
touches a lumberyard.

## The alternatives

Sheet goods are the default, not the only answer. The other three
exist for other builds.

**Fabric.** A membrane skin — canvas, shade cloth, a soft shell
pulled over the frame — closes the frame for the
price of cloth and rope, sheds rain where the seams are taped, and
gives up insulation and years. It is the right skin for a
seasonal dome, a greenhouse's first year, or a frame that is
still deciding; it is the wrong skin for a home, and the book
says so rather than romancing the tent.

**Shingle.** Shingling a compound surface is slow, and it works —
each shingle is small enough to follow the curve, the overlaps
shed water the way the frame's ridges want, and the result is the
most repairable roof there is: one shingle at a time, forever.
The cost is the labour, which is why shingle is the upgrade for
the dome that has already earned its place, not the first skin it
wears.

**Living roof.** Soil and plants over a waterproof layer, the
heaviest and coolest of the four, and the one that asks the most
of the frame: the chapters on load have already said who decides
whether the members carry it. It is listed because it is real and
because somebody always asks; it is not recommended for the first
build, and the reason is the engineer's sentence, not the
gardener's.

The chapter closes where the shell does: the frame is covered,
the seams are taped, and the dome is a *room* in the only sense
that matters outdoors — it keeps weather on its outside. The next
chapter cuts the holes that make it usable, and the chapter after
that is the one that decides whether the shell's honesty holds up
to the weather it was built for.
