# 55. The Six Values of One Pine

*The same tree, priced six ways: on the stump, as firewood, as sawn lumber, as split stock, as standing structure, and as mortgage payments avoided*

## The Six Values of One Pine

A tree has no price. It has a ladder of prices, and which rung you
stand on decides what the tree is worth — which is why two people
can look at the same pine and honestly quote numbers two hundred
times apart.

This chapter is the ladder, walked one rung at a time, on the
big pine the films priced: {{pine.length_ft}} usable feet,
{{pine.butt_in}} inches at the butt, {{pine.solid_bf}} board feet
of solid wood. The arithmetic comes from the same module the
twenty-dollar-pine film runs, so the book and the film cannot
disagree about the tree. The lesson the ladder teaches is the
chapter's whole point: **the wood never changes between the rungs.
The design changes what the wood is for, and the value follows
the design, not the tree.**

Chapter {{ch.middlemen}} walked the chain that prices a board.
This chapter walks the chain that prices a *tree*, and it ends
where Part Two's economics always end — with the honest floor
under the top rungs, printed rather than skipped.

## One tree, six rungs

The ladder, bottom to top, with the arithmetic at each step.

**Standing.** On the stump, the tree is stumpage: its green
weight times the going rate per ton. This pine weighs
{{pine.stump_tons}} tons green, and at the sawtimber stumpage
the film researched the tree is worth about {{pine.stump_usd}}
dollars — the twenty-dollar pine, named for this rung. The
honest footnote belongs right here: a timber buyer mostly does
not want two trees. The price exists; the buyer may not.

**Firewood.** Cut, split and stacked, the stem is
{{pine.cords}} cords of firewood — a cord being
{{pine.cord_ft3}} cubic feet of stack, about
{{pine.cord_solid_ft3}} of it wood — and at {{pine.cord_usd}}
dollars a cord, picked up, the tree is worth
{{pine.firewood_usd}} dollars. Burning is the oldest conversion
there is, and the ladder's lowest full-value rung.

**Sawn lumber.** On a mill, at the film's declared
{{pine.mill_pct}} per cent recovery, the tree yields
{{pine.mill_bf}} board feet; at {{pine.usd_per_bf}} dollars a
board foot that is {{pine.mill_usd}} dollars of lumber. The
recovery is the author's own estimate — the simulator's honest
packing of the same log into two-by-fours says
{{pine.sawn_model_pct}} per cent — and the chapter keeps both
numbers, the way Chapter {{ch.recovery}} taught.

**Split stock.** Split into wedges instead of sawn into boards,
at {{pine.wedge_pct}} per cent recovery, the same tree yields
{{pine.wedge_bf}} board feet — worth {{pine.wedge_usd}} dollars
at the same lumber price, and {{pine.extra_bf}} board feet more
than the mill kept. The split rung is the first one where the
method's own arithmetic appears, and it beats the mill on the
same tree by {{pine.gain_pct}} per cent.

**Structure.** Used as the book uses it — as framing the owner
would otherwise buy — one tree replaces {{pine.use_usd}} dollars
of commercial framing: the shell's framing value, divided by
the two trees that supplied it. The rung is a substitution
value, and the chapter says so again before the ladder is done.

**Financed.** If that avoided framing would have been carried on
a mortgage at the book's illustrative {{pine.financed_usd}}-style
terms, its lifetime payments come to {{pine.financed_usd}}
dollars per tree — the nominal rung, the tallest, and the one
most likely to be quoted without its caveats.

## The ladder, drawn

The drawing is the ladder as a staircase, and it is a staircase
on purpose: the rungs rise evenly, but the *values* do not, and
the gap between one rung and the next is the entire argument.

Look at the distances. The step from standing to firewood is
small — the tree, processed into heat. The step from firewood to
sawn lumber is a few multiples — the tree, processed into boards.
The step from sawn to structure is the jump that matters: the
same wood, asked to *be a building*, is worth on the order of
twenty times its firewood price and six times its lumber price.
And the last step, to the financed rung, is not a step the wood
takes at all — it is a step the *time* takes, the mortgage's
arithmetic standing on the framing's shoulders.

The staircase is the chapter's central object because it
answers the question every visitor to this project asks first:
how can a twenty-dollar tree be worth thousands? It cannot, and
it can. The tree is twenty dollars on the first rung and
thousands on the fifth, and nothing about the tree changed on
the way up. The ladder is the explanation, drawn.

## The wood did not get better

The ratios that embarrass the ladder, and the sentence the
chapter exists to make stick.

Structure against firewood: the framing one tree replaces is
worth {{pine.fire_ratio}} times what the same tree burns for.
Structure against sawn lumber: {{pine.mill_ratio}} times. Even
against the split stock the method itself produces, the built
structure is {{pine.wedge_ratio}} times the stock's price. And
the wood is *identical* on every rung — the same board feet, the
same fibres, the same pine. Nothing was added between the stump
and the frame except decisions.

That is the whole economics of the method, stated as a ratio
instead of an argument. The tree's value was never in the tree;
it was in what the design asked the tree to do. Firewood asks
for heat, and the tree answers with heat. A mill asks for
rectangles, and the tree answers with what rectangles fit. A
frame asks for structure, and the same tree answers with a
building's worth of it — because a triangulated frame is the
one customer that pays for the whole log, in its own shape, and
the shape is the tree's idea. The ladder does not flatter the
tree. It exposes the customers.

## What the ladder does not say

The honest floor under the top rungs, because a ladder printed
without its caveats is a sales document, and this book is not.

**Substitution is not income.** The structure rung's
{{pine.use_usd}} dollars is what the owner did not *spend*, not
what they *earned*. It converts to groceries only if the framing
was going to be bought anyway, and the builder who was never
going to buy a frame has built a shed with their own hours
instead of earning a wage — the same distinction Chapter
{{ch.money}} drew for the fortnight, drawn here for the tree.

**Financed value is future payments, not today's.** The top
rung assumes the avoided framing would have been mortgaged at
the book's illustrative rate for its full term, and then adds up
every payment. It is a real number about a counterfactual
borrower, not a cheque. Quoted alone, it is the ladder's most
flattering rung; the chapter keeps it, labelled, because the
films priced it and the book's honesty extends to its own
sources.

**The owner's net rung.** What the tree is actually worth to
the person standing on the ladder: the structure value, minus
the labour of converting it — {{pine.hours}} hours at the
owner's wage — minus the cash the build still spends. The
arithmetic lands near {{pine.net_usd}} dollars of net value per
tree, and that is the rung this book would build on: not the
tallest rung, the *owned* one.

**Different owners stand on different rungs.** The ladder's
real lesson, stated last because it is the point: the tree's
value is not a property of the tree, it is a property of the
owner's position. The timber buyer stands on rung one, the
firewood seller on rung two, the mill on rung three — and only
the builder stands on the rung where the design, not the
market, sets the price. The book is for the people on that
rung.

## The same ladder as an index

The films put this ladder on screen as a single index rather than as six
prices, and it earns a page here because the index is the more useful thing to
argue with.

The stump value counts as one. Everything above it is a multiple of that:

| rung | multiple of the stump value |
|---|---|
| On the stump | {{index.stump}}x |
| Burned, picked up | {{index.firewood}}x |
| Sawn to lumber | {{index.mill}}x |
| Split to stock | {{index.wedge}}x |
| Holding a frame up | {{index.use}}x |
| Carried on a mortgage | {{index.financed}}x |

{{index.rungs}} rungs, and one number in that table is worth more than the
rest: **{{index.use}}x**. That is the tree as *function* — the framing you no
longer have to buy — against what a timber buyer would pay for it standing.
The ratio does not depend on the price of anything: move the stumpage rate and
both sides move together, which is exactly why the index is a better argument
than the dollars it was computed from.

Two cautions, in the spirit of the section above.

**The top two rungs are not cash.** The {{index.use}}x and the
{{index.financed}}x are "what you did not have to buy" and "what you did not
have to borrow", and neither is money in a hand. A builder has {{index.use}}x
of *displaced* spending: real, and unspendable. That is the same distinction
the money chapter's stack drew between a saving and a receipt.

**The index compares a tree to a tree.** It is not an investment return and
not an interest rate. The financing rung is a nominal figure summed over a
thirty-year schedule; the firewood rung is what somebody pays at the roadside
for a cord. Read it as a ranking of uses, not as a price list.

Which leaves the sentence the films printed under the table, and it is the
chapter's whole argument in seven words: **the wood did not get better; it got
used.** Every multiple in that index is the same tree, cut the same way, and
the distance between {{index.firewood}}x and {{index.use}}x is a decision
about what to do with it — the decision this book has spent its length
teaching, and the one number in the table that is under the reader's control.

## What the ladder rests on

Every rung above came from somewhere, and the book's rule is to say where: a
price without a source is a rumour with a decimal point.

| rung | figure | where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| On the stump | {{pine.stumpage}} a ton | published stumpage for pine, per green ton; the stem weighs {{pine.stump_tons}} ton |
| Firewood | {{pine.cord_usd}} a cord | the going rate for a cord, picked up at the roadside |
| Sawn lumber | {{pine.usd_per_bf}} a board foot | one sawmill's price for the species |
| Milling | {{pine.milling_usd_per_bf}} a board foot | the author's custom-sawing rate |
| Split stock | the same price a board foot | on the {{pine.wedge_pct}} per cent the split keeps |
| Structure | the framing one tree replaces | this book's own money chapter, from the framing share of a house |
| Financed | that value on a mortgage | this book's own rate and term, summed over the schedule |

Two of those lines invite an argument and the book would rather have it than
defend them.

**The mill recovery is generous.** The ladder prices the sawn rung at
{{pine.mill_pct}} per cent of the stem; this book's own solver says
{{pine.sawn_model_pct}} per cent for boards packed out of the same round. The
higher figure is the one the films used and the one most published tables
assume, and it flatters the *lumber* rung — which makes the split rung's
advantage look smaller than it is. A reader who wants the conservative version
should re-run the ladder on the modelled number and watch the gap widen.

**The sawing rate is a rate, not a market.** A {{pine.survey_year}} survey found
custom milling running between {{pine.survey_milling_low}} and
{{pine.survey_milling_high}} dollars a board foot, against the
{{pine.milling_usd_per_bf}} this ladder uses. That is several times the low end;
it is the right number for a small mill doing one tree, and a reader with a
bandsaw and a friend is looking at a cheaper rung than the table shows.

And the financed rung, which is the tallest and the most easily misread. It
assumes the avoided framing would have been borrowed at this book's
illustrative rate for its full term and sums every payment:
{{pine.financed_usd}} per tree, in nominal dollars, over thirty years. It is a
real number about a counterfactual borrower. It is not cash, it cannot be
spent, and quoting it alone — as the ladder's flattering rung — is the one
thing the table refuses to do.

Which leaves the net rung, the one this book would actually build on. Take the
structure value, subtract the labour of converting it — {{pine.hours}} hours at
the owner's own wage, {{pine.labor_usd}} — and subtract the cash the build
still spends, {{pine.cash_usd}}: **{{pine.net_usd}} of net value per tree**, or
{{pine.net_measured_usd}} if you use the measured cash the fortnight actually
spent instead of the planning figure. Both are printed, neither is a wage, and
both are the reason this method's arithmetic survives a reader who insists on
counting their own hours.
