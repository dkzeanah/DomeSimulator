# 4. The V-Bracket Afternoon

*Trying to join shapes that have nothing in common*

## The V-Bracket Afternoon

Between the frankendome and the wedge there was an afternoon spent trying to
make the frankendome's problem go away by making a better bracket. That
afternoon matters because it is the step most builders would have stopped
at, and because the bracket it produced is a genuinely good answer to the
wrong question.

The problem was stated clearly by then: the frame's sticks did not match, so
the joint had to. The fix seemed obvious — stop improvising the joints one at
a time and design one connector good enough to join any stick to any stick.
Do the tolerance in metal, once, instead of in thinking, forty times.

So I sat down and designed the V-bracket: two flat flaps meeting at the
angle the panels make, four holes in each flap, the flaps long enough to
run half the length of a member so the screws spread along the stick instead
of bunching at its end. Fold one from flat stock in a minute on a bench
brake, and it will hold any two pieces of wood that arrive at the corner,
whatever their sections, whatever their bows.

And it worked. That is the trap, again. A bracket that absorbs every
difference works on the first panel and the fortieth. What it costs does not
show up until you look at what the bracket has become.

## A bracket that does not care

Here is why the V shape is good at its job, because the goodness is real and
the method that follows borrows from it.

A V-bracket does not assume a member's width. The flaps are wider than any
stick the build will use, so a two-inch board and a three-inch board both
land on the same plate, and the screw rows find whatever wood is there. It
does not assume a thickness: the bend that forms the V is made to the
panel's fold angle at assembly, not stamped to a tolerance at a factory. And
it does not assume straightness: the screws are what pull the stick to the
plate, and a bowed stick just gets pulled harder.

That is a beautiful property, and it is worth saying what it is in one
sentence, because the wedge method is, in a way, this sentence taken to its
conclusion: **the connector absorbs the difference, so the wood does not
have to be made the same.**

The warning sign was in the same sentence. When the connector absorbs every
difference, the connector is now doing all the work of the frame. Every
joint is a negotiation, held by screws that are the only thing in the whole
structure that know the shape they are enforcing. Eight screws a bracket,
two bolts a seam, all of it site-bent, site-drilled, site-tightened — the
connector was not a detail. It had become the building.

And a connector that does everything has to be *right* everywhere. The
flaps were long, which spread the load, which was honest engineering — and
it meant forty-odd brackets, each consuming stock, each needing folding,
drilling, fitting, checking. The frankendome had replaced the problem of
matching wood with the problem of manufacturing forgiveness, in metal, at
forty corners. Same labour, moved.

## Four joints that nearly worked

The drawing shows the four joints that happened along the way, because the
V-bracket was not arrived at in one step, and each of the earlier ones
failed in a way that named a piece of the final answer.

**The hub.** A flat plate with every member bolting to its own face — the
classic geodesic hub. It assumes all members arrive at the same point at
the same angle, and they never do when the sticks are salvaged. Every
attempt needed shims, washers, longer bolts. The hub is perfect for a kit
of identical struts, and useless the moment the struts differ.

**The angle plate.** A single fold, made to the panel's angle. It joins two
sticks in a line but cannot hold the third that closes the triangle, and
the third is the one that turns three sticks into structure.

**The wrap.** A strip of steel wrapped around both members and screwed
through. It holds anything, for a while, but it crushes nothing into
alignment — the joint stays as crooked as the wood, and every wrap is a
one-off that has to be cut to the pair it is joining.

**The V-bracket.** The survivor, and it failed too — not at holding, but at
repeating. It can join anything to anything, which is exactly why no two of
them are alike, which is exactly why the frame never gets faster to build.

![Four joints, and the one thing each could not do.](../../deliverables/book/figures/bracket-attempts.png)

The pattern across all four is the same. Each joint failed not by being weak
but by being asked to solve, in metal, a problem the wood kept re-creating.
The hub assumed sameness. The plate assumed a line. The wrap assumed
nothing and therefore had to be made for everything. The V accepted all of
it and paid in labour. None of them asked the question the next chapter
does — not *how do I join whatever arrives*, but *what would have to be
true for the sticks to arrive the same*.

## The question that ended it

The afternoon ended with the bracket in my hand doing its job, and a
question that would not go away. The bracket worked by absorbing difference,
and it had taken me all afternoon to make a bracket good at that. But every
flap length, every screw, every bend was the cost of a difference that did
not need to exist.

So: if the bracket no longer had to absorb any difference — if the sticks
arrived at the corner already the same section, already at the same angle,
already the same length — what would the joint collapse to?

The answer, worked out later, is Chapter {{ch.pinwheel}}: a member end
landing on a member side, one key, a few screws, nothing folded, nothing
measured twice. But the more important answer arrived that same afternoon,
and it is the sentence that turned the project around:

**Do not build a joint that tolerates the difference. Build sticks that do
not have one.**

That is the wedge method's founding sentence. The bracket was a good idea
pushed past its natural size; it remains in the method only as a small,
fixed part where a fold is genuinely the right tool. What it taught was
that the wood was the thing to fix, and the wood was the one thing I had
not tried changing — because a stick of wood seemed like something you buy,
not something you decide. Chapter {{ch.the_cut}} is the afternoon that
stopped being true.

The bracket attempts earned their page in this book for one more reason:
they are the roadmap of every wrong turn a builder can make at a dome's
corner. Somebody, somewhere, is designing a hub right now. The book's
answer is the drawing above, and the sentence under it: make the sticks
match, and the joint stops being the hard part.
