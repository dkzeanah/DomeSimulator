# Campaign Illustration Prompts — The 40 Hour Cabin, the Internet Launch

A second series, written for the full digital campaign: the Kickstarter
page, the site, the social cuts — and the book pages that still have no
picture. Every prompt is hyper-verbose on purpose: image models do better
work when the emotion, the composition and the *meaning* of the picture
are all specified, and a campaign image that renders the wrong feeling
costs more than one that renders a wrong detail.

Each prompt names the exact page it belongs to, from the current interior
(`deliverables/book/2-trees-v45.pdf`, 284 pages, whose contents list is
page-numbered). Campaign assets that are not book pages name their
Kickstarter section instead.

**The one style line every prompt shares** (repeat it at the head of each
prompt so the whole set reads as one campaign):

> Warm painterly book illustration, soft visible brushwork, realistic
> proportions, emotionally direct, no text, no letters, no logos, no
> watermark.

**The geometry paragraph every dome prompt shares** — the thing that
separates an image that *is* this project from a stock photo of "a dome":

> The dome is a two-frequency geodesic hemisphere about 22 feet across
> and 11 feet tall, made of 40 flat triangles; five triangles meet at the
> very top. Every triangle is its own framed panel of three split-log
> members — long pie-slice-shaped sticks, rough pine bark on the outside
> face, two pale flat sawn faces. No metal hubs, no plates, no star
> connectors; at every corner the members overlap like a pinwheel and the
> corner point is empty. Neighbouring panels sit side by side with a thin
> seam between them.

**What every prompt must never show:** metal star hubs at the corners;
milled rectangular lumber; a dome made of hexagons; a top that is not five
triangles; any text or lettering; children alone in wilderness; faces
that look like specific real people; pity.

**Three standing rules:**
1. Caption every image as an illustration, never a photograph.
2. KDP will ask whether the book contains AI-generated images: answer yes.
3. Upscale before use — 1,000–1,500 px output becomes 2,550 px across for
   an 8.5-inch page at 300 DPI.

### 1. Campaign hero — "The Head and Its Hats" — Kickstarter hero / book p11 (Part 1 opener)

```text
Warm painterly book illustration, soft visible brushwork, realistic proportions, emotionally direct, no text, no letters, no logos, no watermark. Portrait 2:3.

The campaign's one metaphor, drawn literally: a dome is a head, and it wears hats. A finished two-frequency geodesic dome cabin stands in a clearing at golden hour, and the dome is dressed the way a person would be dressed against a winter coming: the bare frame of split-log triangles shows at one edge like a skull glimpsed through a knit hat, then a thick quilted layer stitched from many recycled fabrics — faded flannel, old denim, a child's outgrown shirt — pulled over the crown like a wool cap, and over that a second, larger quilted layer, and over everything one pale, slightly glossy rain cap strapped down at the base like a shower cap, the single watertight layer. The dome is about 22 feet across and 11 feet tall, 40 triangles, five meeting at the top, no metal hubs anywhere, corners overlapping like a pinwheel. In the foreground, in a pool of low sun, sits the hat that is not yet on: a fresh quilted layer folded on a bench, a thimble and thread beside it, waiting its winter. The mood is tenderness and preparation — a mother dressing a child for school, the same gesture, at the scale of a house. The light is the light of a late afternoon that knows the frost is coming. Let the picture say: warmth here is layered, earned, and made by hand.
```

### 2. "The Tree That Was Already Down" — book Ch 1, page 12

```text
Warm painterly book illustration, early-morning light through pines, soft visible brushwork, emotionally direct, no text, no letters, no logos, no watermark. Landscape 3:2.

A single large pine lies where the storm dropped it, and the picture is a eulogy and a question asked of the viewer at once. The root plate stands torn on edge with soil still clutching its roots, like a wall of earth with a thousand fingers; the trunk runs straight for about 48 feet, a fallen king of ordinary size, about 12 inches across at the butt; the crown lies buried in brush. On the trunk rests a small orange consumer chainsaw with a 16-inch bar, a measuring tape and a lumber crayon — the instruments of a resurrection, laid on a coffin that is not one. Mist rises off the ground; the light is the grey-blue of the hour after a storm when everything is still wet and listening. The mood is grief that has not yet been told it is hope: wood that nobody wanted, waiting for somebody to see that the storm has already done the hardest cut. No people. No triumph. Just the question: what is a fallen tree if not lumber already on the ground?
```

```text
Warm painterly close-up book illustration, soft visible brushwork, emotionally direct, no text, no letters, no logos, no watermark. Square 1:1.

The end grain of a six-foot pine log section, about 10 inches across, split through its centre into eight identical pie-slice wedges — a cake of wood cut into eighths, pushed slightly apart so the eight radial cuts read like the spokes of a sun. ### 4. "The Pinwheel — Nothing Meets at the Corner" — book Ch 15, page 79
```

```text
Warm painterly book illustration, workshop light raking across wood grain, soft visible brushwork, emotionally direct, no text, no letters, no logos, no watermark. Landscape 3:2.

A close, almost architectural view of one corner of a triangular panel made of three split-log members, and the joint is the whole sermon. Each member's squared butt end lands flat on the *side* of its neighbour, and the three chase each other around the corner like a pinwheel caught mid-turn — the first stick's end resting on the second's side, the second's on the third's, the third's on the first's. The very corner point is empty: a triangle of daylight where a hub would have been. Bark faces out, pale sawn faces in, two threaded bolts through each overlap. The composition is a slow clockwise spiral, so the eye does the thing the joint does: it goes around. The mood is the quiet satisfaction of an idea that deletes a problem instead of solving it — no machined connector, no mitre meeting a mitre, no part that a hardware store has to make. Let the empty corner feel like the most load-bearing thing in the frame: the absence that holds everything up.
```
