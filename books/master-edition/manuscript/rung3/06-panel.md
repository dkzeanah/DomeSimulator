**What you are deciding:** how the forty panels get made -- the bench, the jig, the
order, the marking, and how long it takes.

## The reference answer

One flat bench, set up twice: once for the {{dome.aaa}} equal-sided panels and
once for the {{dome.bab}} short-sided ones. The jig holds each member by its two
flat sawn faces against fixed stops, guides the saw for the butt cut and the
flush cut, and takes each panel through twelve stages from bare bench to finished
triangle. The picture is the jig the simulator models.

The forty-hour clock gives this part of the work two estimated lines:
{{clock.cut_h}} hours for cutting both ends and drilling, and {{clock.panels_h}}
hours for bolting members into panels on the jig. Both are **estimates** -- reasoned
from the owner's process, not yet timed on a full run -- and the clock's error
allowance exists partly because of them.

Plan for the learning curve. The first panel off a new jig is a rehearsal, the
second is nearly right, and the fourth is usually the first good one. The
practice panels are not waste: they become test pieces, and the spare struts in
the pile pay for them.

:::box why "Why"
Forty panels that agree with each other close into a dome; forty panels that are
each nearly right do not, because their small differences add up round the
rings. A jig makes agreement automatic: the position is measured once, when the
stops are set, and never again.
:::

:::box watch "Watch out"
The bench must be flat -- a twist in the bench is a twist in every panel. Locate
every member from its sawn faces, never its bark. Check the first panel against
its drawing before you build the second. Pick one pinwheel direction and use it
for every panel.
:::
