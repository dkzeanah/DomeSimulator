**What you are deciding:** where to spend your care, your money and your
checking -- because they are not equally needed everywhere.

## The reference answer

The project's whole argument fits in one sentence from Volume A: *the frame is
the part you are allowed to get wrong; the skin and the ground are not.*

The frame forgives because it is made of triangles. Loads run along the members,
so a bowed or knotty stick still does its job, and the method gives every small
error somewhere harmless to go:

* a stick a little long or short -- the head is cut long and sawn off flush once
  the panel is together, so the panel decides the length;
* a butt end cut a degree off -- the screws pull it onto its neighbour's flat
  face;
* a seam a little wide or narrow -- the key and gasket take it up, shared across
  {{dome.seams}} seams.

Two places do not forgive.

**The skin**, because water that gets in stays in and rots the frame, however
good the frame is. That is chapter eight.

**The ground ring**, because its error does not get absorbed -- it grows. A
strut error of {{geo.ring_err}} inches in the base ring moves the ring's
diameter by {{geo.ring_d_err}} inches: the ring multiplies it
by {{geo.ring_gain}}. That is chapter nine.

:::box why "Why"
A triangulated shell resolves its loads axially, so asymmetry in it is absorbed
rather than amplified -- the lumpy frame in the picture stood. What it cannot do
is keep water out or correct the circle it stands on. Spending precision where
it pays and tolerance where the tree varies is the method's whole economy.
:::

:::box watch "Watch out"
"May be rough" does not mean "may be rotten". Volume B grades every wedge in four
passes before it reaches the jig: the bend, the knot in the middle third, the knot
at the bearing end, and a crack down the pith. A wedge that fails goes to
blocking, not to the frame.
:::
