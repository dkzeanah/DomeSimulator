## The reference answer

Both volumes open the same way: *this book is not engineering.* It prices loads
and does not rate them. Snow, wind, the ground under the ring, the rigging that
lifts a dome and the trees it hangs from are a licensed engineer's numbers; the
permit, the setbacks and whether a building may be slept in are the building
department's; electrical and plumbing connections are licensed trades'. The plan
reaches all three before the first tree is cut.

At the log: a helmet with a mesh visor, ear protection, gloves, boots and
chainsaw chaps on every cut, and a second person present for every session.
Felling uses a planned notch, a hinge left whole, a cleared escape route, and
everyone else at least two tree-lengths away -- the picture shows why the hinge is
the whole art. On the ladder and at the lift: three points of contact, a spotter,
and nobody, ever, under a hanging load. Any lift heavier than
{{en.two_person_kg}} kilograms is a two-person lift.

:::box why "Why"
The method's honesty about what it does not know is what makes it safe to try:
every place a figure becomes a load, the book stops and names who decides. A plan
that has those names on it is a plan you can stand behind.
:::

:::box watch "Watch out"
Fatigue causes more accidents than ignorance; the clock's allowance for getting
it wrong is also a rest allowance -- use it. Stop the work in wind, in rain on
ladders, and whenever anybody calls the stop word.
:::
