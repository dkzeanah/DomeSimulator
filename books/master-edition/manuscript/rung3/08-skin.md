**What you are deciding:** the first coat -- what keeps the water out now -- and how

## The reference answer

**One watertight layer, and only one.** The standard article's skin is a
rain-slick cap over the whole shell -- the "shower cap" -- priced at
{{money.cap}} dollars. Everything else goes under it, where it stays dry.

**Warmth one winter at a time.** Under the cap go quilted layers sewn from
recycled clothing: about {{tt_quilt.shirts_per_layer}} t-shirts a layer, each
adding R-{{tt_quilt.r_per_layer}}, at {{tt_quilt.usd_per_layer}} dollars a layer
through the quilt network the project proposes. Three layers make
R-{{tt_quilt.stacked_r}}. The picture shows how the heating bill falls layer by
layer -- the first layer pays back fastest, and each one after buys a little less.

**A harder shell later, if ever.** The same dome with a laminated hull instead of
the cap lists at {{money.hard_price}} dollars. The cap is the standard; the hull
is the upgrade, and nothing about the frame changes between them.

**The wood's own finish.** Volume A chars and oils the outside bark faces and
keeps oil off the faces a seal or gasket must stick to. Its estimate for the
frame is {{lin.allow_gal}} gallons of linseed oil and {{lin.labour_h}} hours of
hand work.

:::box why "Why"
Water is the silent killer of wooden buildings, and a shell with {{dome.seams}}
seams has {{dome.seams}} places to lose that fight. One continuous watertight
layer outside everything else means there is only one surface to keep perfect.
Inside, warm damp air condenses on anything colder than its dew point, so the
shell is planned to breathe and to drain at the rim rather than to be sealed.
:::

:::box watch "Watch out"
Never seal the inside face airtight against the frame; give the water a path.
Keep oil off any surface a tape, sealant or gasket must stick to. And oily rags
can catch fire by themselves -- they go straight into a lidded metal tin of
water, outside, the same day.
:::
