## The reference answer

This is the one part of the build that is **measured**. The owner timed real
sessions at the log: {{hr.split_session}} wedges in a {{hr.session}}-hour

The saw drank {{rip.tanks_hour}} tanks of fuel an hour while ripping (also

to {{rip.gal_hi}} US gallons. The calendar the paperback teaches gives the saw

{{rip.days_bucking}} of bucking the trunks to length and {{rip.days_ripping}} of

Why split instead of saw into boards? The picture shows the same log converted
both ways. Packed with rectangles, a round log loses its curved sides; split
into eighths, it loses only the thin lines of the saw's cut. Volume B's tree
keeps {{tt_tree.recovery_pct}} per cent of its wood split, against about
{{tt_tree.sawn_recovery_pct}} per cent sawn into two-by-fours.

## How the cutting goes

Each section is cut lengthwise three times over: once through the centre to
halve it, then each half, then each quarter -- seven straight cuts, all through
the pith. The first cut is the only one where the log can roll, so the log sits
in a cradle; after that, every piece has a flat face to sit on. The wedges go
straight onto spacer sticks, bark up, to dry.

:::box why "Why"
Halving needs no measured angle, so the saw does the geometry. A chain filed for
ripping cuts along the grain far faster than a crosscut chain, and a sharp chain
keeps the cut narrow -- a dull one widens it and eats the recovery. Splitting is
the largest single block of the frame's hours, which is why it is the one the
owner measured first.
:::

:::box watch "Watch out"
Never alone at the log. Keep bar oil full: ripping buries the bar for minutes at
a time. File the chain at every fuel stop whether it asks or not. Aim every cut
through the pith -- a wedge whose point misses the centre has a soft, split-prone
edge exactly where it bears. And the split is a saw cut, always: this method does
not use axes, mauls or splitting wedges.
:::
