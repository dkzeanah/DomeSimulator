**What you are deciding:** what the dome stands on -- or hangs from -- and how the

## The reference answer

Volume A prices the grounds a dome can stand on. For the standard article: a
compacted gravel base, {{money.gravel}} dollars; a framed deck on piers,
{{money.deck}}; a full concrete slab, {{money.slab}}. Across the Dome Creator's
foundation menu the cabin's whole price swings by {{sty.by_foundation_span}}
dollars, from {{sty.by_foundation_lo_usd}} on {{sty.by_foundation_lo}} to
{{sty.by_foundation_hi_usd}} on a {{sty.by_foundation_hi}}. The picture lines
the options up.

The cheapest ground is ground somebody else already built. The project proposes
**pads**: serviced platforms a host builds once and rents, so the dome on top can
leave. On a pad, the platform -- {{money.pad}} dollars of it for the standard
article -- is the host's bill, not the dome owner's.

Two upgrades at the edge are worth planning from the start. A short straight
**pony wall** under the rim lifts the dome and turns the low ring into standing
room: on Volume B's {{tt_pony.floor}}-square-foot reference floor, a three-foot
wall costs {{tt_pony.cost_3}} dollars and buys back {{tt_pony.gain_3}} square feet,
taking the usable share from {{tt_pony.pct_0}} to {{tt_pony.pct_3}} per cent. And
a **brim** at the rim throws rain clear of the base and can catch it in a tank.

:::box why "Why"
A dome puts its weight on a circle, not a floor, so the footing belongs under the
ring -- concrete across the middle carries no frame at all. The ring is the one
place error accumulates instead of being absorbed. And wind over a dome lifts it
like a wing, so the ring has to be tied down, not just set down.
:::

:::box watch "Watch out"
Never skip the hold-downs. Footing depth, frost and soil bearing are site
questions for somebody who can read your ground. And if you hang the dome
instead, the trees and rigging are your foundation -- see chapter seven, and
treat them with the same seriousness.
:::
