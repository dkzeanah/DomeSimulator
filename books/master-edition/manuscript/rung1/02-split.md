First, the builder cut the long trunk into shorter pieces, each one about as
long as a grown-up is tall. Each piece was a fat round log, like a giant spool
of thread.

Then came the clever part.

Have you ever cut a birthday cake? You cut it right down the middle, and you
have two halves. You cut each half down the middle, and you have four. You cut
each of those down the middle, and you have **eight** pieces.

And here is the magic: all eight pieces are the same shape. You did not have
to measure anything. You did not have to be careful about the angle. The cake
was round, so cutting it in half, and half again, and half again, gives eight
pieces that match -- because the cake did the measuring for you.

The builder did exactly that to each round log, with a chainsaw, carefully, in
long straight cuts down its length.

Half. Half again. Half again.

Eight sticks came out of every log. Each one was shaped like a long slice of
cake: pointy at the middle of the log, and round on the back where the bark
is. A stick shaped like that is called a **wedge**.

Most builders would never do this. They would cut the round log into square
boards. But try to cut a square out of a circle, and look at what is left: four
rounded corners of perfectly good wood, thrown away.

The cake cut throws almost nothing away. Every slice is used.

:::box do "Try it together"
You need two things that are round -- two oranges, two round slices of bread,
or a round cake (the best one, because you get to eat it after).

1. Cut the first one in half, then each half in half, then each quarter in
   half. Count the pieces. Are they the same?
2. Now try to cut the biggest *square* you can out of the second one. Put the
   leftover pieces in a pile.
3. Which way wasted less?
:::

:::box why "For the grown-up"
The trunk is cut into sections, and each section is ripped lengthwise with a
chainsaw through its centre: once to halve it, twice to quarter it, four times
to make {{cut.splits}} sectors of {{cut.sector}} degrees each. No angle is ever
measured; the round log supplies the symmetry. Split this way, a trunk keeps
about {{pine.wedge_pct}} per cent of its wood as usable members; sawn into
two-by-fours, the same log keeps about {{pine.sawn_model_pct}} per cent. The cuts
are made with the saw -- never with a maul or a splitting wedge -- and only by
an adult in full safety gear.
:::

:::box wonder "Wonder question"
Why do all eight pieces come out the same, even though nobody measured them?
:::
