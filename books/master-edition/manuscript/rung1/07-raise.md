Forty triangles were stacked up and ready. Now it was time to make them into a
house.

You cannot start at the top. The top has nothing to stand on. So the builder
started at the bottom.

First the bottom ring: the triangles that touch the ground, all the way round
in a circle. Then the next ring of triangles, leaning in, resting on the first.
Then the next. Every ring gets a little smaller, leaning in a little more,
like the rings of a beehive.

While the rings are leaning, they want to fall inward. So the builder held
them up with long sticks pushed against them from inside, called **props**.
A triangle goes up -- a prop goes under it. Another goes up -- another prop.

And then, at the very top, the last few triangles close the hole in the roof.

Here is the wonderful part. The moment the top is closed, every triangle is
holding up every other triangle. Push on one, and it pushes on its neighbours,
and they push on theirs, all the way round and all the way down. The dome holds
itself. One by one, the props come out, and nothing falls.

The builder of this book did something even more daring. Instead of building
on the ground, the builder strung strong ropes between big living trees, like a
giant spider web in the air, and lifted each triangle up into the web with a
winch -- a machine that winds a rope around a drum. The triangles were joined
together up in the air, and the whole dome hung between the trees, like a
lantern.

:::box do "Try it together"
Stand up some books in a ring on the floor, leaning a little inward. Do they
fall? Now lean a second ring on top of the first. What did you need to keep
them from falling? Those are your props.
:::

:::box why "For the grown-up"
A dome goes up course by course from the base ring, propped from inside until
the crown closes; once it closes the shell carries itself and the props come
out. The author's own method builds in the air instead: a web of wire rope
between living trees, a {{web.winch_lb}}-pound 12-volt winch, and each triangle
lifted flat in a harness and joined in place. Raising anything heavy is
grown-up work, and nobody -- ever -- stands under a load that is hanging.
:::

:::box wonder "Wonder question"
Why does a dome hold itself up once the very top is closed, but not before?
:::
