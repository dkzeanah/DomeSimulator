Now the builder had a pile of wedges in two lengths, and a plan for a ball of
triangles. But how do you hold three sticks together at a corner?

Many dome builders use a metal star at every corner. All the sticks run into
the star, and bolts hold them there. It works -- but only if every stick is
exactly the same, exactly straight and exactly the right length. Remember the
wedges? They are *not* exactly anything. A metal star would not fit them.

So the builder did something surprising. At the corners of these triangles,
**the sticks do not meet at all.**

Here is how. Hold three pencils in a triangle. Instead of making their ends
touch in the corners, slide each pencil along a little, so that the end of one
pencil rests on the *side* of the next one. The end of the first rests on the
side of the second. The end of the second rests on the side of the third. The
end of the third rests on the side of the first.

They go round and round, like children holding hands in a circle where each
one holds the next one's elbow. Or like the blades of a pinwheel. And right in
the corner, where you would expect the sticks to meet, there is... nothing.
Just air.

Because each stick only has to rest on a flat side, it does not need to be
perfect. A side is a big place to land.

And every triangle has its *own* three sticks. Where two triangles sit next to
each other, two sticks lie side by side, with a thin strip of wood called a
**key** pressed between them, like the filling in a sandwich.

:::box do "Try it together"
Take three craft sticks, pencils or straws. Lay them on the table in a
triangle so that the end of each one rests on the side of the next, going round
in the same direction. Now try going round the other way. Do you get the same
triangle, or its mirror image?
:::

:::box why "For the grown-up"
This is the pinwheel joint, and the frame is hubless: each member's butt end
lands on the flat sawn side of its neighbour, and no two ends ever meet. Every
panel owns all three of its members, which is why a dome of {{dome.panels}}
panels has {{dome.members}} members rather than one per edge, and why two
members and a key sit in each of the {{dome.seams}} seams. The butt end carries
the frame's one compound cut, made against a fixed setting; the other end is
left long and sawn off flush once the panel is together.
:::
