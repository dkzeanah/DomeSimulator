Imagine trying to build a ball of triangles up in the air, on a wobbly ladder,
holding three long sticks at once and a drill in your teeth.

The builder did not want to do that. So the triangles were built somewhere
much nicer: flat on a table, on the ground.

The table was special. Before any wood went on it, the builder screwed little
blocks onto it in exactly the right places -- blocks to hold the first stick,
blocks to hold the second, blocks to hold the third, and a straight edge to
guide the saw. A table like that is called a **jig**.

With a jig, nobody needs to measure anything twice. You put the first stick
against its blocks. You put the second stick against its blocks. You put the
third stick against its blocks. You fasten them together. You saw the long
ends off along the guide. And off the table comes a triangle -- exactly like
the last one, and exactly like the next one.

Then you do it again. And again. Forty times.

Making the same thing over and over sounds boring. But it is the secret of how
one person can build a house. The first triangle takes a while, because you are
learning. By the fourth one, your hands know what to do. By the fortieth, you
could almost do it with your eyes closed (please don't).

And because each triangle is finished on the ground, it can be checked, and
fixed, and even painted, before it ever goes up.

:::box do "Try it together"
Draw one triangle on a piece of cardboard. Push a pin into the cardboard at
each corner, just outside the lines. Now lay a sheet of paper on the
cardboard, and trace a triangle using the pins as your guide -- then another
sheet, and another.

Lay all your paper triangles on top of each other. Are they the same? Now try
drawing three triangles *without* the pins and compare.
:::

:::box why "For the grown-up"
The jig is one flat board that holds every member against fixed stops, so the
position of each stick -- not a tape measure -- decides the panel's shape.
The dome needs {{dome.aaa}} panels of one shape and {{dome.bab}} of the other,
so the jig is set up twice. Repeatability beats accuracy when you need forty
of something: a jig that is slightly off builds forty panels that still agree
with each other, and agreeing is what makes the dome close.
:::

What is something you have done so many times that your hands know how to do
it without you thinking?
