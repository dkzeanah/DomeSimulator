One night there was a big storm.

The wind pushed and pushed on a tall pine tree at the edge of the woods. The
tree bent, and swayed, and creaked. And in the morning, when the sun came up,
the tree was lying down on the ground.

Its roots were sticking up in the air, still holding on to the dirt. Its top
was lost in the bushes. And in between was the trunk: long, and straight, and
round, like the biggest pencil you have ever seen.

Most people who walk past a fallen tree see a problem. *It's in the way. It
will rot. Somebody should cut it up for firewood.*

But the builder in this story looked at the tree and asked a different
question.

*What if this tree could become a house?*

That sounds silly at first. Houses are made of boards from the store, aren't
they? Flat, square boards, all the same.

But here is a secret about boards. Every board in the store started out as a
round tree, just like this one. Somebody cut the tree down. Somebody dragged it
out of the woods. A truck carried it to a mill. Big saws cut it into squares,
and a lot of the round tree was cut away and thrown on a pile. Then the boards
were dried, and smoothed, and sorted, and loaded on another truck, and carried
to a store, and stacked on a shelf, and sold.

That is a lot of hands, and a lot of trucks, and a lot of waiting, before a
single board reaches the house.

And this tree was already here. Right where the house could be.

So the builder made a plan: no mill, no truck, no store. Just the tree, a
small saw, a lot of careful thinking -- and a lot of help from the shape of
the tree itself.

The rest of this book is that plan.

:::box do "Try it together"
Go for a walk and look for a fallen tree or a big fallen branch. When you
find one:

1. Find where it is straight, and where it starts to bend or branch.
2. Wrap a piece of string around the fattest straight part, and mark the
   string with your finger. That is how fat the tree is *all the way round*.
3. Walk along it and count your steps from the fat end to where the straight
   part stops.

Take the string home. You will use it again on rung two.
:::

:::box why "For the grown-up"
The method in this book builds a cabin frame from trees split on the spot with
one chainsaw. The reference dome is {{dome.diameter_ft}} feet across with

members. What matters about a tree at this stage is only three things: how
fat it is at the bottom, how fat at the top of the straight part, and how long
that straight part is. Everything else in the plan is worked out from those.
:::
