The frame is the part you are allowed to get wrong. The skin is not. A dome has
{{dome.seams}} seams, and every one is a place water could get in, and wood
that stays wet rots. So the skin is planned more carefully than anything else,
around two ideas you can test on the table.

**Shed, don't seal.** A seal tries to stop water by being perfect, and nothing
outdoors stays perfect: wood shrinks and swells, tape lifts, and the first gap
lets water in. A roof that *sheds* does not try to stop water at all. It hands
the water downhill, each layer overlapping the one below it, so the water is
always on top and always moving away.

**Water from the inside.** Warm air holds more water than cold air. When warm,
damp air from inside a house touches something cold, it drops some of its water
on it -- the way a cold glass gets wet on a summer day. That is *condensation*,
and it is why the inside of a dome has to be planned too, not just the outside.

The picture shows the dome dressed for winter: soft layers inside, one
watertight cap over them all.

:::box do "Make it"
**You need:** card or paper, wax paper, tape, a spray bottle of water, a tray, a
glass jar, ice and water.

1. **Seal or shed.** Make two little roofs from four strips of card each. On the
   first, butt the strips edge to edge and tape the joins. On the second, lay
   them overlapping like shingles, each strip over the one below. Tilt both on
   the tray and spray from above. Look underneath.
2. **One raincoat.** Cover your paper dome with a single piece of wax paper,
   pleated round it like a shower cap. Spray it. Is the paper under it dry?
3. **Water from nowhere.** Fill the jar with ice and water and leave it on the
   counter for ten minutes. Where did the water on the outside come from?
:::

:::box why "Why it works"
The cabin's design has exactly one watertight layer, the cap, with everything
else under it where it stays dry. Under the cap go quilted layers sewn from
recycled clothing, each adding R-{{tt_quilt.r_per_layer}} of warmth, added one
winter at a time. The water on your jar came out of the air: the jar was colder
than the air's *dew point*, the temperature at which it has to let water go.
Volume A spends a whole part on keeping the dome's seams above the dew point, or
deliberately catching the water where it is welcome.
:::

:::box done "You've got it when"
You can say which roof kept the tray dry and why, and where the water on the jar
came from.
:::
