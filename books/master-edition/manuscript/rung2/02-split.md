On rung one, a cake showed why cutting a round log in half three times gives
eight matching pieces. This chapter checks it two ways -- by folding, which
needs no knife at all, and by trying the alternative, which is to cut squares
out of a circle and see what is lost.

The real cut is made with a chainsaw, by an adult, along the whole length of a
six-foot log: once through the middle to make two halves, then each half
through the middle to make quarters, then each quarter to make eighths. The
picture shows the order. It is the order that matters, because after the first
cut every piece has a flat side to sit on, and a piece that sits still is a
piece a saw can cut safely.

Every cut runs through the very centre of the log -- the *pith*, the small dark
dot where the tree started growing. Because every cut goes through the centre,
every piece ends up with a point at the centre and a curved back of bark. The
log is round, so every piece is the same.

:::box do "Make it"
**You need:** three paper circles (trace round a plate), scissors, a ruler, and
a protractor if you have one.

1. **Fold it.** Fold the first circle exactly in half. Fold it in half again.
   And again. Unfold it. Count the triangle-shaped pieces between the creases.
   Use a protractor to measure the point of one piece.
2. **Cut it.** Cut the second circle along its creases into eight wedges.
   Stack them. Do they match?
3. **Try squares.** On the third circle, draw the biggest square you can fit
   inside it, with its corners touching the edge. Cut the square out. Now put
   the four leftover curved pieces next to your eight wedges from step two.
   Which pile of *useful* pieces is bigger?
:::

:::box why "Why it works"
Halving a circle three times divides it into {{cut.splits}} equal sectors of
{{cut.sector}} degrees, and nobody has to measure an angle: folding (or
sawing) through the middle is the measurement. A square inside a circle
wastes its four curved sides -- and a mill cutting rectangular boards out of a
round log wastes those sides too, plus the sawdust of many more cuts. Split
into wedges, a trunk keeps about {{pine.wedge_pct}} per cent of its wood;
sawn into two-by-fours, about {{pine.sawn_model_pct}} per cent. The real log is
cut lengthwise with a chainsaw, by an adult wearing full safety gear, with a
second person present -- never with an axe, a maul or a splitting wedge.
:::

:::box done "You've got it when"
You can explain, without a picture, why three halvings give eight equal pieces
-- and why a square wastes wood that a wedge keeps.
:::
