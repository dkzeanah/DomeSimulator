A builder planning a house from a tree needs to know only three things about
the tree. Not its age, not its species, not what a timber buyer would pay for
it. Just these:

1. **How fat it is at the bottom** of the straight part (the *butt*).
2. **How fat it is at the top** of the straight part, where the first big
   branch starts.
3. **How long the straight part is**, from the butt to the first big branch or
   bend.

The fat bottom matters because a fatter log gives fatter sticks. The thin top
matters because the sticks from the top of the tree will be the thinnest ones
in the pile. And the length matters because the trunk is cut into pieces of
one length -- about as long as one person can carry -- and the number of pieces
decides how many sticks the tree can give.

The picture shows a worked example, the tree measured in Volume B: fat at the
bottom, thinner at the top, and long enough to cut into eight pieces of the
same length.

How do you measure how fat a tree is? You cannot put a ruler across the middle
of a trunk. But you can put a string *around* it, and a little bit of maths
does the rest.

:::box do "Make it"
**You need:** a long piece of string, a tape measure, a marker or chalk, a
notebook, and a calculator.

1. Find a fallen tree or log (or a standing tree, for practice). Find where
   the straight part starts and stops. Chalk a line at each end.
2. Wrap the string around the trunk at the bottom chalk line, pull it snug and
   pinch it where it meets itself. Measure the string to your pinch. Write it
   down: that is the distance *around*.
3. Do the same at the top chalk line.
4. Divide each distance around by **3.14**. The answers are the two
   *diameters* -- how fat the trunk is straight through.
5. Measure the straight part from chalk line to chalk line.
6. Now count: how many six-foot pieces fit in the straight part? (Throw away
   the leftover -- a short piece is firewood.) Each piece will give eight
   sticks. How many sticks does your tree give?
:::

:::box why "Why it works"
The distance around a circle is always about 3.14 times the distance across
it -- that number is called *pi*, and it is the same for every circle in the
world. So a string tells you the diameter without cutting anything. The
reference trunk in Volume A is {{cut.trunk}} inches across; a string around
it would measure {{mb.trunk_around_in}} inches. The cabin needs
{{dome.members}} sticks, and every six-foot section gives eight, so a planner
works out the number of trees from these three measurements alone -- that
sum is the first worksheet on rung three.
:::

:::box done "You've got it when"
You have written down the bottom diameter, the top diameter and the straight
length of one real trunk, and you can say how many sticks it would give.
:::
