## 01. Built in the air

Time: 00:00:00.000 - 00:00:33.100

Forty triangles, hung from a web.

Most domes are built from the ground up, a ring at a time, with scaffolding inside. This one is built in the air. A web of cable strung between four trees, and every triangle of the dome hauled up, swung into its place, and left hanging there on three cords, not yet joined to the next. The gasket, the skin and the foundation can wait. Here is how, with every load worked out, and the numbers that argue back.

## 03. Four trees, a square

## 04. Two ropes make an X

Time: 00:01:38.420 - 00:02:13.120

And how hard they pull.

Two wire ropes, crossing in the middle. Hang the whole frame from the crossing, the worst case, and each rope carries half of 8.5 kilonewtons. A rope that sags 5 percent of its span, 0.64 metres here, pulls on its trees with 21.3 kilonewtons, five times the weight it carries. That flat a rope needs to be half-inch wire rope, worked at a fifth of its strength. Let it sag twice as far and the pull halves, to 10.9: three-eighths rope will do.

## 05. What the trees take

Time: 00:02:13.120 - 00:02:51.600

The guys carry more than the web.

And the trees take all of it, eight metres up, as a lever. A 45 centimetre pine, green, bends at about 35 megapascals. Pulled with 21 kilonewtons at the tie, its trunk is worked to a margin of 1.8, and a living tree, which can hide rot, should keep three. So each tree gets a back-guy, to an anchor 8 metres behind it, and that guy pulls 30 kilonewtons: more than the web itself. Or sag the web ten percent, and the margin is 3.7: no guys.

On-screen math:

- M = 21.3 kN x 8 m = 170 kN m

- margin 35 / 19.1 = 1.84 (want 3)
- guy at 45 deg: 30.1 kN, 5/8 in
- 10% sag: margin 3.67

## 15. A taut web is a stiff web

Time: 00:07:27.284 - 00:08:06.796

Trees move. Give the rope a spring.

Now the honest part. Trees move in the wind, and they move apart. A rope sagging 5 percent goes straight after its trees lean apart only 8.5 centimetres. After that the rope stretches, and every further centimetre adds about 4 kilonewtons. A storm sways a tree more than that. So the X gets a spring at every end, a heavy tensioner that lets it give, or a slacker sag: at ten percent the trees can spread 34 centimetres before it bites. Hung triangles swing too, once every 2 to 4 seconds.

On-screen math:

- slack = 8 s^2 / 3L = 8.5 cm
- stretch: EA/L = 3.9 kN per cm
- 10% sag: 34 cm
- swing 2 pi sqrt(L/g): 1.8 to 4.0 s

## 19. Leave it hanging?

Time: 00:09:41.644 - 00:10:21.540

The frame, yes. The house, not on these ropes.

And maybe it never comes down. The frame alone, 869 kilograms, is what these ropes were sized for. But a house is not a frame. With its skin, its floor and everything in it, this dome is about 4.6 tonnes, 5.3 times as heavy, and hung from the same X it would pull 113 kilonewtons: more than any rope in the table works to. Living in the trees is a different structure, and it wants an engineer, an arborist, and an inspection every season. This is priced, not rated.

On-screen math:

## 20. Never under the load

Time: 00:10:21.540 - 00:10:54.640

Twenty-three kilograms at forty kilometres an hour.

And the rule that matters most. Nobody stands under a triangle on its way up. Dropped from the top of the hoist, 6.8 metres, it lands at 12 metres a second, 41 kilometres an hour, with 1.5 kilojoules. Mark a ring under the hook and keep it empty. Wear a helmet. Climb on a proper climbing system, tied in twice, or hire an arborist for the ties. The web is only as safe as the worst knot in it.

On-screen math:

- v = sqrt(2 g h) = 11.5 m/s = 41 km/h
- E = m v^2 / 2 = 1.53 kJ
