# DomeSim and The 40 Hour Cabin: the project so far

*Written 14 October 2026. Every figure on this page is counted or computed by the project's own code that day.*

**What it is.** One person's open-source project to make a small, owned home cheap enough that the
house stops eating the household. It has two halves that keep each other honest: a *method* for
framing round buildings from logs split lengthwise with one chainsaw, and the *software* that
solves, prices, films and writes about that method, so that no number anywhere is typed by hand.
It is built by Donovan Zeanah, a U.S. Navy veteran, in the open at github.com/dkzeanah/DomeSimulator.

**The method.** A two-frequency geodesic dome, 19.42 ft across with 277 sq ft of floor, framed
from 120 wedges split from logs and assembled as 40 flat panels on one jig, joined in
pinwheels so nothing meets at a corner; 37.5 hours to frame by the project's clock, about
67 for the whole building. Splitting keeps 89% of a log against 57% sawn
into two-by-fours. The product built on it is the *stem cell*: one standard body with a utility
core ($1,890), snap-in panels, seams that double as channels for rain, wire and air, and a
silicone-soaked cotton skin, listing at $12,036 ($43.45 a sq ft against $217 for a new
site-built house), in 15 fit-outs. It stands on a serviced pad: own the building, rent the
ground. The same wedges now frame a zome too: 36 panels, every cut one 22.5° bevel,
9 log sections against the dome's 15.

**The software.** 610 Python files, about 337,364 lines. At its centre is the raw-wedge
solver and its 3-D world, which cuts and joins every member; around it, the Dome Creator, the
Triangle Infill calculator, the new Wedge Zome world (with rulers, a six-foot person and a
click-to-measure readout), cost models for the stem cell, the campaign and a park of pads, the
seam-climate controller, the cell wall, the sky-web build, a CNC shop, a book engine whose
manuscripts carry live number tokens, a film pipeline that renders narrated lessons in a shared
3-D "Cabin World" in the owner's own synthesised voice and resumes after a crash, a website
(zeanahlab.com) that sells the book and runs a dome network, and a recorder that logs how the work
gets done.

**What has been written.** *The 40 Hour Cabin: Complete Edition* (83 chapters in 15
parts, sold on the site with the code); *The 40 Hour Cabin: 2V Geodesic Dome Wedge Method*, the Amazon
paperback (55 chapters); the *Master Edition*, a 853-page reader's proof that tells
the whole idea in 6 rungs from a bedtime story to published experiments, across 12
threads, with both books bound in; *'Square' on Demand* (32 pages), on two wedges
paired into a 3.87 in square only where a square is needed; a title page for *Zome Zoomer*;
and *Domology*, a three-in-one book in progress.

**What has been filmed.** 85 films registered and 34 rendered; 211 video
files in deliverables, counting phone cuts and teasers, and 109 PDFs. The newest, *Beyond the
Dome*, takes the method past the dome: square on demand, shims, roof pitches, octagons, joints and
the zome.

**The ask, and who it is for.** A grant one-pager and a sheet of efficiency charts ask for
$100,650, the sum of costed lines: prove it, then make it repeatable. It is built by a veteran for
veterans -- 32,882 were homeless on one night in January 2024 (HUD) -- to sit beside the programs that
already house them.

**What is proved, and what is not.** Measured: the splitting rate (30 wedges per
4-hour session), strut weights (15-22 kg) and a full-size prototype frame.
Modelled: cost, energy, rain, recovery. Not yet: an engineer's rating, an instrumented year of
weather, the moulded shell and its tooling. And the numbers that argue back stay on the page --
paired squares keep only 53% of a log, less than the mill.
