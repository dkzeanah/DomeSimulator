# The 40 Hour Cabin — Investor Overview

*The elevator pitch, the why, the how, and the questions an investor
actually asks. Every figure here is the project's own computed number, not
a pitch-room invention.*

## The elevator pitch

**Ten seconds.**

Housing is expensive because wood has to become a rectangle before it can
become a house. We cut out the rectangle — three trees, one chainsaw off a
shelf, forty hours — and get a 365-square-foot geodesic cabin that a
builder makes with no mill, no crew and no loan.

**Thirty seconds.**

One person, one $199 saw, three trees, and a fortnight: that is the entire
factory. The method is called the wedge — split a log into eighths, let
the pie-slice shape be the structural member, lap the corners like a
pinwheel so nothing needs a machined connector. The frame costs $692 to
cut against a store-bought equivalent, and the same solved geometry — 120
members, 40 panels, two lengths — powers a product ladder from a $35
plan set to an $11,400 bring-your-own-trees kit to a $19,800 complete
one, plus a network of quilters who sew the insulation and pad hosts who
provide the land. We are raising $110,000, itemized line by line, to buy
the tooling and stand the first dome through a measured year.

## Why

### The problem, stated without adjectives

A house is a stack of middlemen. Between a standing tree and a board on a
rack sit fourteen operations — fell, limb, buck, haul, set-up, slab,
rotate, cant, resaw, edge, trim, sort, dry, plane — and every one of them
has fuel, wages, rent and margin in it. The wood is the smallest line on
the receipt. The method's arithmetic, published in the book: a log sawn
into two-by-fours keeps about 45 per cent of itself as saleable lumber;
the same log split into wedges keeps 87.4 per cent. The rectangle is the
tax. This project removes the tax by removing the rectangle.

### The people it is for

Three groups, and the campaign is built for all three at once:

* **The person with trees and no money.** Land, standing timber, a small
  saw, more time than cash. On one night in January 2024, the federal
  count was 32,882 veterans with no home of their own. The method was
  written by a Navy veteran, for people who build their way out the way
  veterans have always built their way out — with their hands, in crews,
  one day at a time. And it fits the other end of the same table: the
  single mother with an evening, a sewing machine, and a pile of
  outgrown clothes — because in this system the insulation is a quilt,
  and sewing it is paid work, done at home, after the children are
  asleep.
* **The person with money and no trees.** They buy the kit, the hub, the
  cap — the building as an article, honestly priced, with the 20 per
  cent margin named on the page instead of hidden in the freight.
* **The person with land and neither.** The pad host. A serviced circle
  of ground that lets a dome land, and the unit that turns one building
  into a network.

### The moment

Three things are true at once, which is why this is the moment:

1. **Housing is unaffordable in the way that finally breaks.** The
   median new home is a mortgage that owns the buyer. A dome built this
   way puts shelter at the price of a used car and the labour of a
   fortnight — and the labour is the point: it is *kept* by the person
   who does it, not billed to them.
2. **The waste streams are the supply chains.** Textile waste is the
   insulation. Windfall and unmerchantable timber is the structure.
   The campaign does not ask the world to produce new materials; it
   re-prices the ones the world already throws away.
3. **The software is finished and open.** Every number the book prints
   is computed by a solver that is published, with the book, at
   github.com/dkzeanah/DomeSimulator. This is not a sketch. It is a
   solved geometry, a tested joint, a documented fortnight, and a
   film series.

## How

### The method, in four sentences

Buck the trunk into six-foot sections. Split each section into eight
pie-slice wedges — bark outside, two sawn faces, a point at the pith.
Lay three wedges into a triangle on a jig so each butt end lands on the
*side* of its neighbour, corners lapping like a pinwheel with nothing in
the middle. Raise forty of those triangles, course by course, propped
until the top closes — and, in this project's own build, winch the whole
frame up between two standing trees, where the ground can wait.

### The moat, answered honestly

The geometry is open — that is the point, and the book sells *because*
it is checkable. The moat is the pipeline around the geometry:

1. **The tooling.** A CNC router and plasma table turn "one dome, made
   once" into "the same panel set, made repeatably" — the goal's largest
   lines exist to buy exactly this.
2. **The network.** Quilters and pad hosts are a two-sided community that
   cannot be cloned by reading the repo; it has to be grown, and the
   campaign's $5,000 quilt-seed line pays the first layers.
3. **The trust.** The book prints the number that does not help, names
   its own corrections, and budgets $6,800 for an engineer and $1,850
   for a year of wall sensors *that it promises to publish whichever way
   the data comes out*. In a market of housing promises, that habit is
   the brand.

### The return path

The Kickstarter is the pre-sale that buys the factory. Behind it sits a
small manufacturing business with three revenue rails: kits and
components at a named 20 per cent margin; pads and the pad-host network;
and the published work itself — the Complete Edition with the codebase,
the design sessions, the film series — which funds the project while the
hardware scales. An investor who wants in after the campaign is buying
into a company that already owns its tooling, its prototypes and its
audience — and can show, in the open, exactly what each dollar became.

### The intangible return

Because it should be said plainly: the first funders of this project are
the first names on the test platform, the first line of the backer page
in the next edition of the book, and the first crew credited when the
film series ships. This is a project that keeps its ledgers in the open —
which means it will always be possible to point at the thing your money
built, standing in a clearing, wearing the winter it was promised.

rectangle before it can become a house, and fourteen middlemen get paid
for that rectangle. We removed it. Split a log into eighths and the
pie-slice is already a structural member — bark outside, two sawn faces,
a point at the pith. Lap three of them into a triangle so each end lands
on its neighbour's side, like a pinwheel, and the joint needs no machined
connector at all. Forty triangles, 120 members, one $199 saw, three
trees, 37.5 hours of hands-on work: a 365-square-foot cabin standing.
The frame costs $692 to cut. The complete dome costs $10,030 to build
and prices at $12,036 — $43 a square foot — with the margin named. The
same solved geometry powers the whole ladder: a $35 plan set, a $95
quilter's kit, a $1,450 rain cap, a $2,400 utility hub, an $11,400
bring-your-own-trees kit, a $19,800 complete one. Around the hardware
sits the network: quilters who sew insulation from waste textiles and
get paid for it, pad hosts who provide the land, and owners who provide
the trees. It is built by a Navy veteran, for the 32,882 veterans the
federal count found homeless on a single night, and for the single

house. We are raising $110,000, in twelve named lines — tooling,
sensors, an engineer, the first paid quilters, the shipping — to stand
the first dome through a measured year and turn a solved geometry into
something that can be made twice. The software is open. The arithmetic

return is a factory, a network, and a method for housing that starts
with a storm-felled tree.
