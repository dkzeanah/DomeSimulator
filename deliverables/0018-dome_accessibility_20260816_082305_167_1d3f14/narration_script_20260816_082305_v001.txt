WHEELCHAIR-FIRST DOME LIVING — NARRATION V001
Run ID: dome_accessibility_20260816_082305_167_1d3f14

[S01 — 18 seconds]
The strongest accessible home is not a conventional plan with a ramp added later. It begins with the person, the chair, and the path. A single-level dome can make that path the center of the architecture—if every threshold, turn, and reach is designed honestly.

[S02 — 20 seconds]
One continuous floor removes stairs and lift dependency. A broad central space replaces the corridor maze, so the living room, kitchen, bedroom, and bathroom sit on short, legible routes. Most importantly, the accessible route is the welcoming route everyone uses.

[S03 — 21 seconds]
A good accessible plan is a movement map. Enter without a level change. Turn without reversing through a narrow hall. Reach the kitchen, bedroom, and bathroom along short, obvious paths. The dome earns its value when its open central volume is reserved for that movement.

[S04 — 18 seconds]
A plan can look generous on paper and still demand dozens of avoidable maneuvers every day: a threshold to bump, a door to back away from, a corridor to three-point-turn through, or a switch placed beyond reach. Small frictions compound into dependence.

[S05 — 20 seconds]
The dome's structural shell can leave the center visually open and flexible. Straight radial partitions can hold kitchens, bathrooms, storage, and grab-bar reinforcement while the middle stays generous. The result can be fewer turns, clearer sight lines, and less wasted corridor area.

[S06 — 20 seconds]
A beautiful interior does not matter if the resident cannot enter independently. Begin with a broad, straight approach, a level landing, a flush threshold, an easy-to-operate door, and weather protection. Curving the ramp for style can make slope and cross-slope harder to control.

[S07 — 22 seconds]
The kitchen is where accessible design becomes quality of life. Knee clearance, reachable storage, side-opening appliances, and room to turn keep a wheelchair user participating instead of watching. The dome's open center can give the kitchen generous circulation without isolating it.

[S08 — 22 seconds]
Bathroom space protects more than safety; it protects privacy. A wide sliding opening, zero-threshold shower, reachable seat and controls, and clear transfer zones let more routines remain independent. Straight reinforced service walls solve the practical mounting problem inside a curved shell.

[S09 — 23 seconds]
Three early checks change the plan: a sixty-inch turning space, a thirty-two-inch clear door opening, and a ramp no steeper than one vertical to twelve horizontal when that benchmark applies. These are starting references, not a substitute for local residential code or the user's actual chair and reach.

[S10 — 22 seconds]
More than one in four older adults report a fall each year, and many falls bring injury or restricted activity. A single-level plan cannot eliminate risk, but it can remove repeated stair exposure, reduce rushed transfers, improve sight lines, and make support easier to add later.

[S11 — 24 seconds]
A conventional retrofit often spends money fighting the original plan: adding ramps, widening doors, sacrificing rooms, or installing a lift. A purpose-built accessible dome can place the route, turning space, kitchen, bathroom, and future support needs into the first sketch—not the final compromise.

[S12 — 23 seconds]
The dome is not magic. Curved walls, drainage, acoustics, structure, and local code all need expert resolution. But if the project starts with the resident's real movement, reach, transfer, and daily routines, the result can be a home that preserves agency instead of merely accommodating decline.
