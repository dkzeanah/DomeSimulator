## 01. One log

A whole house frame, before it is cut.

This is a house frame before it is cut. One log, ripped with a chainsaw straight through its heart into 8 wedges. Every stick in the dome behind it is one of those wedges. By the end of this film you will know what each one does, and what the gap between two of them can carry.

- wedges per log = 8

## 02. Point in, round face out

Time: 00:00:24.300 - 00:00:45.000

Nothing is squared.

Each wedge is a 45 degree slice of the log. In the frame the round outer face looks out at the weather and the point faces the centre of the dome. Nothing is squared, so the only wood that leaves the log is the width of the saw.

On-screen math:

- sector = 360 / 8 = 45 deg

## 03. Two lengths, flat panels

Time: 00:00:45.000 - 00:01:06.272

Built on the ground, one triangle at a time.

There are 120 members in two lengths: 60 cut to 70.2 inches and 60 to 62.7. Three of them make a triangle, and each triangle is built flat on the ground as its own panel. 40 panels make the dome.

On-screen math:

- members = 120
- long 70.2 in x 60
- short 62.7 in x 60

## 04. No hubs

Time: 00:01:06.272 - 00:01:29.772

The sticks hold each other.

Inside a panel the three members do not meet at a point. Each one butts into the side of the next, all turning the same way, like a pinwheel. That is why this dome has no hubs. The corners hold because the sticks lean on each other, not because a steel star is holding them.

## 05. Every edge is two sticks

Time: 00:01:29.772 - 00:01:52.388

And between them, a seam.

Where two panels meet, each one brings its own member, so every inside edge is two sticks side by side. That is 55 seams, and 110 of the 120 members. The last 10 sit on the rim. Everything interesting from here on happens inside those seams.

On-screen math:

- seams = 55
- members in seams = 2 x 55 = 110
- rim members = 10

## 06. The V

Time: 00:01:52.388 - 00:02:25.156

Two sawn faces cannot close flat.

Look at the two members end on. Their sawn faces touch at the ridge on the outside and open toward the inside, at the sector angle less the fold between the two panels. This dome has only two folds: 18.0 degrees at 25 seams and 22.5 at 30. So every V is 27.0 or 22.5 degrees, and 6 inches deep: the radius of the 12 inch log.

On-screen math:

- V = 45 - fold
- 45 - 18.03 = 26.97 deg x 25
- 45 - 22.46 = 22.54 deg x 30
- depth = log radius = 6.0 in

## 07. The key

Time: 00:02:25.156 - 00:02:48.444

Solid, a spline. Hollow, a pipe.

The key fills the V, and it is what 8 bolts a seam pull the two panels against. Make it solid and it is just a spline. Make it hollow and it is a pipe running the length of every seam: 309 feet of it, touching every panel, reaching the crown and the ground.

On-screen math:

- bolts per seam = 8
- channel = 309 ft

## 08. How much room

Time: 00:02:48.444 - 00:03:08.564

That is the whole budget.

Inside a key with 3 millimetre walls there are 5.3 square inches in the tighter seam and 6.5 in the wider one. The largest round thing that passes through is 1.69 inches, or 1.97. Everything that follows has to fit in that.

On-screen math:

- inside, tight seam = 5.31 sq in
- inside, wide seam = 6.51 sq in
- largest round = 1.69 / 1.97 in

## 09. The sizes we assumed

Time: 00:03:08.564 - 00:03:34.180

Nominal where there is a standard; estimates where there is not.

Before anything goes in, here are the sizes this film counts on. Pipe and tubing sizes are nominal, from their standards. Cable sizes are estimates, because every brand differs. And the rule for how full a channel may be is borrowed from conduit: 40 percent of the area, so things can still be pulled through.

On-screen math:

- 1/2 in PEX water line: 0.625 in  (nominal)
- 14/2 NM-B cable, 15 A circuit: 0.400 in x 0.20  (estimate)
- 12 V pair to the Peltier plates, 14 AWG: 0.300 in  (estimate)
- condensate drain, 1/4 in ID vinyl: 0.375 in  (nominal)
- round ducts: 3 and 4 in
- fill allowance: 40% (conduit rule)

## 10. Water and wire fit

Time: 00:03:34.180 - 00:03:55.764

Room to spare.

A half-inch PEX water line, a fifteen-amp circuit, the twelve-volt pair to the cooling plates and the condensate drain. All four fit in either seam, and together they fill 11 percent of the tighter one, against the 40 allowed. There is room left over, and that room matters next.

On-screen math:

- bundle fill, tight seam = 11%
- bundle fill, wide seam = 9%
- allowed = 40%

## 11. Round ducts do not

Time: 00:03:55.764 - 00:04:19.148

The number that does not help.

Now the number that does not help. The smallest ordinary air duct is three inches round, and the key's largest opening is 1.97 inches at best. A three-inch duct does not fit. A four-inch one is nowhere close. If the plan was round ducts through the seams, the plan does not work as drawn.

On-screen math:

- 3 in duct > 1.97 in opening

## 12. The channel is the duct

Time: 00:04:19.148 - 00:04:41.048

It does not need a duct in it.

But the channel does not need a duct in it, because it already is one. With the water, wire and drain inside, one seam still moves 13 cubic feet a minute at a quiet 400 feet a minute. The whole dome needs 18 for fresh air. 2 seams carry the building.

On-screen math:

- dome needs 17.6 cfm
- one seam = 13.2 cfm at 400 fpm
- seams needed = 2

## 13. Peltier plates and the three-way gate

Time: 00:04:41.048 - 00:05:10.548

Drying the air where the wood is.

That air runs past a thermoelectric plate, a Peltier: cold on the channel side, throwing its heat into the room below. Water drops out on the cold plate and runs to the drain. A three-way gate on each seam sends the air along the wood faces to dry them, through a silica cartridge when the weather is too wet, or shuts the air path off from the water path entirely.

On-screen math:

- plates = 360 W
- run 6 h a day

## 14. What the plates are worth

Time: 00:05:10.548 - 00:05:32.708

A dehumidifier, not a water supply.

And honestly: 360 watts of plates, run 6 hours a day, make 73 gallons a year. That is 0.4 inches of rain on this roof. The plates are a dehumidifier that happens to make water. They are not a water supply. The roof is the water supply.

On-screen math:

- 73 gal / year
- = 0.39 in of rain

## 15. If you want a duct: the key becomes a spacer

Time: 00:05:32.708 - 00:06:00.940

Every panel moves out.

If you do want a round duct, the key stops being a filler and becomes a spacer. Push every panel out along its own face and every seam opens. A three-inch duct needs the panels moved out 7.0 inches. A four-inch duct with everything else beside it needs 16.4, and the seams open to 6.4 and 5.1 inches at the ridge, each needing a cap.

On-screen math:

- 3 in duct: panels out 7.0 in
- 4 in + bundle: panels out 16.4 in
- seams open 6.4 / 5.1 in

## 16. What the spacer costs

Time: 00:06:00.940 - 00:06:21.012

A bigger dome, all round.

And a spacer is not free. Moving every panel out makes the whole dome bigger: 6 percent for the three-inch duct, 14 percent for the four. More wood, more skin, a bigger pad, to carry air the plain channel was already carrying.

On-screen math:

- 3 in duct: radius +6.0%
- 4 in duct: radius +14.1%

## 17. Or start with a bigger log

Time: 00:06:21.012 - 00:06:52.940

The other lever is the log. The room in the channel grows with the square of the log's radius: a 10 inch log gives 3.5 square inches, a 12 inch log gives 5.3 square inches, a 15 inch log gives 8.8 square inches, a 18 inch log gives 13.1 square inches. Even the 18 inch log opens only 2.65 inches round: still not a three-inch duct.

On-screen math:

- 10 in log: 3.5 sq in, 1.37 in round
- 12 in log: 5.3 sq in, 1.69 in round
- 15 in log: 8.8 sq in, 2.17 in round
- 18 in log: 13.1 sq in, 2.65 in round

## 18. Printing the key in halves

Time: 00:06:52.940 - 00:07:20.236

Each member carries its own half.

Here is how the key gets made. Split it down the middle and each member carries its own half, printed and screwed to its sawn face before the panel is built. When two panels meet, the halves close into one channel. The whole dome is 110 half-keys in just 2 profiles, printed 8 pieces to a stick on a 250 millimetre printer.

On-screen math:

- half-keys = 110
- profiles = 2
- printed pieces = 820
- printer bed = 250 mm (assumed)

## 20. Where the channels meet

Time: 00:07:47.220 - 00:08:25.920

The frame has no hubs. The services do.

The frame has no hubs, but the services need them. The channels meet at 6 five-way and 10 six-way points, and 10 more on the rim, where they drop to the pad. As built, the V's close to a point there, so each junction is a printed rosette box on the inside of the vertex, where a pipe turns and a wire is spliced, somewhere you can reach. With the four-inch spacer the vertex opens into a hole about 11 inches across, and the hub becomes a printed node the channels plug into.

On-screen math:

- 5-way = 6, 6-way = 10, rim = 10
- node with spacer: 10.9 / 11.2 in across

## 21. Two rules

Time: 00:08:25.920 - 00:08:51.680

A hidden leak is the failure this exists to prevent.

Two rules. Keep pressure joints out of the seams: run the PEX continuous, and make every fitting in a rosette you can open. A hidden leak inside a wooden seam is the one failure this whole system exists to prevent. And whether a cable may run in this chase is your inspector's call, not this film's.
