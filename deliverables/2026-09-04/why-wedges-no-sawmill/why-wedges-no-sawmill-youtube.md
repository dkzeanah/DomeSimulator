Framing lumber starts round, tapered and slightly bent, then gets cut, edged, dried,
planed and graded until it is a rectangle. That rectangle is not a structural
requirement. It is a handling requirement — it stacks on a pallet and fits a stud wall.

A geodesic dome needs none of that. It needs sticks whose ends can be made to meet. So I
stopped making rectangles and started splitting logs into 45-degree wedges, using them
raw. Here is the case, in numbers I measured rather than adjectives I chose.

WHAT THE TREE GIVES
Split radially, 88% of a trunk's board feet survives as structure. Packed with
rectangles, under half does — and none of the difference is bad wood. It is slabs,
edgings and shavings that failed a geometry test, not a strength test.

WHAT IT COSTS
Two timed sessions: twelve wedges in two hours on ten dollars of fuel and bar oil, and
separately forty-two feet of rip at six inches of depth in one hour. They measured
different things, so dividing one by the other gives 7.00 ft of cut per wedge. The
geometry predicts 6.12 ft independently, because eight sectors take seven passes down a
section. They agree to 12%, and that agreement is the only reason any rate here is worth
quoting.

One wedge from a twelve-inch trunk carries 2.69 dressed studs' worth of section. Priced
two independent ways it lands at $10.00 and $10.10 — $50/hour gross at five an hour, $45
net of fuel. Against buying the same section, including the boards you reject and the
truck runs: $1,476 in cash versus $120 of fuel. Three machines leave the chain — sawmill,
edger, planing mill — and fifteen sets of hands, listed one by one rather than waved at.

A CORRECTION, ON CAMERA
An earlier cut of this film said nothing here is mitred. That is wrong, and chapter 15
is the correction. The pinwheel removes the shared vertex — no two ends meet, nothing is
coped — but every butt still lands on the sloped side of the next member, which is a
compound cut and the hardest operation in the build.

Then the figure that rescues it. The bevel is identical on all 120 members and equals
half the sector angle exactly, because it is set by how the log was split rather than
where the member sits. Set once, never touched. The mitre takes three values in the
whole dome, all inside a common saw's swing. Gauging by hand becomes a cradle with three
stops.

THE NUMBER THAT COULD SINK IT
Whether a raw sector beats a two-by-four is a question about diameter, not a yes or no:
section grows with the square of the radius, stiffness with the cube. So the film solves
for the crossover. Above 7.31 in of trunk a sector has more section than a dressed 2x4;
above 9.14 in it is stiffer; above 11.00 in it beats a full-dimension one. Below nine
inches this argument fails. My trees are twelve to fifteen.

FOUR ROTATIONS, FOUR BUILDINGS
Point the wedge outward and the panel opening tapers 1.53 in, so a panel drops in from
outside and is caught like a keystone — panels become swappable modules, not cladding.
That rotation also leaves a connected V along every seam: air, water, wire, pipe,
anywhere on the shell. Point it inward and both invert to the warm side. Sideways gives
no lip, but brings the sawn faces to 22.5 degrees off the surface instead of 67.5, which
is what sheathing wants — and one sideways rotation takes the butt bevel to zero, making
the hardest cut a plain mitre.

A HOUSE, NOT A SYMMETRY REFERENCE
Build the same dome from ten to fifteen inch logs and not one angle changes. The surface
steps up to two and a half inches where thin meets fat; the rest lands in the key, which
was already the variable part.

Not a claim that split wood is better wood. What makes it sufficient is the building: a
triangulated shell puts its members mostly in axial load, doubles every interior edge,
and asks only that the ends can be made to meet.

I did not find a better board. I picked a building that does not need one.

Not an engineer's design check — a real build needs species, grade, moisture and
somebody who will sign it.
