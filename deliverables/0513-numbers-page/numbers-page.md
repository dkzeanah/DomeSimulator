## 1. The market, in one row each

| number | what it is | what it means |
|---|---|---|
| **$665,299** | median new-home price, United States | the house a first-time buyer is told to want |
| **$162 / sq ft** | new construction, per square foot | the going rate for walls |
| **$86.62 / sq ft** | a manufactured home, per square foot | the cheapest mainstream option |
| **$43.45 / sq ft** | this dome, complete, priced | **73% under new construction. Half the trailer.** |
| **7.6 months** | average new home, start to finish | the schedule the industry runs on |
| **37.5 hours** | this dome's frame, tree to standing | the schedule this method runs on |

One honest sentence, because the page must be able to say it: the
fortnight builds the **frame and shell** — the part that costs the
most per square foot — and the rest (skin, floor, ground) is added in
seasons. The comparison is per square foot of shelter, and on that
comparison the dome is less than half the price of the cheapest thing
a bank will finance.

## 2. The fortnight, priced as a job

| line | dollars |

| Avoided cost | **≈ $9,100** |

$243 an hour, against a carpenter's median **$29.12**. And the book
says, in the same chapter, what that number is *not*: it is not
income. It is a substitution value — what you do not spend, kept,
because the builder is also the buyer. Investors should read it the
same way: it is the size of the value gap this product steps into.

## 3. The tree math — why the method is cheaper at the source

* A log sawn into two-by-fours keeps **45 per cent** of itself.
* The same log split into wedges keeps **87.4 per cent**.
* The difference is the fourteen middlemen the method deletes — fell,
  limb, buck, haul, set-up, slab, rotate, cant, resaw, edge, trim,
  sort, dry, plane. Eight operations remain.
* Lumber prices added **$35,872** to the cost of an average new home
  since 2020. The wedge method buys none of that market; its raw
  material is the storm-felled tree and the woodlot nobody logs.

## 5. Why $110,000 is a small number

**If a potato salad can raise $55,000, a house can raise $110,000.**
The Potato Salad Kickstarter — a joke, four dollars a pledge, a bowl
of starch — closed at $55,492. This campaign is a working prototype,
a published book, and a cause its audience already believes in.

And the arithmetic of the goal fits on a napkin:

| mix | backers | raised |
|---|---|---|
| 150 plans @ $35 | 150 | $5,250 |
| 100 quilter's kits @ $95 | 100 | $9,500 |

Three hundred and forty-eight people. Not ten thousand. A single
veterans' community page, one well-timed story about the quilt
network, and one housing newsletter is more traffic than this goal
requires. The goal is set low **on purpose** — it is the sum of
twelve named lines, not a round number somebody liked, and the
stretch goals above it are the real ambition.

## 6. The market behind the mission

* **32,882** veterans had no home of their own on one night in
  January 2024 (HUD). The method is written by one of their number and
  built to be *taught* — one panel at a time, one person on a ladder,
  no crane, no mill. The stretch goals give a frame to a veterans'
  housing program as a build event their residents crew.
* Roughly **four in five** single-parent households in the United
  States are headed by a mother. This system pays them for an evening
  at a sewing machine — the insulation is a quilt, sewn from waste
  textiles, paid per layer, the maker's name on the tag. *(Census
  figure to re-verify at campaign copy time.)*
* Neither audience needs a mortgage to start. One needs trees, one
  needs an evening, one needs land — and the campaign sells all three
  the thing they are missing.

## 7. What the money becomes

**$110,000, twelve lines, zero salary, zero marketing budget:**

* $4,200 — a test platform: one standing dome, left out in a year of
  weather.
* $1,850 — sensors in the wall, logging and **publishing** for a year,
  whichever way the data comes out.
* $6,800 — an engineer, for the parts that carry people. The refusal
  to guess, budgeted.
* $9,500 — the utility hub, three iterations to a design that ships.
* $14,500 + $11,800 + $8,900 — CNC router, plasma, laser: the shop
  that turns "one dome" into "the second one".
* $18,000 + $12,500 — moulded-strut tooling and the composite
  triangle: the member that arrives with its hardware in it.
* $7,600 — automation for the repetitive work.
* $5,000 — seeding the quilt network: the first paid layers.
* $9,350 — making and shipping what backers are owed, budgeted at
  cost.

Every dollar becomes a machine or a measurement. A year after funding,
an investor can open the published sensor data and *see* the money.

## The one-paragraph close

New housing costs $162 a square foot and takes seven months. This
method produces shelter at $43.45 a square foot in a fortnight of
hands-on work, from trees the market prices at firewood, with the
margin named and the arithmetic published. The goal is $110,000 —
348 backers at a realistic mix — to buy machines and measurements,
not salary and spin. The customers are the two groups the housing
market serves last and charges most: veterans, and single mothers.
The product for both already exists, standing, and can be checked
line by line. That is the whole investment case: **a housing method
with a test suite.**
