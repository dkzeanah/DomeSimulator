# Cover prompt — Geodesic Dome Wedge Method: The 40 Hour Cabin

Two versions. Try **A** first: attach the current cover (`Book_Cover_final.png`)
and paste A, so everything that already works is kept. If Gemini redraws the
whole picture anyway, use **B**, which describes the complete cover.

The frame description is taken from the book (Chapter 12, "Nothing Meets at
the Corner") and the raw-wedge simulator's own joint rule: every panel is its
own three-member frame; each member's end butts into the side of the next;
all three chase round in the same direction; the corner point is empty.

---

## A — Edit the attached cover

```
Edit the attached book cover. Keep EVERYTHING the same -- the sunset, the lake and hills, the pine trees, the builder in the plaid shirt lifting a timber, the split log with its pie-slice wedges, the stacked triangular wedges, the chainsaw on the stump, the wood chips, the colours, the lighting, the lettering and its positions -- and change ONLY the dome frame, so that it is built the way this book describes. Keep the same size, position and camera angle for the dome.

HOW THE FRAME MUST BE BUILT (this is the whole point of the book):

1. EVERY TRIANGLE IS ITS OWN SEPARATE WOODEN FRAME. The dome is made of forty triangular frames, like forty picture frames, each built from its own three timber members. Neighbouring triangles do not share a strut.

2. SO EVERY LINE OF THE DOME IS DOUBLED. Wherever two triangles meet, there are TWO timbers lying side by side, parallel, touching -- one belonging to each triangle -- with a thin tapered filler strip (a "key") visible in the seam between them. From outside, every seam of the dome reads as a double line of timber, never a single strut.

3. THE CORNERS ARE A PINWHEEL, NOT A MITRE AND NOT A HUB. Inside each triangle, each member runs straight through to its corner, and the END of the next member stops short and butts squarely into the SIDE of it, a hand's width in from the tip. At the next corner the roles pass on: that member now runs through and the third one butts into its side; at the third corner the third runs through and the first butts into it. So the three members chase each other round the triangle in one direction, like the three blades of a pinwheel toy or a camera shutter. No member is cut to a point, and no two ends meet end-to-end.

4. EVERY TRIANGLE TURNS THE SAME WAY. All forty pinwheels spin in the same direction (all clockwise when seen from outside the dome), so the whole dome has one consistent, subtly swirling rhythm of offset member ends.

5. NOTHING MEETS AT THE POINTS. Where five or six triangles come together, their corners gather around a small EMPTY centre -- a little open space with nothing in it. There is NO hub, NO connector plate, NO bolt cluster, NO ball, and NO single point where the timbers converge. The offset corner ends swirl around that empty centre like petals.

6. EACH MEMBER IS A WEDGE. Every timber is a solid split-log wedge with a TRIANGULAR cross-section (one-eighth of a round log, like a slice of pie): the wide flat face points OUTWARD, forming the outside surface of the dome, and the sharp point faces INWARD toward the centre. Where the end of a member is visible, its end grain is a triangle.

7. THE OVERALL SHAPE: a two-frequency (2V) geodesic hemisphere on a ten-sided base -- forty flat triangles, with five-pointed star patterns (one at the top, five around the lower wall), exactly half a sphere, about twice as wide as it is tall. The top is still unfinished: a few triangles at the crown are missing, and the builder is lifting a member toward that opening.

Keep the few flat plywood panels already fitted into some of the lower triangles.

Output the result as large as possible, portrait, same proportions as the attached image.
```
