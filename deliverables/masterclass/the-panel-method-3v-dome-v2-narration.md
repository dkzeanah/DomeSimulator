## 01. Two ways to frame a dome

Same hill, two methods.

On this hill stand two domes framed two different ways. One is the wedge dome: 120 members split from logs with a chainsaw, carrying their angles in their own shape. The other is a three-frequency dome of framed panels: 105 triangles, each one its own small frame of sawn sticks, built flat on a jig, skinned in the shop, and screwed to its neighbours on site. It is how most small timber domes built from bought plans are made, and it is worth knowing properly.

## 03. Three frequency

Time: 00:01:06.900 - 00:01:46.292

Three strut lengths, two kinds of point.

Take the icosahedron's twenty faces and cut each into nine smaller triangles, three to an edge, then push every new point out onto the sphere. That is a three-frequency dome, and it has only three strut lengths. At our size, 16 feet across the base, A is 34.0 inches, B is 39.4 and C is 40.2. Twelve points have five neighbours and every other point has six. The triangles round the fives make pentagons; the rest make hexagons.

On-screen math:

- A = 34.0 in
- B = 39.4 in
- C = 40.2 in

## 04. Five eighths

Time: 00:01:46.292 - 00:02:20.592

Cut below the middle.

This sphere has no level ring of points at its middle, so the usual cut goes lower, to the points nearest five-eighths of the way down. What is kept is 105 triangles: 30 in pentagons and 75 in hexagons. Its height is 0.594 of its width, a little under the five-eighths of its name. That wall below the middle is the point of the cut: it stands up nearly straight, so the floor near the edge is floor you can stand on.

On-screen math:

- panels kept = 105
- height / width = 0.594

## 05. A base that zig-zags

Time: 00:02:20.592 - 00:02:54.032

Some sections taper; some do not.

The bottom of the cut is 15 edges, and it does not lie on one level. Its corners alternate between two heights, 40 millimetres apart, about 1.56 inches. So 10 of the base sections must be tapered, low at one end and high at the other, and 5 sit level. Build the base ring first, set it on the deck, check its diagonals, and fasten it down. Everything above it goes together easier when this first ring is right.

On-screen math:

- 15 base edges
- 10 tapered + 5 level
- rise = 40 mm

## 06. Two panels, over and over

The hexagon panel has the same base and two 40.2 inch sides, and there are 75. These outlines are the outside faces of the panels. Nothing may stick out past them, and nothing may fall short of them.

## 07. Finish, then rip in half

Time: 00:03:24.280 - 00:04:03.380

A cut through the centre makes two copies.

The sticks are sawn lumber. Finish the boards first, while they are whole: the face you rip will be hidden in the joint. Then tilt the table saw blade to the bevel and rip each board down its middle. A cut through the centre of a rectangle leaves two pieces that are copies of each other, turned end for end. It is the same symmetry that makes a split log give identical wedges. Turn the offcut over and send it back through for a hair more, and the two halves come out exactly alike.

On-screen math:

- stick = 1.50 x 1.19 in
- bevel set at 6.5 deg

## 08. Draw the jig with two arcs

Time: 00:04:03.380 - 00:04:42.080

Three lengths make one triangle.

The jig is a sheet of plywood with the panel drawn on it full size. Snap a straight line for the base and mark its two ends. A thin stick with a nail at each end is a trammel. Set it to one side length and swing an arc from one end; set it to the other side and swing from the other end. Where the arcs cross is the third corner. Measure all three sides again, then join the points. For the hexagon panel the apex lands 35.1 inches above the base.

On-screen math:

- two arcs from the base ends
- they cross at the apex
- hexagon apex height = 35.1 in

## 09. Rails, and the line

Time: 00:04:42.080 - 00:05:12.780

The jig holds any stick of the right section.

Lay a stick along each side, flat face down and bevel out, with its outer edge splitting the line. Clamp it, butt a rail against its inside edge, and screw the rail down. Do all three sides. Now any stick pushed against a rail lands exactly on its line. The jig does not care what size the sticks are, so long as every stick is the same size and the bevel is right.

On-screen math:

- stick edge on the line
- rail behind every stick

## 10. Sticks that turn like a pinwheel

Time: 00:05:12.780 - 00:05:58.680

Equal sides, different sticks.

The three sticks do not meet at a point. Each runs to one corner and butts into the inside face of the next, so from above the corners turn like a pinwheel. That is why the two equal sides of a panel still take different sticks. In a hexagon panel the two C sticks are 38.47 and 38.51 inches along their outer edges, with tail cuts 31.4 and 29.3 degrees off square. Cut one end, lay the stick in, mark where the next stick crosses it, and cut that end with the miter and the bevel set together. Clamp a stop block and repeat, and check the fit every few sticks.

On-screen math:

- C sticks: 38.47 in and 38.51 in
- tail cuts: 31.4 and 29.3 deg
- 6 distinct sticks in the dome

## 11. One bevel cannot be exact

Time: 00:05:58.680 - 00:06:34.180

The number that does not flatter the method.

Here is the number that does not flatter this method. The panels do not all turn by the same angle where they meet, so the exact bevel runs from 5.7 degrees to 7.2. A shop rips every stick at one setting, 6.5 degrees, so the worst joint is 0.8 degrees off. That is too small to see and not too small to leak. Lay a bead of sealant or a gasket between the frames as they go together; that is what takes up the difference.

On-screen math:

- exact: 5.7 to 7.2 deg
- one setting: 6.5 deg
- worst error: 0.8 deg

## 12. Skin it in sections

Time: 00:06:34.180 - 00:07:05.436

One sheet per pentagon or hexagon.

Before raising, screw panels into whole pentagons and hexagons on the shop floor and cover each group with one sheet. At this cut 6 pentagons and 10 hexagons come out whole, and 15 panels are skinned one at a time. Pull the film tight across the group and staple it on the bevelled edges, where the next section hides the staples. A torn section is then one section to replace, not the whole dome.

On-screen math:

- 6 pentagons + 10 hexagons
- 15 single panels

## 13. A window is a panel with hinges

Time: 00:07:05.436 - 00:07:33.336

A spare frame on two hinges.

For each window, build one extra frame. Leave the skin off that panel in the dome, cover the spare frame, and hinge it along its top edge. A small gas strut of the kind kitchen cabinets use holds it open. Weather-strip the face it closes on so rain running down the dome cannot creep under it, and tape the hinge seam once the dome is up.

On-screen math:

- one spare frame per window
- hinged at the top

## 14. Raise it loose, then tighten

Time: 00:07:33.336 - 00:08:11.636

Flexible until the last panel.

Assemble from the base up, with a helper and plenty of clamps. Put only a few screws in each joint until the last panel closes the dome: until then the shell flexes, and that flex is what lets the last panels in. Lay a bead of sealant or a gasket between frames as they meet. Then tape the open seams between sections from the bottom up, each strip lapping over the one below, so water always runs onto tape and never under it. Do not raise a loose shell in a wind.

On-screen math:

- base up, few screws
- gasket as it goes
- tape bottom up, lapped

## 15. A floor the shape of the base

Time: 00:08:11.636 - 00:08:47.020

Joists to the corners, girders on the five-fold.

This dome stands on a radial deck that copies its base: 15 joists from the centre, one to each base corner, and a rim board along each base edge, each end cut 12 degrees off square. Under the joists, a ring of 5 girders follows the dome's own five-fold symmetry, with a post under each girder joint. Mark the centre, lay the girders out on the ground to place the posts, set the post heights to a level line, then roll in the joists.

On-screen math:

- 15 joists, 15 rim boards
- rim end cut = 180 / 15 = 12 deg
- girder end cut = 180 / 5 = 36 deg

## 16. Hold it down

Time: 00:08:47.020 - 00:09:11.720

Light for its size; wind lifts.

A dome is light for its size, and wind lifts. On a deck like this one, angle brackets tie every base section to the frame below. On gravel or earth, steel stakes driven at a slant and strapped to the base sections do the same job. Anchor it before the skin is finished, not after the first storm.

On-screen math:

- deck: brackets
- gravel or earth: slanted stakes + straps

## 17. A zome without a bevel

Time: 00:09:11.720 - 00:09:39.904

One stick, one miter, per row.

The same panel thinking frames a zome. On the bench is a one-to-5 model of a nine-sided zome, 36 panels in 4 rows of 9. Each panel is four identical sticks in a pinwheel, square through their depth, with a miter at each end and the two ends parallel. Nothing is bevelled at all: one stick and one miter for every panel in a row.

On-screen math:

- 36 panels = 4 rows x 9
- 4 identical sticks per panel
- no bevels

## 18. The gap that is allowed

Time: 00:09:39.904 - 00:10:18.168

Square edges leave a V outside.

Square-edged panels butted on their inside edges leave a V-shaped gap on the outside. In a two-by-four on edge ours opens from 1.2 to 1.7 inches. On a small zome, screw the inside edges together and let the roof bridge the gap. On a big one, a wedge block bevelled to half the fold fills it and gives the joint more to bear on. Fasten loosely at first: the panels hinge, and the angle sets itself as the inside edges line up. The hub problem of a zome is gone.

On-screen math:

- gap = 2 x depth x tan(fold / 2)
- = 1.2 to 1.7 in
- block bevel = fold / 2
