# Why Build This Way

*Every figure below is computed from `two_v_demo/why_build_economics.py` and `two_v_demo/house_economics.py`; the author's own round figures are listed at the end and labelled as the author's.*

## How much shelter does an hour buy

**Not how cheap the house is. How much shelter an hour buys.**

The case for this dome is not that it makes a house cheap. It is that it changes the trade between a person, their time, raw wood and shelter. This is one shell, 300 square feet, from 2 trees. The question worth asking is not how cheap the house is, but how much shelter one hour of your life can buy, and this way of building changes the answer.

On screen:

- 300 sq ft of shell
- 2 trees

## A long chain, and a short one

**Income, tax, store, contractor, loan. Or tree, wedge, jig, shell.**

Conventional housing asks you to join a long chain. You sell hours for money, pay tax on it, buy lumber that was sawn, dried, shipped, stocked and marked up, hire the labor to put it together, and very often borrow for all of it for decades. This system shortens the chain. A tree is split into wedges, the wedges go into a jig, and the jig makes the shell. Part of your labor turns straight into structure, without passing through a paycheck, a tax bill, a store and a loan on the way.

## The tree is the product

**Split, not sawn: the round log keeps its wood.**

A sawmill forces a round tree into rectangles. The wedge does not. An 8 inch log splits into 8 sectors, and each one keeps 6.28 square inches of wood, against 5.25 in a dressed two by four. That does not make every wedge stronger than every board; from a log that small, a single wedge is the weaker stick in bending. What it means is that very little happens between the tree and the frame: 8 operations, where a graded board takes 14.

- 6.28 sq in, one wedge (from an 8-inch log)
- 5.25 sq in, a dressed 2x4
- -33% bending strength

## The geometry does some of the work

**A wedge arrives with its angle already in it.**

An eighth of a log arrives with two split faces 45 degrees apart. The dome's panels fold at only 2 angles, 18.03 and 22.46 degrees. The wedge does not match them exactly, but it starts with useful angle already in it, and a shaped key takes up the rest, so nobody has to manufacture every angle out of a rectangle. That is the rule the whole system runs on: do not make every piece perfect; make the system force imperfect pieces into the right relationship.

*the angles*

- · 22.46° the wider fold

## Locate first, cut second

**The jig solves the geometry once, not for every stick.**

Custom timber work means measuring, transferring angles, fitting, checking and trimming, again and again. Here the jig decides where each member belongs and which way it turns. The members are left long, their ends hang past the triangle, and once the jig holds all three in their true relationship, the excess is cut flush. Locate accurately first; cut accurately second. The hard geometry is solved once, in the jig, and a complicated dome becomes 40 repeats of one job.

## What an hour produces

**Not a paycheck. Value you no longer have to buy.**

300 square feet in 80 hours is 3.75 square feet an hour. At 100 dollars a square foot, that floor is worth 30,000 dollars finished, but framing did not create all of it; plumbing, wiring, windows and finishes are still to come. So take only the framing, 16 percent of it: 4,800 dollars. Divided by 80 hours, that is 60 dollars an hour. Nobody hands me that as a wage. A wage is what someone pays you for your hour; this is what your hour saves you from having to buy.

On screen:

- 3.75 sq ft an hour

- 300 sq ft of shell

- = $30,000 finished space
- × 0.16 framing's share
- = $4,800 of framing
- ÷ 80 hours
- = $60 an hour

## Why the hour beats a wage

**It replaces the wood as well as the work.**

Of that 4,800 dollars, the labor is 40 percent: 1,920 dollars, and over the same 80 hours that is 24 dollars an hour. That is the framing labor alone. The dome way produces 60, which is 2.5 times as much, an index of 250 where framing labor alone scores a hundred. Be clear about where that comes from: it is one over the labor share. You supply the labor, and you also replace the wood you would have bought. That second part is exactly what a wage comparison leaves out.

- · 2.5× the dome's hour

## Debt makes it larger

**An avoided dollar can be an avoided interest-bearing dollar.**

Now suppose that framing would otherwise ride on a 30 year mortgage at 6.5 percent. Then 4,800 dollars does not stay 4,800; it becomes 10,922 dollars of payments. Spread over the same 80 hours, that is 137 dollars an hour. That is not a wage, and it is not today's money: it is payments that never have to be made, stretched across 30 years. Today's value is still 60 dollars an hour. But a dollar avoided before it enters a mortgage is a dollar that never collects interest.

*debt-avoidance value*

- = $137 an hour, avoided

## Interest builds nothing

**No bedroom, no wall, no square foot.**

Take a 100,000 dollar mortgage at the same rate. Over 30 years you pay 127,544 dollars of interest on it, 227,544 in all. At 100 dollars a square foot, the interest alone would buy 1,275 square feet. Interest builds no bedroom, no workshop, no wall. It is the price of having the house before you have the money. Building directly does something else: effort becomes equity. Hours become members, members become panels, and panels become square feet.

## Count it in hours of your life

**Money is stored labor.**

Here is the comparison that matters most. Buying that 4,800 dollar frame, at 25 dollars an hour take-home, takes 192 hours of work. Building it takes 80 hours, plus 32 more to earn its 800 dollars of cash, 112 hours in all. That keeps 80 hours of your life, 2 full working weeks, on one shell. And the typical worker takes home less than 25, about 19.69 an hour, which makes buying cost 244 hours instead.

*life-hours*

- $4,800 to buy the frame
- ÷ $25 an hour, take-home
- = 192 hours to buy it
- − 112 hours to build it
- = 80 hours of life kept
- 244 hours, median pay (to buy it at $19.69 an hour take-home)

## When to build, and when to buy

**Build while your hour creates more than it earns.**

This is a rule, not an ideology. What building earns per hour is the money it avoids, less the cash it still costs, over the hours it takes. 4,800 less 800 is 4,000, and over 80 hours that is 50 dollars an hour. If you take home less than that, those hours are worth more building than working extra to buy the same frame. If you take home a good deal more, buying may be the better use of your time. Build while the value your hour creates beats what it earns after tax.

On screen:


*the break-even wage*

- $4,800 avoided
- − $800 cash it still costs

- = $50 an hour: the break-even

## Out of the commodity chain

**Every member from a nearby tree is one less price shock.**

A conventional house also runs a long critical path, about 33 weeks from start to finish, carrying financing, weather, scheduling and overhead the whole way. And it rides on commodity lumber. In the spring of twenty twenty one, framing lumber topped 1,500 dollars a thousand board feet and added 35,872 dollars to an average new home. A dome still needs fasteners, glazing, wiring, plumbing and a foundation. But every structural member cut from a nearby tree instead of bought at a yard is one less exposure to that market. A tree on the land stops being scenery and becomes inventory.

- 33 weeks, a new house (start to finish, the Census average)

**One shell, on one sheet.**

A break-even of 50 dollars an hour. And one shell a year for 30 years is 144,000 dollars of framing and 9,000 square feet.

## What the model does not promise

**A model, not a guarantee.**

None of this is a guarantee; it is a model. The 60 dollars an hour is money not spent, not money earned, and it only counts if you would otherwise have bought the frame. The frame is 16.6 percent of what a new house costs to build, and permits, wiring, plumbing, windows and finishes are still ahead, some of it needing a licensed trade and an inspection. The house labor budget I once compared against was 80,000 dollars; the builders' survey puts that house's labor nearer 51,491, about 39.01 dollars per working hour of the build, so the dome's 60 is 154 percent of it, not the same. And my 4,800 dollar frame is cheaper than either published price, 5,186 at a manufactured home's rate and 9,798 at a new house's, so the case is made at its weakest.

On screen:

- 154% of the survey's rate ($39.01 a work-hour, not $60.61)
- $4,800 my frame (published: $5,186 and $9,798)

## The why

**More shelter from each hour of a finite life.**

So the reason to build this way is not that domes look different, or that ordinary houses are bad, or that lumber is useless. It is that conventional housing is built around buying highly processed materials and buying specialized labor, and this asks whether the owner can take back part of both. If the answer is yes, the rest follows: less processing, less bought lumber and labor, less financing, less interest, less exposure to prices and schedules, and more use of what is already standing on the land. The question is not how cheap the house is, but how much shelter an hour of your life can buy. On these numbers, this way of building changes that exchange rate. That is the reason.
