# The $20 Pine

*Every figure below is computed from `two_v_demo/pine_value_economics.py`, which borrows the tree from `wedge_geometry.DEFAULT_LOG` and the framing figures from `why_build_economics.py`; the author's own figures are listed at the end and labelled as the author's.*

## What is a pine worth

**Not one price. A ladder of them.**

What is a pine tree worth? Ask a timber buyer, a firewood dealer, a sawmill and a builder, and each gives a different answer, because a tree has no single value. It has a ladder of them, and the rung it reaches depends on what somebody does to it, and on how much of it they keep. Take one representative pine, the log the wedge film is built on: 60 feet of usable stem, 15 inches across at the butt and 5.5 at the top. Let us climb its ladder, one rung at a time.

## What the ladder rests on

**Measured, published and mine, kept apart.**

Every rung rests on a figure, so here they all are before any of them is used. The tree is my representative pine, the wedge film's own log. Some figures are published: a sawmill's price for common pine, what standing pine sells for, and the size of a cord. Some are mine: a mill keeps 60 percent of the stem as the lumber it wants, splitting keeps 88, and the sawing rate and the cord price are mine too. The simulator checks both recoveries. Sawn into two by fours, this log keeps only 45 percent; split, it keeps 88.4 before any trimming. So the mill gets the benefit of the doubt, and the wedge gets no more than the model allows.

## The stem, in board feet

**442 board feet, and about $24 standing.**

The usable stem is a long, tapering cone: 36.8 cubic feet of solid wood. A cubic foot holds 12 board feet, so that is 442 board feet, the most that any method could ever get out of it. Green, it weighs about 1.01 tons, and at the published stumpage of $23.23 a ton, it sells standing for about 24 dollars. That is the twenty-dollar pine. Every rung above this one is something somebody does to it.

*the stem*

- = 442 bd ft in the stem
- · $24 standing, on the stump

## Burned, it is heat

**Burned, it is worth its heat: $113.**

The first rung up is fire. This stem is 36.8 cubic feet of solid wood. A cord is 4 feet high, 4 deep and 8 long, 128 cubic feet stacked, but air fills the gaps, and only about 90 of those cubic feet are wood. So the stem makes 0.41 of a cord. At 275 dollars a cord picked up, that is 113 dollars, or about 123 delivered. That values the tree for its heat. Every rung above this one values it for its shape.

On screen:


*as firewood*

- 36.8 cu ft in the stem
- ÷ 90 cu ft of wood in a cord
- = 0.41 of a cord
- × $275 a cord, picked up
- = $113 as firewood
- · $123 delivered

## Split, not sawn

**A mill wants rectangles. A wedge takes the log as it is.**

Now shape. A sawmill has to turn a round log into rectangles, and everything outside them leaves as slabs, edgings and sawdust. The picture is the simulator's, packing this log with two by fours, and it keeps only 45 percent. A good mill cutting mixed sizes does better, so I give it 60 percent. Splitting follows the round log instead of fighting it. From the same stem it keeps 88 percent, 389 board feet, where the mill keeps 265. That is 124 more board feet of usable wood from one tree, because the corners of a log are not bad wood. They are only not rectangular.

On screen:


*the same stem, two ways*

- 442 bd ft in the stem
- × 0.88 kept by splitting
- = 389 bd ft split
- − 265 bd ft sawn, at 60%
- = 124 bd ft more, from one tree

## Almost half as much again

**46.7% more wood, 70% less thrown away.**

Put the two recoveries side by side. 88 percent over 60 is 1.467, so splitting gets 46.7 percent more usable wood out of the same tree. Now look at what is left behind. The mill leaves 40 percent of the stem, 177 board feet of it. Splitting leaves 12 percent, 53. That is 70 percent less of the tree thrown away, from the same tree, with the same saw.

On screen:


*the gain*

- 88% kept by splitting
- ÷ 60% kept by a mill
- = 1.467 times the wood
- · +46.7% more usable wood
- · 70% less left behind

## The same wood from fewer trees

**2 trees split give as much wood as 2.93 sawn.**

A 300 square foot shell needs 2 of these trees felled. Each one, split, gives 389 board feet, so the pair gives 778. A mill keeping 60 percent gets 265 from a tree, so to match the pair it would need 2.93 trees. Put the other way round, the mill's way needs almost half as many trees again, and a tree not cut is a tree still standing, or a tree left for the next building.

On screen:


*the pair's wood*

- 389 bd ft split, one tree
- × 2 trees
- = 778 bd ft from the pair
- ÷ 265 bd ft sawn, one tree
- = 2.93 trees, sawn

## Priced as lumber

**Priced as wood, splitting is worth $186 more a tree.**

Now put a price on the wood. The split yield, 389 board feet, at one sawmill's list price for common pine of $1.50 a board foot, is worth 584 dollars. The sawn yield, 265 board feet, is worth 398. That is 186 dollars more from one tree, and 372 from the shell's 2. And the sawn path still has to pay for the sawing: at my rate of $0.60 a board foot, about 159 dollars for this tree.

## Built, it replaces framing

**Not what the wood would sell for. What it does.**

The next rung is not a price for wood at all. In the dome, these trees become a shell of 300 square feet. At 100 dollars a square foot, with framing 16 percent of it, the framing that shell replaces is worth 4,800 dollars, the figure the last film built. The frame uses 90 feet of trunk, 1.5 trees' worth, but 2 are felled, so I divide by 2: each tree's share is 2,400 dollars. That is use value: not what the wood would fetch, but what you no longer have to buy, because the wood is doing the job.

On screen:


*as structure*

- · 300 sq ft of shell
- $4,800 of framing, one shell
- ÷ 2 trees felled
- = $2,400 one tree's share

## Carried on a mortgage

**What the same framing costs when it is borrowed.**

Most framing is not paid for in cash. It rides on a mortgage. On a 30 year loan at 6.5 percent, 4,800 dollars of framing becomes 10,922 dollars of payments, and this tree's half of that is 5,461. Read this rung carefully. It is nominal dollars, paid a month at a time for 30 years, not money in your hand today. But it is money a household never has to send to a lender, because a tree did the job instead.

On screen:


*as financed framing*

- · 6.5% for 30 years
- $10,922 of payments, one shell
- ÷ 2 trees felled
- = $5,461 one tree, nominal

## The ladder

**One tree, every rung.**

Now put the rungs together. Standing, about 24 dollars. Burned, 113. Sawn into lumber, 398. Split into wedge stock, 584. Built into structure, 2,400. And as the mortgage payments that structure avoids, 5,461, nominal, over 30 years. It is the same tree on every rung. What changes is how much of it is kept, and how far along the chain it travels before anyone has to pay for the next step.

## The wood did not get better

**It got used.**

Compare the structure rung with the ones below it. 2,400 dollars is 21 times what the tree brings as firewood, 6 times its sawn lumber, and 4.1 times its split stock. The wood did not get better between those rungs. The same fibres, from the same tree, stopped being sold as a commodity and started doing a job. The biggest step on this ladder is not from the woods to the mill. It is from material to function.

*structure, against*

- · 6× the sawn lumber
- · 4.1× the split stock

## What the tree is worth to me

**What it replaces, less what it costs me to make it do so.**

So here is the valuation that matters to a builder. A tree is worth to me what it can replace, less the cash it costs to process, less what my own hours could have earned instead. This tree replaces 2,400 dollars of framing. Its share of the work is 40 hours, and at 25 dollars an hour take-home, that is 1,000 dollars of my time. Its share of the cash is half the shell's 800, 400 dollars. That leaves 1,000 dollars. A pine that sells for 24 dollars standing is worth 1,000 to the person who builds with it, after paying for their own time. With the fortnight's actual receipts of 497 dollars, it is 1,152.

## The household as the whole chain

**Logger, mill, yard, framer and lender, kept at home.**

This is vertical integration, at the scale of one household. A commercial house pays every hand between the stump and the wall: the logger, the hauler, the mill, the kiln, the yard, the framer, and very often the lender. Each one takes a margin, and every margin is inside the price. This method does not make those businesses unnecessary for everyone. It lets one owner do the few steps a chainsaw and a jig can do, and keep what those steps would have cost. That is where the rungs above the wood come from.

## What the ladder does not say

**The generous figures, and the ones against me.**

Three cautions, because a ladder like this is easy to oversell. First, my mill figure is generous to the mill: the simulator's own packing of this log keeps 45 percent, and I used 60. Second, my sawing rate may be high. A survey of portable sawmill owners in 2010 found them charging $0.20 to $0.30 a board foot. Prices have risen since, but the sawn path may cost less than I said. Third, the top two rungs are not cash. The 2,400 dollars counts only if you would otherwise have bought the frame, and the financed rung is nominal payments, spread over 30 years.

## Thousands of dollars of structure

**The difference is what you do with it.**

So a twenty-dollar pine is not a twenty-dollar pine. On the stump it brings about 24 dollars. Burned, 113. Sawn, 398. Split, 584. Built into a shell, it replaces 2,400 dollars of framing, and 5,461 of payments on a loan. The tree is the same on every rung. What moves it up the ladder is how much of it you keep, and how many of the steps between the tree and the wall you are willing to take yourself.
