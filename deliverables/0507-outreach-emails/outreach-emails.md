# Outreach Emails — Promotion, Paid Shoutouts, and Propositions

Templates for every person the campaign needs to reach, plus the rules
for writing them. The campaign's cause is real and the emails should
*know* it — but the reader should be moved by the facts, not told to be
moved. One statistic. One image. One concrete ask. No adjectives about
heroism. The project does not beg; it offers.

---

## The doctrine

1. **The emotion lives in the numbers.** "32,882 veterans had no home on
   a single night in January 2024" needs no commentary. "A log sawn into
   two-by-fours keeps 45 per cent of itself; split into wedges it keeps
   87.4 per cent" is a whole argument in one sentence. Quote the fact,
   stop, let the silence do the work.
2. **The reader is the subject of the email, not us.** Their audience,
   their format, their next video, their newsletter's open rate. The
   story exists to serve their channel, not the other way round.
3. **Every ask carries its payment in the same paragraph.** The
   intangible payment is real and is listed: content nobody else has,
   founding-backer credit that outlives the campaign, early access, the
   halo of a cause their audience already believes in.
4. **Money is offered plainly or not at all.** Paid requests name the
   deliverables and the budget band up front. Nobody negotiates with
   someone who pretends the money doesn't matter.
5. **One email per person, one follow-up, then silence.** The campaign
   is the long game; the project is the proof.

## The intangible-payment bank

What a partner, promoter or backer receives beyond any cash:

* **Content nobody else has** — exclusive stills, the solved geometry,
  the build film's chapters before release, the book's unpublished
  numbers, the prototype's story beats.
* **Founding-backer credit** — name on the backer wall, in the next
  edition of the book, and in the film credits. Books get revised;
  credits persist.
* **The nameplate** — a sponsor's name on a machine that will cut
  panels for years, or on the test platform that will be measured and
  published about for a year.
* **The story itself** — a working-class housing method invented by a
  veteran, insulation sewn and paid for at kitchen tables, a house that
  begins as a storm-felled tree. That is three videos and a newsletter
  for anybody who covers it.
* **Early access** — the codebase, the design session, the first run of
  the plans, before the public.
* **The halo, honestly earned** — association with open-source housing,
  waste-stream insulation, and veterans' housing work, evidenced by
  receipts and published data rather than slogans.

## E1 — Organic promo: off-grid / tiny-house creator

**Subject:** A $692 house frame — your next video is standing in a clearing

Hi [name],

I've been following your [off-grid/tiny-house] builds for a while — the
[mention a specific video or detail] one especially.

I wanted to send you something that fits your format exactly. A Navy
veteran built a 365-square-foot geodesic cabin from three trees and one
chainsaw off a hardware-store shelf. The frame cost $692 in fuel, oil
and fasteners, and took 37.5 hours of hands-on work. The whole method is
published — every number computed by open software, the book, the cut
lists — and the prototype is standing, winched up between two trees.

What I think your audience would care about: the wedge. You split a log
into eighths and the pie-slice is already a structural member — no mill,
no planer, no lumberyard. A log that keeps 45 per cent of itself as
boards keeps 87.4 per cent as wedges. That is a build video, a cost
breakdown, and a "should you do this" all in one.

I can send exclusive stills, the build footage, and the numbers before
they're public, and we'd credit you on the campaign page and in the next
edition of the book as a founding backer. The Kickstarter (it funds the
tooling and a year of wall sensors) launches [date].

If it's a fit, I'd love to get it in front of you. If not, no reply
needed — keep building.

[name], for the 40 Hour Cabin project

## E2 — Organic promo: woodworking / maker channel

**Subject:** The joint with nothing in the middle (for your channel)

Hi [name],

Your [joinery/fixture/jig video] made me write this one down for you.

This project joins a geodesic dome's 120 members without a single
machined connector. Each member is a wedge split from a round log —
bark outside, two sawn faces, a point at the pith — and at every corner
the three members lap each other like a pinwheel, each butt end landing
flat on the next member's *side*, with the corner point left empty. One
compound cut per member, made once, off a jig. The whole joint is three
surfaces and two bolts.

There's a book of the arithmetic, an open solver, and a standing
prototype, and the campaign that funds the tooling launches [date] —
goal lines are public: a CNC router, a plasma table, a laser, and a
year of moisture sensors in the wall, published whichever way the data
comes out.

If the pinwheel joint is worth a video to you, I'll send the solved
geometry, the jig drawings and unpublished build stills ahead of the
launch, and put your channel on the backer wall and in the book's next
edition. No strings — if the joint doesn't interest you, the drawings
are yours to keep either way.

[name], for the 40 Hour Cabin project

## The paid-shoutout package (internal — what we actually pay for)

| deliverable | budget band | usage |
|---|---|---|
| Single story frame / reel mention | $[50–150] | campaign week, evergreen |
| Dedicated post (photo + caption) | $[150–400] | 90 days, our channels too |
| 60-second video segment | $[400–1,200] | 1 year, cut-downs for ads |
| Full video / podcast episode | $[1,200–5,000] | 1 year, excerpt rights |
| Newsletter dedicated send | $[200–1,000] | one send, one archive link |

**Rules for any paid deal:** deliverables in writing; editorial control
stays with the creator; the founding-backer credit is included at every
band; payment half on agreement, half on proof; no deal that requires
us to claim the creator endorses the product beyond what they actually
said. The campaign's whole brand is that its numbers can be checked —
the marketing has to be able to say the same.
