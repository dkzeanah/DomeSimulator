# 78. Sink the Money Into the Part That Moves

*The utility core is the one component that outlives its shell, so it is the one worth buying once: fourteen parts, five services, one penetration*

## Sink the Money Into the Part That Moves

Every dome this method builds will be outlived by one part of
itself. Put the money there.

The part is the utility core: the column through the centre
of the dome, carrying power, water, waste and air in one
object that unbolts from the building and moves to the next
one. Every other component of the dome is either cheap enough
to leave behind or bolted to a frame that will stay — the
core is the one purchase that survives its building, which
makes it the one purchase the book tells you to make properly,
once. The chapter is the core: what it is, the order it is
built in, and the accounting that turns a purchase into an
asset.

## What the core is

The column, stated as the campaign states it: fourteen parts,
five services, one penetration.

The dome's centre is the one point every radius meets, and
the column that stands there is the building's backbone for
services — the riser that carries power up from the pad, the
chase that carries water and waste down, the mounting point
for the fan, the light and the cooling that the room's
centre naturally serves. Five services, one object, and one
hole in the floor — the penetration that would otherwise be
five holes scattered through a structure whose whole
argument is closure. The column is the dome's answer to the
utility wall, and the answer is better than the question: a
wall divides a room, the column *defines* it, and the round
room's centre — the one place the furniture chapter kept
empty — is where the services already are.

The top of the column is the socket: the seal cap, the mast
of Chapter {{ch.the_mast}}, the vent, the lightning's path to
ground — the apex is an *interface*, not a roof, and the
interface is standard across every building the catalogue
sells, which is the first half of why the core moves.

## Built on a bench, proved before it stands

The build order, which is the module-build film's whole
teaching and the core's quality system: drain first, because
it is the one you cannot re-route; water second, and proved
dry before anything electrical exists; power last, into a
core already known to be dry.

The order is not caution; it is sequencing the *checks*.
A drain tested before the walls close is a drain that can be
fixed with a wrench; a drain tested after is a renovation.
Water is proved by pressure before the first wire is pulled,
because water and electricity in one column are only ever
friends in the order they were tested. And power lands last,
in a core whose wet services have already passed — which is
why the module-build chapter can say the core is *proved
before it stands*, and why the campaign sells it as the one
part the owner does not make: the part that carries five
services is the part whose failure mode is the house, and
the house is not where that test belongs.

## A purchase that becomes an asset

The accounting, and the chapter's title made into a rule.

The core is the most expensive single component of the
dome's services, and the only one that transfers: unbolt the
column, cap the pad, and the next dome's core is already
bought. A purchase that outlives its building is the
definition of an asset, and the campaign's arithmetic rests
on exactly that — the core's share of the dome's cost,
justified by the domes it will serve rather than the one it
sits in. The rule for the builder is the chapter's title:
sink the money into the part that moves. The frame is
splendid and cheap and made of trees; the core is unglamorous
and precise and made of decisions — and when the building is
eventually left behind, the core is what you carry to the
next one. Everything else in the dome is a building. The
core is the project's *continuity*, and continuity is the
one thing worth buying once.
