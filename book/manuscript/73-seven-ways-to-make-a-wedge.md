# 73. Seven Ways to Make a Wedge

*The solid split-log wedge is the reference, not the whole idea: hollow, laminated, log-derived, manufactured and hybrid members all exist, and each one trades something*

## Seven Ways to Make a Wedge

Every member in this book so far has been one solid eighth of a
log — the reference member, chosen because it needs nothing but
a saw and a tree. This chapter is the rest of the idea: the
other six ways to arrive at a triangular-section member, each
one trading something the reference member has for something it
has not.

The seven are not rivals fighting for one crown. They are a
ladder of capability, and the book's method sits at the bottom
of it on purpose — the rung that costs the least and asks the
least, and the one from which every other rung is reachable
later. A builder who starts with split logs and later wants a
factory member has not changed methods; they have climbed this
chapter's ladder, one rung at a time, and each rung is listed
here with what it buys and what it gives up.

## The solid split wedge

The reference member, stated as the baseline the other six are
measured against.

What it is good at: it exists the moment the saw finishes the
seventh cut. No shop, no glue, no kiln, no supply chain — the
tree supplies the material, the saw supplies the shape, and the
harvest advantage of Chapter {{ch.recovery}} applies in full,
because nothing is ever squared, edged or planed. It is also
the member that grades itself: bark out, pith in, the
orientation cue of Chapter {{ch.orientations}}.

What it cannot do: it cannot be bigger than the tree it came
from, straighter than the tree grew, or drier than the day
allows. Its section is whatever the butt diameter and the split
count decide, its quality is whatever the forest produced, and
its perfection is whatever the grading passes let through. The
reference wedge is the *available* member, and availability is
its entire virtue — and its entire ceiling.

Every other entry in this chapter exists to raise one part of
that ceiling, at the price of giving up another part of the
floor.

## Hollow members

Stiffness lives near the surface: in bending, the material at
the middle of a section does almost nothing, which is why a
hollow triangle can use roughly half the material for most of
the stiffness. The frame's own weight — the green pine the
campaign computed — falls by a like fraction, and what two
people can lift changes, which changes how the dome gets
built.

The argument against, which the wedge book's own page calls
decisive: you cannot make one with a saw. A hollow triangular
member is four thin pieces, ripped accurately, joined along
four long glue lines, in a jig, by somebody with a shop — the
exact work the wedge method exists to avoid. And the whole
harvest advantage disappears: a hollow member is made from
milled boards, so it starts from the mill's recovery rate, not
the split's.

Where it would make sense: a manufactured dome, in a factory,
where the jig is a fixed cost spread over hundreds of frames
and the weight saving compounds through shipping. Not for
somebody with a chainsaw and {{dome.trees}} trees.

The middle option nobody tries, noted because it is the bridge:
solid wedges with the middle bored out along the length. It
keeps the sawn faces, keeps the continuous grain at the
surface, removes some weight — and it is fiddly, it needs a
long auger, and nobody has measured what it does. Listed as an
idea rather than a recommendation.

## Laminated members

Glued strips buy what the tree refuses to sell: size beyond
the tree, straightness on demand, and defect-spreading — a
knot in one strip is surrounded by clean wood in its
neighbours, where a knot in a solid wedge is a knot in the
member.

The price is the shop, again: laminating is a glue, clamp and
press operation, it starts from milled boards, and it inherits
the mill's recovery rate and the mill's supply chain — the
whole middlemen ladder of Chapter {{ch.middlemen}}, paid back
in exchange for straightness. Lamination is the answer when
the build needs members the local woodlot cannot supply in one
piece, or when the frame must be *predictable* to a degree
rough timber cannot promise. It is not the answer for the
first dome, and the chapter says so plainly: the first dome's
job is to prove the method, and the method's proof is that it
needs nothing the tree did not already provide.

## Log-derived, manufactured, and hybrid

The middle three rungs, grouped because each one trades the
tree's own shape for a different kind of leverage.

**Log-derived.** The films' variant of the reference: buck the
log into longer sections, split each into eight, and crosscut
every wedge in the middle — two struts per section instead of
one. It is the same solid wedge, organised differently, and it
suits the sixty-foot trunk the pine film prices: five long
sections, eighty struts a tree. It is not a different member;
it is a different *schedule* for the same member, and the book
keeps both schedules visible so the two trees the films count
and the {{dome.trees}} trees this book fells can be reconciled honestly.

**Manufactured.** The wedge, moulded in a factory: screw
holes, threaded inserts and the spline ridge already in the
part, a steel core where the strength must be, a load rating
off a test rig instead of an argument. It is a better product
and a worse argument — better because consistency, grading and
repeatability arrive with the part; worse because it costs a
factory, a tooling run and a supply chain, and the whole point
of the method was that a building could be made where those
things do not exist. The manufactured member is the method's
*export* version, the one the campaign's tooling budget
exists to develop — and Chapter {{ch.stem_cell}} is where the
campaign's version of this rung lives.

**Hybrid metal and wood.** Steel where the load is a joint,
wood where the load is a span: a steel base ring under the
frame's own compression, a steel mast through the column,
steel apex and base connectors where the wood's weakest
directions meet. The book's later chapters already assume this
rung in places — the mast of Chapter {{ch.the_mast}}, the
core of Chapter {{ch.the_core}} — and the honest note is the
one those chapters carry: metal in a wooden frame is a
*decision* about thermal movement, galvanic contact and
connection detail, made with the engineer's sentence from
Chapter {{ch.forces}} in force.

## Choosing one

The decision tree that sends each builder to one of the seven,
and the two questions that do most of the sorting.

The first question: **where does the wood come from?** If it is
standing on the site, the solid wedge — log-derived or
bucked-short — is the whole ladder you need, and every other
rung is an upgrade to be bought later, if ever. If the wood
must be bought, the ladder inverts: the manufactured or
laminated member stops being an extravagance and starts being
the honest comparison against the lumberyard, with the split's
harvest advantage already forfeited.

The second question: **who is building it?** One person with a
saw and a fortnight — the reference wedge, full stop. A shop
with clamps and patience — laminated or hollow. A company
amortising a mould — manufactured, with the hybrid's steel at
the joints. The method has never been one member; it has been
the *triangle*, and the seven ways to fill one are this
chapter's inventory of how far the triangle travels before it
stops being the book's cheap, tree-shaped, saw-cut friend.
