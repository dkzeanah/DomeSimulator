# 76. Hubs, or No Hubs

*The trade this method answers, counted across the whole catalogue rather than argued: hubs buy a familiar kit and charge for it in parts, no-hubs buys the woodpile and charges in wood*

## Hubs, or No Hubs

Every geodesic dome in the world faces one decision before any
other: do the struts meet at a connector, or at each other?

The hub is the familiar answer — the machined star at every
vertex, the kit's photograph, the thing every dome catalogue
shows. The hubless answer is this book's — the pinwheel of
Chapter {{ch.pinwheel}}, where each member's butt lands on the
next member's side and the vertex is air. This chapter is the
trade, counted across the whole catalogue rather than argued,
because the two answers are not rivals: they are the same dome
sold to two different builders, and the numbers say which
builder each one is for.

## What a hub buys

A hub makes every joint the same part. One machined connector —
drilled to the frame's angles, plated, catalogued — replaces
every question about how three, five or six struts meet at a
vertex. The struts all end at the same point, each end cut to
the same pattern, each bolt in the same hole. For manufactured
struts from a factory, where every stick arrives identical and
every angle is already known, the hub is the right answer: it
is the cheapest way to join parts that were made to be joined
by it, and it makes the dome a *kit* — ship the parts, follow
the drawing, bolt the stars.

The hub also buys familiarity, which is a real asset with a
real price tag: the inspector has seen one, the appraiser's
comparable has one, the spare part exists in a catalogue. A
hubbed dome speaks the language the building world already
speaks, and for a manufactured product sold to people who will
never cut a stick, that language is worth the hub's cost.

## What a hub costs

The hub's bill arrives in three places, and the third is the
one this book exists because of.

First, the parts: a hub per vertex, plus its bolts, washers and
drilling — a real line item, paid per corner, for the life of
the build. Second, the precision: a hub assumes its struts all
arrive at the same point at the same angle, and every strut
that does not — every bowed stick, every drifted cut — fights
the hub instead of fitting it. The kit economy buys its
sameness from a factory; the hub inherits the factory's
requirements.

Third, and the decisive one for this book: the hub cannot
absorb what rough timber *is*. The frankendome's hub
experiments — Chapter {{ch.the_v_bracket_afternoon}} — were
this cost, paid in afternoons: shims, washers, longer bolts,
every joint a negotiation between a machined star and a stick
that never agreed to the drawing. The hub is perfect for
identical struts and useless the moment the struts differ —
which is exactly the moment this method begins, because the
struts are split logs, and the split log is the one member
the hub economy was never asked to join.

## What no-hubs buys and costs

The pinwheel deletes the hub by letting each member end land
on the side of the next, and it pays for the deletion in
wood — the {{edges.duplication_ratio}} members per unique edge
that Chapter {{ch.own_edge}} accounted for. That ratio is the
no-hub bill: a frame that owns every edge in its own panels
carries more timber than a frame that shares struts between
neighbours, and the extra timber is the price of never needing
a connector.

What the wood buys, in order: a joint that tolerates the
stick's own shape, a panel that can be built flat on a bench,
a seam with room for a key and a gasket, and a frame in which
every member is a member you can watch and swap — the
redundancy Chapter {{ch.small_units}} spent on grading.
The no-hub dome is not cheaper in timber; it is cheaper in
*machines*, and it is the only version a woodpile can be
built into.

## How to choose

The three questions that sort it, stated as the chapter's
whole conclusion.

Who is cutting the wood? A factory — hubs. A chainsaw —
no-hubs, and the chapter's answer is not a preference but a
consequence: the hub assumes the stick, and the saw cannot
supply the assumption.

Who is assembling it? A buyer following a drawing — hubs, the
kit's instructions are the hub's. A builder who cut the pile
and built the bench — no-hubs, the jig is the instruction.

Will the dome ever be taken apart? A kit that ships and
re-ships wants its connectors back — hubs. A frame that moves
whole on a truck, or stays where the tree stood — no-hubs,
where the only hardware is the screws and the keys, and
nothing was ever bolted to a star.

The chapter closes with the book's own position, stated the
way the breadth tables stated it: hubs are a fine building
system, the right one for most of the dome world — and this
book is the book for the rest of it, the part with {{dome.trees}} trees
and a saw, where the question was never "which connector" but
"what would it take for there to be no connector at all". The
answer is the pinwheel. The bill is the {{edges.duplication_ratio}}.
The whole account is Chapters {{ch.pinwheel}} and
{{ch.own_edge}}, and this chapter was the summary sheet.
