# 45. The Money, Both Ways

*What was spent, and what was avoided*

> **This chapter corrects something.** The $50/hour figure in the project brief, which doubled a volume ratio for safety rather than computing the section.

## The Money

The accounting chapter, and it begins by saying how it counts,
because the two numbers a build produces — what was spent and what
was avoided — are different species, and most dome books breed
them into a hybrid that flatters both parents.

This chapter counts cash out, itemised, with the dashes left
where the original build did not log a line. It counts the value
of the frame three ways, with the disagreement between the ways
printed rather than averaged away. And it corrects its own
project's most-quoted hourly figure, on the page, the way this
book corrects everything. The number that does not help the
argument is here, several of them are, because the book's
reputation was staked on printing them, and a chapter that only
argued one way would un-stake it.

## What was actually spent

The table is cash out: the saw, the fuel, the chain, the
fasteners, the gaskets, the sheeting — every line the fortnight
actually paid, with the source of each number and the honest
dashes where the original build did not keep a line.

The table's headline row is the one the whole method exists to
produce: timber, zero dollars. The structural wood cost the fuel
to cut it, and the fuel is one of the small lines above. Everything
else in the table is the *conversion* — the saw, the chain, the
screws, the keys — which is the entire point of Chapters
{{ch.middlemen}} and {{ch.recovery}} made into a receipt: the
building material was free, and the receipt is for the machine
that made it building material.

The dashes are not a gap to be filled. They are lines the
original build did not log, and this book will not fill them with
plausible numbers — the declared-constants discipline from the
front matter applies to money exactly as it applies to geometry.
A cost that was not recorded is a cost that is not claimed.

## Substitution value is not income

The most important paragraph in the chapter, and it gets its own
section because every honest number after it depends on the
distinction.

What the frame *avoids* is not money the builder *has*. The frame
bought at the national rate would cost {{value.frame_buy}}
dollars; the fortnight spends {{value.frame_cash}}; the
difference — {{value.frame_saved}} dollars — is what the build
*keeps*. (That cash figure is the film's own ledger, which
counted two trees standing; this book's build fells
{{dome.trees}}, and the third tree's stumpage — {{pine.stumpage}}
dollars a ton at the pine film's researched rate, on a tree the
size of a car — barely moves the line. The
book says so rather than silently rounding.) It is a real number, and it is not income. It buys
groceries only if the builder was going to buy the frame anyway,
and the builder who was never going to buy the frame has not
earned nine thousand dollars; they have simply not spent it.
The same distinction runs through the whole book: the strut's
value, the hour's rate, the pine's ladder rungs — all of them
are substitution values, stated as such, and the one sentence
that converts a substitution value into a lie is the sentence
that drops the word "substitution".

The useful, honest use of the number is as a *rate on time*:
{{value.frame_saved}} dollars kept, over {{value.hours}} hours of
hands-on work, is {{value.rate}} dollars an hour — the wage the
fortnight paid the builder in avoided cost. Against a
manufactured home's cheaper rate the same arithmetic gives
{{value.rate_trailer}}; against a carpenter's median
{{wage.carpenter}} dollars an hour, and a typical worker's
take-home {{wage.take_home}}, buying that frame would have cost
{{value.buy_hours}} hours of ordinary pay. Those comparisons are
the chapter's real product: the fortnight's work, priced against
the hours a wage would have needed to buy the same frame. The
cash never existed. The hours did.

## The strut, valued three ways

The table is the same arithmetic run at the strut's scale, and
it is printed with the disagreement left in, because the
disagreement is the lesson.

One strut, valued against a nominal two-by-four, against a
dressed one, and by the board foot — three methods, three
answers, and the reasons they differ are printed beside them.
The nominal comparison is flattered by the fact that a nominal
board does not exist; the dressed comparison is the honest one,
and it comes out *higher*, because a dressed board is smaller
than its name and a strut replaces more of them — exactly the
direction a sceptic should check first, and the table invites
it. The board-foot row refuses to give a single number, because
the market does not. The last row is the estimate this project
started from, kept in the table so the errata below has
something to correct against.

## The fifty dollars an hour claim

The errata, named and corrected, because this project's brief
said fifty dollars an hour and the arithmetic says otherwise.

The brief's fifty was reached by doubling a volume ratio for
safety rather than by computing the section — a shortcut that
made the number up, generously, and the films repeated it. The
computed rates are {{work.rate_nominal_usd}} dollars an hour
against nominal two-by-fours and {{work.rate_dressed_usd}}
against dressed ones — smaller numbers, arrived at by measuring
the actual cross-section and pricing the actual replacement. The
honest note, stated the way the strut table stated it: the
dressed number is the true comparison, and it is the *higher* of
the two computed rates, because the dressed board is the smaller
board. The brief's fifty overshot even that.

The correction is the model for the chapter and the book:
the claim, the error, the arithmetic that produces the smaller
number, all on the page, and the errata pages are where the rest of the
list lives. The money chapter's own verdict closes the circle: the
method's economics are strong enough to be measured properly,
and measuring properly is the only version this book will
print.
