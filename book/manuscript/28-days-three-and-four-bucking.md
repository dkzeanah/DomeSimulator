# 28. Days Three and Four: Bucking

*Cutting the trunk into the only length that matters*

## Bucking

The trees are down. Days three and four turn each bare trunk into
{{tree.sections}} sections of {{tree.section_length_ft}} feet — the
only length in the whole method, and the number that sizes everything
downstream.

Bucking looks like the simple day of the fortnight and is not,
because every section after it is the *whole* building in embryo: the
section length sets the member length, the member length sets the
dome, and a bucking error made on day three arrives on day fourteen
as a frame that will not close. The chapter's job is the one
measurement, repeated, without drift — plus the two unglamorous
subjects that decide whether the day is a one-person job: where the
sections start, and how a green six-foot round moves.

{{tree.usable_length_ft}} feet of trunk, cut into {{tree.sections}}
sections, offcut {{tree.offcut_ft}} feet. That is the whole day,
counted. The rest is doing it the same every time.

## Marking and cutting to length

The procedure, built around the one fact that makes bucking hard: the
trunk tapers, so a tape laid along the bark measures around a slope
and over bumps, and drifts.

**Mark with a stick, not a tape.** Cut one {{tree.section_length_ft}}-foot
stick — a story pole — from anything straight, and mark every section
with it. The stick does not stretch, does not creep, and reads the
same at the butt and at the top. It is the jig before the jig:
the length made physical, once, instead of re-read forty times.

**Mark a line around, not a tick.** One tick on the bark disappears
into the cut. Mark the full circumference with the saw's paint or a
lumber crayon, rolled around the trunk with a square laid flat, so
the cut is guided from any side. A crooked crosscut is a section
with sloped ends, and a sloped end is a wedge with one short face —
the error that hides until assembly.

**Cut from the top side of the bend.** The downed trunk is bent by
its own weight, exactly as the limbing chapter warned: cut from the
compression side and the kerf closes on the bar. Roll the trunk or
cut from above, and let each section's weight drop it free the moment
the cut goes through. A section that hangs by a thread of bark is a
section that will fall when it wants to; finish the cut.

**Cut in the air, not into the ground.** The bar through the trunk
and into the dirt dulls the chain in one cut. Roll a short log under
the cut line, or cut most of the way through and roll the trunk to
finish from above. The chain's sharpness budget belongs to the
ripping days, not to the ground.

**Check the count.** {{tree.sections}} sections per tree, counted
twice — once as cut, once stacked. The count is the chapter's whole
deliverable, and a missing section is a missing {{tree.sectors}}
members, which the frame will notice before you do.

## Where to start, and why not at the butt

The instinct is to start at the butt and measure up. The method says
start further in, and the reason is the taper's fault.

The butt end of a felled tree is not cylinder — it is flare. The
root swell runs up the trunk a foot or two, and a section cut from
the flare is an oval, its rings running wild, its first split a
guess about where the pith actually is. So the bucking starts where
the flare ends: stand the tape against the trunk, find where the
diameter stops falling fast, and make that the first cut. The flare
itself becomes firewood — the offcut of the plan below — and every
section after it is true round, which is what the split count
assumes.

The same rule finds the start around bends. The felling chapter left
the trunk with its kinks in it, and bucking is where the kinks come
out: cut the sections on the straight runs, leave the bent lengths
between them as firewood, and count only straight sections toward
the {{tree.sections}}. The trunk does not owe you its bends; the
plan knows they exist and pays them in firewood.

The top end, too, gets trimmed: the last section before the crown
is where the diameter is smallest and the knots thickest, and if the
crown side of it looks like a branch knot rather than a log, it
joins the firewood pile. The usable length the tree chapter measured
was never the whole trunk; it was the straight, flare-free, crown-free
run, and bucking is the day the distinction becomes a pile of rounds.

## Moving six-foot green sections alone

The unglamorous logistics, because the method's one-person claim
lives or dies on this afternoon, not on the geometry.

A {{tree.section_length_ft}}-foot section of green pine is a wet,
round, heavy thing — the butt sections near the flare can be a full
day's load on their own. The method does not pretend otherwise; it
moves them the way they want to be moved: **roll them**. A round
section rolls; the only lift is the short end-over-end to clear a
branch or a bank, and the ground does the carrying. Lay the bucking
line out so the sections can roll to the splitting station — downhill
is free, level is fine, uphill is the day's only real fight — and
the "moving" half of bucking disappears into gravity.

For the sections that must lift: never a dead lift of the whole
section. Roll it onto a low block, lever the end with a limb, or
cut it into two shorter sections and accept the split-count change —
the section length is a plan, not a law, and a back is not a
substitute for a winch. The peavey, if one is on hand, turns the
roll into a one-handed job; without it, the wedge and the lever do.

The real measure of day four is not the pile. It is whether day
five can start without lifting a single section twice — the
splitting station laid out, the sections staged where the ripping
will happen, and the fortnight's only heavy lifting behind you. The
bucking days end with {{tree.sections}} sections a tree, stacked
dry-side-up, counted, and the saw's chain still sharp for the work
that actually makes the members.
