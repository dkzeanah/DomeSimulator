# 12. Split Like a Cake

*Three passes, eight sectors, and why eight*

## Split Like a Cake

This is the chapter with the actual cut in it — the one motion the whole
method hangs on, reduced to a procedure you could hand to somebody at the
log.

The idea was introduced in Chapter {{ch.the_cut}} as a discovery. Here it
is a technique. A round section of trunk, bucked to
{{tree.section_length_ft}} feet, gets split along its length into
{{tree.sectors}} equal sectors — like a cake — by seven cuts, all of them
straight rips through the pith. Every stick that comes off is the same
shape: a {{tree.sector_angle_deg}}-degree wedge, {{member.width_in}} inches
across its bark face, {{member.depth_in}} inches deep from point to bark,
{{member.area_in2}} square inches of section. There is no squaring, no
planing, no second machine. The saw that felled the tree makes the
members.

The rest of the chapter is the order of the cuts, the reason the order
matters, and the reason it is eight and not six or twelve. The chapter is
short because the operation is. That is the point, and it is worth
remembering when the pile is knee-deep: the method's entire conversion
step is seven cuts per section, and each cut is a straight line.

## The three passes

The procedure, in order. The order is not tradition; it is what keeps the
section stable while you cut it, and a cut made out of order costs you a
pinched bar and a ruined pass.

**Pass one: halve it through the pith.** Stand the section on end or lay
it in a cradle, aim the bar down the centre of the end grain, and rip
from one end to the other. You now have two halves, each with one flat
sawn face and one round bark face. Do this pass first, every time,
because it is the only cut in the whole sequence where the section is
still round and wants to roll; once it is halved, every piece has a flat
to sit on, and everything after is stable.

**Passes two and three: quarter each half.** Lay each half on its flat
face and rip it into two quarters, again through the pith line. Four
quarters, each {{tree.sector_angle_deg}} times two degrees wide, each
with one flat and one round face still on it. The flat sits on the
cradle; the saw follows the centre line; nothing moves.

**Passes four through seven: eighths.** Each quarter gets one more rip,
from the remaining flat face through to the bark, and becomes two
eighths — the final sector. Seven cuts in total: one halving cut, two
quartering cuts, four eighth cuts. {{tree.sectors}} sticks, all the same
sector, from a section of trunk that no longer exists.

Two things make the sequence forgiving rather than fussy. First, every
cut after the first is made on a piece that already has a flat face to
sit on, so the workholding is the piece itself. Second, you are never
measuring an angle: the cut list is "through the middle", repeated, and
the tree supplied the symmetry. A sector is {{tree.sector_angle_deg}}
degrees because a circle divided in half three times is; nobody held a
protractor to it.

## Three cuts, eight sticks

The drawing shows the sequence end-on: one circle, then two halves, then
four quarters, then eight sectors, with every cut a diameter. Read it as
a production plan rather than a diagram, because the sequence is what
makes the work fast.

The first observation is that every pass doubles the number of pieces —
one becomes two becomes four becomes {{tree.sectors}}. The second is
that the work per piece falls as the pieces multiply: the halving cut is
the whole width of the log, the quartering cuts are half that each, the
eighth cuts a quarter of the log's width each. The third is that the
sequence never asks a decision. Nothing in it requires choosing where the
next stick comes from or how thick it should be; the geometry decided all
of that when the log was round. A procedure with no decisions is a
procedure you can repeat forty times without drifting, and a procedure
that doubles its output per pass is a procedure whose rhythm pays you as
you go.

Chapter {{ch.days_five_to_eight_ripping}} puts the sequence on a clock —
{{work.struts_per_hour}} sticks an hour, sustained, once the cradle and
the rhythm are settled — and Chapter {{ch.jig_reference}} shows
what happens to the pile after. Here the sequence stands alone, because
it is the one thing in the method that cannot be substituted: no mill,
no planer, no router table will produce this member, because the member
*is* the cut.

## One log, two, four, eight

The production hierarchy deserves its own paragraph, because it is the
part of the method that looks obvious and is quietly load-bearing.

One log becomes two halves becomes four quarters becomes
{{tree.sectors}} members. Every major cut doubles the piece count, which
means the whole tree converts in three layers of work: one cut at the
top of the tree, two in the middle, four at the bottom. That shape —
wide at the bottom, narrow at the top — is exactly what one person with
one saw can exploit. The slow cuts are few and wide; the fast cuts are
many and shallow. And because the pieces at each layer are identical to
their siblings, the workholding at each layer is one cradle, one stop,
one posture, repeated.

This is also the shape a guide rail, a cradle or a splitting fixture
would be built around, if you ever stop doing it freehand — the geometry
already knows where every cut goes, so a fixture that knows it too is
just the sequence, made of steel. The book keeps the hand version,
because the hand version is the whole point: the method exists so that a
person with a chainsaw and {{dome.trees}} trees needs nothing else.

## Why eight and not six or twelve

The question gets asked, usually at the first split pile: why eight?
The answer has three parts, and each one is a hard constraint.

**The sector angle has to leave the seam key workable.** A sector is
{{tree.sector_angle_deg}} degrees, and the seam between two panels folds
by {{seam.fold_a_deg}} to {{seam.fold_b_deg}} degrees depending on where
it sits — Chapter {{ch.seams}} prints the full schedule. The gap the key
has to fill is the difference between the sector angle and the fold, and
at eight splits that difference is a real, makable, compressible thing.
Split the log into six and the sector is sixty degrees, the key becomes
a structural wedge in its own right, and the seam starts deciding the
geometry instead of the other way round. Split it into twelve and the
sector is thirty degrees — a sliver, hard to handle and hard to keep
straight over six feet.

**A sixth is too fat to handle alone.** Six sectors from a
{{tree.butt_diameter_in}}-inch butt are heavy, wide pieces with a
sixty-degree point; they are awkward to cradle, awkward to carry, and
awkward to fasten. Eight lands the member at {{member.width_in}} inches
across and {{member.area_in2}} square inches of section — the size a
single person moves, lifts and sets all day, which is the constraint
Chapter {{ch.days_three_and_four_bucking}} keeps coming back to.

**Eight is the halving count.** Two, four, eight — the sequence above
only needs cuts "through the middle", never a measured angle. Six needs
a sixty-degree first cut, held by eye or by fixture; twelve needs a
thirty-degree refinement. Eight is the finest split you can reach with
nothing but halving, and halving is the only cut a saw can aim by
symmetry alone.

choice is visible rather than asserted.

## What other split counts give you

The table is the arithmetic of the choice: six, eight, ten, twelve,
each with its sector width, depth and section. Eight is the reference
row, and the reason is not that eight is magic — it is that eight is the
row where the key fits, the stick is carryable, and the cut list is
still "halve it". Every other row trades one of those three away, and
the table makes you look at which one.

One more note for the pile, because it will come up: the split count is
a *build decision*, set before the first section is opened, and it
travels. The member width it produces sets the pinwheel inset, which
sets the cut list, which sets the jig. So the decision is made once,
from this table, and every number downstream follows. Change it later
and you are not adjusting a cut; you are starting the frame again. So
the table gets its own page, and the choice gets its own warning: read
the row, then commit.
