# 19. Bends, Knots and Small Units

*Why six-foot pieces forgive what sixteen-foot boards do not*

## Small Units

Every chapter so far has argued from what the method saves — the mill,
the haul, the middlemen. This chapter argues from what it *tolerates*,
which is the quieter advantage and, for a builder working with whatever
grows, the more useful one.

The method's smallest unit is {{tree.section_length_ft}} feet: a
bucked section, then a wedge, then a member. That smallness changes
what counts as a defect. In a stick-frame economy, a bend or a knot
ruins a sixteen-foot board and gets charged to the whole load. Here, a
bend costs you a section, a knot costs you a stick, and the pile
around them is untouched. The method does not need straight trees; it
needs short straight *runs*, and a forest full of trees that are
"nearly straight for six feet" is a forest full of building material
nobody else will pay for.

That is the whole chapter: the defect economy of small units. It is
why the method can grade its own stock by eye, why it can build from
storm debris and clearing waste, and why the wood it uses never has to
travel further than the ground it fell on. Chapter {{ch.middlemen}}
counted the chain the wood avoids. This chapter counts the *wood* the
chain avoids — the crooked, knotty, low-grade trees that never get on
the truck in the first place.

## A bend costs you a section, not a tree

Take the crookedest trunk you have — the one a mill would wave off the
landing without pricing. The method looks at it differently.

A bend does not make a tree useless. It makes the tree into two
straight logs with a kink between them. Buck the trunk on either side
of the bend — cut the kink out as firewood — and what remains is two
sections of straight-ish timber, each long enough to split into
{{tree.sectors}} members. The bend cost you a foot of trunk and a
minute of saw time. In a mill's accounting the same bend costs the
whole log its grade, because a mill needs its boards straight over
their full length, and a kink in the middle of a sixteen-foot board
is a board with no market.

The reason is the unit size. A mill's product is long boards; a bend
anywhere in the length ruins the board. This method's product is a
{{tree.section_length_ft}}-foot member; a bend only ruins the member
it falls inside. Cut around it and keep cutting. A trunk with three
bends yields four good sections and three short firewood rounds, and
all four sections are full-value frame stock. The same tree, sawn,
would have produced a pile of shorts worth the haul to the dump.

And because the frame's members are short, they can be *short of*
straight in a way long boards cannot. A member with a slow bow still
sits in the jig, still lands its butt on its neighbour's side, still
closes the panel — the pinwheel's overlap takes up what the eye can
see, and the panel comes out flat because the jig, not the stick,
decides where the corners are. Chapter {{ch.the_jig}} is that sentence
built in wood.

## Knots, and where they are allowed

The second defect economy rule: a knot is not one problem, it is two,
and only one of them matters here.

**A knot mid-span is a stress riser.** In a beam — a joist, a rafter —
a knot in the middle of the span is where the bending stress
concentrates, and a big knot there can decide the board's grade by
itself. Even in this frame, a knot in the middle of a member is the
worst place for it, because a compression member buckles at its
weakest section, and the weakest section is the one with the knot.
So the mid-span rule still applies: grade the pile, and keep the
heaviest knots out of the middle third of compression members.

**A knot at a bearing face is a joint problem.** Where one member's
butt lands on the next member's side, the load spreads across the
whole contact patch — {{force.bearing_in2}} square inches of it, as
Chapter {{ch.forces}} measured. A small knot inside that patch is a
dent in the bearing, not a hinge; the load goes around it through the
wood beside it. A knot *at* the contact line, on the other hand, can
split the face the screws are pulling against. So the bearing rule:
sort the knotty ends away from the butt, and let the head end carry
them — the head end gets sawn off anyway, and Chapter
{{ch.head_overfit}} makes that offcut free.

The sorting takes an afternoon and a crayon, and it is the difference
between "the pile has knots" and "the pile has a plan". The next
section gives the procedure.

## One bent trunk, salvaged

The drawing shows one crooked trunk twice. On the left, the mill's
view: a log that will be rejected or down-graded, because no board
can be sawn straight out of it. On the right, this method's view: the
same trunk bucked on either side of its bends into straight sections,
each section split into eighths, the kinks themselves stacked as
firewood.

The two pictures are the same tree. The difference is not in the
wood — it is in what each system demands of a length of wood. The mill
demands sixteen straight feet, because its product is a board of
sixteen straight feet. The method demands {{tree.section_length_ft}}
usable feet at a time, because its product is a member of
{{tree.section_length_ft}}. The tree that fails the first demand and
passes the second is not a worse tree. It is the same tree, measured
against a different product — and the trees that fail the mill's
demand are exactly the trees that are free.

## Grade your own stock

The procedure, because "sort the pile" is advice until it is a list.

**One: the bend pass.** Walk the pile and mark any member with a
visible bow over its length. A slow bow — under a thumb's width over
six feet — goes in the general pile; the jig will hold it straight.
A hard bow goes to blocking, bracing and stakes, where a curve costs
nothing.

**Two: the knot pass.** Mark the middle third of every member as it
comes off the split, and reject to the blocking pile any member with
a knot cluster in that third. A single small knot mid-span stays; a
knot cluster does not.

**Three: the bearing pass.** Look at the butt end of every member —
the end that will land on a neighbour's side. A knot within a hand's
width of the butt face sends the member to the other orientation:
flip it, so the knotty end becomes the head end and the clean end
becomes the butt. Only if both ends are knotty does the member leave
the frame pile.

**Four: the pith pass.** A member that split along the pith — a crack
running down its point — goes to blocking. It will keep splitting
under load, and a member with a crack in it has stopped being wood you
can trust.

The passes take an afternoon for the whole pile, and they are the
entire quality system. No kiln, no grader, no certificate — four
passes, a crayon, and the same eyes that will build with the wood. The
frame's redundancy does the rest: {{frame.panels}} panels mean a
suspect member is a member you can watch, and a watched member that
moves is a member you can swap, which is the honest engineering behind
"grade your own stock".

## Trees nobody else wants

The economic consequence of tolerating variation: the pool of building
material widens, and the price of the widened pool is nothing.

Storm debris. Clearing waste. The windfall of Chapter
{{ch.the_tree_that_was_already_down}}. Low-value sawlogs no mill will
pay haulage on. A tree too bent or too short for a mill is
{{tree.sectors}} good sticks a section here, and it is often already
lying on the site. Which removes the haul, the mill fee and the
markup, and replaces them with fuel and bar oil — the ledger Chapter
{{ch.money}} closes to the dollar.

The point is not that crooked trees are as good as straight ones. The
point is that this method's unit is small enough that *a usable
straight run* is the requirement, not *a straight tree*, and the
world is full of usable straight runs inside trees nobody wants. A
builder who can use them builds from the one timber supply whose
price never rises: the wood that is already on the ground where the
building goes.

## And the journey it does not take

Close the chapter where Chapter {{ch.middlemen}} opened, because the
two arguments are the same one seen from different ends.

The industrial route moves wood through {{route.mill_steps}}
operations and a chain of hands: forest, logger, mill, kiln,
distributor, retailer, site. This method's route is
{{route.wedge_steps}}: tree, cutting station, drying stack, jig. Every
removed stage is fuel, time and a margin — and every removed stage is
also a *filter*, because each middleman selects for the wood worth
hauling and grading and selling. The crooked tree never survives the
first filter. The bend costs it its ride.

The method's answer is to build where the wood is, from what the
filters rejected, in units small enough that the rejection never
happens. The chain argument was about money. This one is about
material — the same {{route.wedge_steps}} steps, counted as distance
instead of cost, and the same conclusion: the best wood is the wood
that never had to travel, and the method is the only route that can
use it.
