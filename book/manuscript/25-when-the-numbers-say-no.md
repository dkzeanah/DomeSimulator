# 25. When the Numbers Say No

*Reading a result that means do not build this*

## When the Numbers Say No

The two sizing methods of the previous chapters end in numbers, and
sometimes the numbers say no. This chapter is the permission to listen
to them, and the procedure for what to do after.

A book about a build method is at its most useful when it tells you
when *not* to build — before the tree is down, before the pile is
split, before the jig is built. The failures this method can produce
are not dramatic; they are quiet, and they arrive as arithmetic. Two
trees that give one hundred and twelve struts when the frame needs one
hundred and twenty. A member too heavy for the person who has to lift
it. A stick too slender to trust. Each one is a sentence the numbers
say, and each one has a standard answer — sometimes a smaller dome,
sometimes a fourth tree, sometimes a different bucking length, and
sometimes, honestly, not this build at all.

The chapter is short and it is howto, because the decision it teaches
is the one that saves the most: reading a bad result as information
instead of as a setback, while the fix is still cheap.

## Not enough struts

The classic case, and the one the title of this book invites.
{{dome.trees}}
trees, bucked and split, give {{tree.struts_per_tree}} struts each —
{{dome.struts_available}} in all, against a frame that needs
{{frame.members}}. The arithmetic works out with {{dome.spare_struts}}
to spare. But run the same method on trees that are shorter, thinner
or bucked longer than this book's, and the count shrinks: {{dome.trees}} trees
can come out short of the frame's count, and then the method
says no.

Read the shortfall as a measurement of how far off the trees are, not
as a verdict on the plan. The frame's count — {{frame.members}} — is
fixed by the geometry; it does not negotiate. The trees' count is the
variable, and it is a product of three choices: the usable length you
bucked, the section length you chose, and the number of trees you
felled. Any one of the three can be changed, and the section below —
"Three ways out" — runs the arithmetic for each. The one answer the
chapter forbids is the silent one: one hundred and twelve struts do not
become one hundred and twenty by cutting more carefully. The count is
not a quality issue; it is a quantity issue, and only a quantity fixes
it.

The spare margin deserves one honest note, because it is easy to read
wrong. This book's {{dome.spare_struts}} spare struts are not
generosity; they are the rejection allowance for the grading passes of
Chapter {{ch.small_units}} — the bent, the knotty, the pith-split that
leave the pile. A build planned with zero spares is a build that
assumes every stick passes, and no pile does. So the real threshold is
not "one hundred and twenty struts"; it is "one hundred and twenty,
plus the rejection rate your own pile actually showed you". If two
trees give you exactly one hundred and twenty, the numbers are not
saying yes. They are saying *tight*, and tight is a no with a schedule.

## Too big to lift alone

The second way the numbers say no has nothing to do with counts. A
{{tree.section_length_ft}}-foot green wedge is heavy — the book's own
members run {{member.area_in2}} square inches of wet pine — and the
whole method is built around one person carrying, setting and
fastening every piece alone. A dome sized by the tree can produce
members past the point one person handles, and then the numbers say no
in your back instead of in the ledger.

The honest test is not the table, it is the afternoon: pick up the
heaviest member the design produces, green and wet, and carry it the
distance the build will ask. If it can be carried, set and held with
one hand while the other drives a screw, the design passes. If it
cannot, the fix is not a stronger builder — it is a shorter bucking
length, which makes every member lighter, or a second pair of hands,
which Chapter {{ch.building_with_other_people}} treats as its own
design change. The arithmetic follows the carry: shorter sections
mean more sections, more splits, and the same frame from lighter
pieces — and the tree count re-solves with the new section length,
which is the "shorter bucking" row of the section below.

The limit that matters here is the one Part Two formalises. Chapter
{{ch.flat_rate}} names the declared longest member one person will
handle — {{flat.solo_member_ft}} feet — and shows what diameter it
reaches, because the flat-rate argument this book makes is bounded by
exactly this physical ceiling. The sizing methods do not know about
your back. You do, and the numbers wait for you to tell them.

## Stock too small in section

The third no, and the one that hides inside the first two: the trees
are long enough and the count works out, but the sticks are slender —
a small butt diameter, split eight ways, yields a sector too thin to
trust.

The check is against the comparison Chapter {{ch.versus_board}} runs:
a wedge from a very small log can fall below the dressed board it is
supposed to replace, and then the method's central claim quietly
inverts. The crossover is real and it is diameter-dependent — below
some butt size, the wedge is the wrong answer and the honest move is
to buy the boards. The chapter on sections prints the comparison; here
the procedure is the thing: compute your own tree's sector area before
committing, compare it against the dressed two-by-four's
{{board.dressed_in2}} square inches, and if your tree loses the
comparison, the numbers are saying no to *this tree*, not to the
method.

The options are the same three, with a twist. Shorter bucking does
not fatten a stick; it only shortens one. An extra tree adds count, not
section. The real fixes for a slender section are a thicker tree, a
coarser split — six sectors instead of eight, the row Chapter
{{ch.split}} priced — or a different member entirely, which is what
Chapter {{ch.wedge_variations}} is for: the hollow, laminated and
manufactured wedges exist precisely because some trees say no.

## Three ways out

The three standard exits, with the arithmetic each one runs, so a
"no" from the numbers always lands on a next step.

**Shorter bucking.** Cut the sections shorter and every member gets
lighter and shorter; the dome the tree sizes shrinks accordingly, and
the strut count per tree rises. Run Method B again with the new
section length — Chapter {{ch.method_b}} is a procedure, and a
procedure re-runs. The cost is a smaller floor and more sections to
split; the gain is a build one person can carry.

**A fourth tree.** Add a tree and the count problem disappears — four
trees of this book's size give far more than {{frame.members}} — while
the member weight and section stay exactly as designed. The cost is
one more fell and one more buck, and Chapter {{ch.money}} shows what
an extra tree
actually adds in fuel and hours: not nothing, and rarely the thing
that kills the build.

**A smaller dome.** Solve Method A downward — Chapter {{ch.method_a}}
runs in either direction — until the tree you have is the tree the
dome needs. The floor shrinks with the radius, and the shrink is
where the honesty of this chapter lives: a dome you can actually
build is better than the dome whose numbers said no.

And the fourth option, stated because a howto chapter owes it: **do
not build this.** Not every tree, not every site, not every back
should become a dome. The method's own arithmetic will say so when it
is true, and the builder who hears it has lost an afternoon of sums
instead of a fortnight of felling. The numbers saying no are the
cheapest form of the no there is.
