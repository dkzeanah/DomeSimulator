# 31. Days Ten to Twelve: Forty Panels

*The same twelve motions, forty times*

## Forty Panels

Days ten through twelve are the production run the jig was built for:
{{frame.panels}} panels, one after another, on one bench, from the
pile of wedges the ripping days left.

The run is where the method's promise becomes arithmetic. One panel
is twelve motions — load, locate, butt, head, drill, screw, flush,
out — and forty panels of twelve motions is a production line with
one worker, one station, and no waiting. The days go wrong only when
the motions stop being the same: a stick sorted into the wrong
class, a marking that washed off in the rain, a panel started before
the jig was checked. This chapter is the procedure, the sorting, and
the two honest notes that make the run real — the fourth panel is
the first good one, and the variant count is smaller than the pile
makes you fear.

## One panel, start to finish

The twelve motions, in order, as they will run forty times. Chapter
{{ch.jig_reference}} names the same stages from the simulator's side;
this is the version your hands do.

**Check the bench.** Stops tight, cut planes clean, clamps where the
hand expects them. Thirty seconds, every morning, before the first
panel — the jig's truth is the day's truth.

**Sort the three sticks.** One long member and one short for the
sides, one more of whichever the triangle asks, drawn from the
marked piles of the sorting pass below. The sticks arrive at the
bench already decided; the bench never decides.

And for an isosceles panel, *which* side each stick goes on is fixed:
**the longest — {{cut.master_long_in}} inches — is the base, the middle
length ({{cut.master_mid_in}}) is the left side, and the shortest
({{cut.master_short_in}}) is the right.** Lay the base nearest you and the
left member is the longer of the two: it reaches over to the top point,
and the short right side runs up to meet it — and it is that short side the
base butts up to. An equilateral panel is three
long members and has no such rule to remember, which is most of why the
run is quick: {{dm.equi_count}} of the panels are the easy kind, and the
{{dm.iso_count}} isosceles ones carry the one instruction that must not be
swapped. At the bench the two sides look almost identical — the
difference is two inches over five feet — so the rule is applied by
picking from the marked piles in order, never by eye.

**Load the first side.** Butt against the butt stop, sawn faces down
on the bench, head running long past the head fence. The stop does
the locating; the hand only presses.

**Butt-cut if it is due.** Some classes carry their butt angle from
the saw; some are cut at the bench. Either way the cut is made
against the plane the bench holds, never freehand. Chapter
{{ch.butt_cut}} is that cut in full.

**Load the second and third sides.** Each lands against its own
stops, butts to butts, and the triangle assembles itself under the
clamps — three sticks, three overlaps, the pinwheel of Chapter
{{ch.pinwheel}} appearing without anybody arranging it.

**Drill and screw the overlaps.** Through the outer stick into the
inner, at the spread the connector chapter established, pulling the
overlap closed. The screws are the only fastening in the whole
panel, and the jig's holes — or the fence's positions — make them
land the same on panel forty as on panel one.

**Flush the heads.** The three heads, still long, are sawn off
against the head fence: one plane, three cuts, and the panel is
exactly the triangle the bench describes. Chapter {{ch.head_overfit}}
is why the heads were long in the first place; here it is simply the
last motion of the panel.

**Lift it out.** The panel comes off the bench as one finished
thing — {{frame.members_per_panel}} members, their own edges, ready
to be checked, marked and stacked. That is the whole panel. Twelve
motions, measured in minutes once the rhythm is in.

## Sorting the pile

The run only stays a run if the pile does not get to decide
anything at the bench, so the sorting pass happens once, before the
production days, with a marking scheme that survives the weather.

**Sort by class, not by stick.** The four member classes of Chapter
{{ch.two_lengths_not_forty}} each get their own stack, marked at the
cut — a crayon code on the sawn face, one stripe per class, one
colour per tree. The bench pulls from stacks; it never chooses
between sticks.

**Mark the faces, not the ends.** The ends get cut off. The sawn
face is where the mark lives through every operation that follows,
and it is the face that lies *down* on the bench, so the mark also
reads as an orientation cue: mark up, bark up, wrong way round is
visible before the clamp closes.

**Mark with what survives rain.** Lumber crayon, not pencil.
Pencil washes out of wet pine in one night; the crayon is the
marking scheme's entire weatherproofing, and the fortnight has
already had one rain.

**Run the grading passes as you sort.** The four passes of Chapter
{{ch.small_units}} — bend, knot, bearing, pith — are the same walk
as the sorting pass, and doing them together means every stick
reaches its stack already judged. The {{dome.spare_struts}} spares
the tree chapter promised exist as the reject pile, and the reject
pile is the margin the run spends when a stick misbehaves at the
bench.

## The fourth panel is the first good one

The honest note, stated early so nobody panics on day ten.

The first panel of the run is a rehearsal. The motions are new, the
clamps are in the wrong places, and the result is a panel you
would not hang a roof on. The second panel is faster and nearly
right. The third finds the rhythm and the fourth is the first panel
that comes off the bench exactly the way the fortieth will — and
the run's real output is not forty panels, it is the thirty-seven
that follow the learning curve.

The book says this plainly because the curve is the plan, not a
failure of it. The method's whole claim is that the motions repeat,
and repetition is what turns the first three panels from mistakes
into tuition. The rejected first panels are not waste; they are the
cheapest practice the build will ever get, and they end their lives
as the jig's own test pieces — the panels you cut into, shim, and
check the saw against, so the bench's truth is probed by the very
thing it built.

## Panel variants

The table closes the chapter with the count that keeps the run
sane: how many genuinely different panels the dome contains.

Not forty. The frame's {{frame.panels}} panels are two shapes —
{{dm.equi_count}} equilateral, {{dm.iso_count}} isosceles, the
counts of Chapter {{ch.forty_panels_one_hundred_and_twenty_members}}
— and each shape is built from the same four member classes, so the
run is two panel variants in a repeating pattern, not forty
originals. The table is generated from the solved dome, and its job
at the bench is the same as every table in this book: the pile looks
like chaos, the count says otherwise, and the count is right.

By the evening of day twelve the run is done: {{frame.panels}}
panels, stacked by variant, the reject pile spent, the bench
quiet. The fortnight has two days left, and they are the days the
photographs are taken on — the raising, where forty flat triangles
become the building the next chapter describes.
