# 6. Triangles Do Not Care What Shape Your Stick Is

*Why a triangulated frame can accept a member no mill would sell*

## Triangles Do Not Care

This chapter is the permission slip for the whole method, so it goes first
in the explanation strand and it is short enough to be unfair to nobody.

Start with the sentence the title page made its argument out of: **your
skull is not symmetrical either, and it protects the brain just fine.**
The frame is the part of this building you are allowed to get wrong — the
lumpy, split, self-labelled frame that the frankendome proved will stand.
The parts that must be right are the skin and the ground, and the book
treats those with the care the title page promised. This chapter is the
*reason* the frame is allowed to be lumpy, stated as geometry rather than
as reassurance.

The wedge method proposes to build a building's frame out of sticks that are
not rectangular — a fifth of a round log, sawn faces, bark back, a point at
one edge. No lumberyard sells that. No building code assumes it. And yet the
frame stands, because of one property of triangles that is easy to state and
takes the rest of this chapter to spend:

**A triangle does not care what shape its sticks are. It only cares how long
they are, and that they reach each other.**

Everything the mill does — square the edges, flatten the faces, dress the
section to a standard — exists because rectangles ask their pieces to be
beams: flat boards, loaded across their faces, whose stiffness and strength
depend on the exact shape of the section. A triangulated frame asks
something else entirely of its pieces. It asks them to be *struts*: pushed
or pulled along their own length, ends only, never sideways, never flat.
For a strut, what matters is how much wood is in the section and how far the
load is from pith to bark — not whether the section is a rectangle.

So the central question of the book is not "is a wedge as good as a 2x4?"
in general — Chapter {{ch.versus_board}} runs that comparison honestly and
prints the column the wedge loses. The right question is narrower and much
more useful: *inside a triangulated frame, where members are loaded along
their length, is a wedge a good member?* This chapter builds the frame that
question assumes, so the later chapters can answer it.

## Axial, not bending

The difference between a strut and a beam is the difference between the two
ways a building can load a piece of wood, and most of conventional framing
is the second way.

**A beam is loaded across its length.** A floor joist carries the floor on
its narrow face; a rafter carries the roof on its wide one; a stud carries
weight down its length but braces against sideways push. In all of them the
load is trying to bend the piece, and bending is cruel: the material at the
middle of the section does almost nothing, which is why an I-beam has its
steel at the top and bottom and air in between. Bending strength lives in
the extreme fibres, far from the section's centre, so the shape of the
section — rectangle, and which way round — decides how strong the piece is.
This is why a 2x4 on edge is dramatically stiffer than the same 2x4 laid
flat, and why the mill bothers to dress boards to a true rectangle at all.
A rectangular building is a system for turning wood into beams, and beams
need rectangles.

**A strut is loaded along its length.** Squeeze the ends of a stick toward
each other, or pull them apart, and every fibre of the section shares the
load equally. Shape stops mattering; the amount of wood matters. A round
stick, a square stick, a wedge — for axial load, they differ only in
section area (and, for a long thin strut in compression, in the way the
section resists buckling, where depth matters more than shape).

Now look at a triangulated frame. Every load that arrives at a joint is
carried to the next joint along the members, as push or pull along their
lengths. The frame does not bend its members; it moves force through them.
That is the entire trick of geodesic structures, and it is why a dome's
struts can be slender for their span, and why the frame stands up without a
single piece of sheet material keeping it square. A rectangle needs a
diagonal, or a sheet of plywood, or it racks over and lies down. A triangle
is already its own diagonal. The drawing on the next page is that sentence.

## The same load, two frames

Look at the two frames side by side.

On the left, a rectangle. Push on its top corner the way wind pushes on a
wall, and the rectangle does what rectangles do: the corners stop being
ninety degrees, the frame leans, and unless a sheet of plywood or a diagonal
brace is holding it, it goes over. The joints are doing everything the
shape cannot. That is why a conventional wall is a frame *plus* sheathing —
the studs are beams, and the sheet is the triangle they never built.

On the right, the same corner, triangulated. Push on it. The load cannot
change the angles, because the angles are fixed by the lengths of the three
sticks, and a stick's length cannot change without stretching or squashing
the stick itself. So the push travels down the members as pure compression
and pure tension, along the grain, exactly where wood is strongest and
exactly where shape does not matter. Nothing bends. Nothing needs a sheet.
The frame is its own bracing, at every corner, forever.

The whole method follows from that picture. Because members are loaded
axially, the frame will accept a member of any section that has enough area
and enough depth — which is precisely what a split log provides. Because
nothing bends, the frame does not ask for the flat, dressed, graded faces a
beam economy demands. And because the triangle's angles are fixed by its
lengths, the frame enforces its own geometry: get the lengths right and the
shape has no choice. The frankendome stood on that fact alone, with no two
members matching, and it stands on the same fact with every member
identical. Chapter {{ch.the_frankendome}} was the demonstration. This
chapter is the reason.

## What the wedge is good at

If the frame wants area and depth, the wedge should be measured by area and
depth — and on a modest log, it beats the store-bought board at both.

A dressed two-by-four carries {{board.dressed_in2}} square inches of wood,
planed down from its nominal size — a rectangle, because it must be a beam. The wedge this book cuts is {{member.width_in}} inches
across its bark face and {{member.depth_in}} inches from its point to its
bark, for {{member.area_in2}} square inches of section. The comparison that
matters is in Chapter {{ch.versus_board}}, computed properly on an
{{versus.diameter_in}}-inch log so the wedge gets no advantage from a fat
tree. Here, two qualitative points are enough to set the direction:

**Depth is where stiffness lives.** In bending, stiffness rises with the
*cube* of depth; double the depth and a beam gets eight times stiffer.
A wedge runs from the pith to the bark — the full radius of the log. For
the same width of stick, that is more depth than any board a mill cuts
from the same tree, because the mill's rectangle has to fit inside the
circle, and the wedge does not.

**Area is where axial strength lives.** The wedge is a sector: one eighth
of the log's circle. Measured as a strut — which is what the frame asks it
to be — it carries more wood in its section than the dressed board the
same log would have produced, because it never gave up the corners to the
edger and the planer. Chapter {{ch.recovery}} counts those corners; the
count is the difference between {{tree.recovery_pct}} per cent and
{{tree.sawn_recovery_pct}} per cent of the tree.

The honest note, saved for the full table: a wedge is not a better *beam*.
Lay it flat and ask it to be a shelf and the missing rectangle matters.
The claim is narrower and it is the only one the book makes — in this
frame, loaded this way, the wedge is a better use of the tree than the
board, and the shape the tree already had is not a defect the mill forgot
to remove. It is the feature the frame was waiting for.

## One eighth of a tree

The section, stated plainly, because the next chapters argue with it:

A {{tree.section_length_ft}}-foot section of this book's tree, split into
{{tree.sectors}} equal sectors, yields sticks of {{member.area_in2}} square
inches each — {{member.width_in}} inches across the bark face,
{{member.depth_in}} inches deep from point to bark.

The dressed two-by-four the same tree would have been sold as carries
{{board.dressed_in2}} square inches. The nominal one — the name on the
ticket, which no longer exists in the wood — carries {{board.nominal_in2}}.

So the wedge is a bigger section than the board the mill would have cut
from the same log, in the one measure that matters for a strut, and it
cost a chainsaw's fuel instead of a mill, a kiln, a planer and a yard.
That single line — {{member.area_in2}} against {{board.dressed_in2}},
from the same tree — is the economic engine of the whole book, and
everything after this page is the machinery that turns it into a standing
frame.
