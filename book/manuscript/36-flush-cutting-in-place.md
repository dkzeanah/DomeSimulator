# 36. Flush-Cutting in Place

*The cut that makes the panel true*

## Flush-Cutting

The flush cut is the final operation on the jig and the moment the
panel becomes itself: the three overfit heads of Chapter
{{ch.head_overfit}}, sawn off against the fence, in one plane, so
the assembled triangle ends up exactly the triangle the bench
describes.

It is a small cut and the book gives it a chapter anyway, because
the order of operations it completes is the method's entire
tolerance argument, and because the cut is the one place a hurried
builder can undo, in thirty seconds, the half-inch of slack the
whole design paid for. The chapter has two sections: how the cut is
made, and why it cannot be made any earlier — the second being the
reason the first is written at all.

## Against the fence

The procedure, with the saw held the way it has to be held —
against the fence, never freehand.

**The panel stays clamped.** The flush cut is made on the
assembled, screwed, clamped panel — the panel does not come off the
bench for the cut, because the bench is what makes the cut true.
The clamps hold the members against their stops; the saw rides the
fence; the two truths together are the cut.

**The bar rides the plane, flat.** The saw is laid on the fence's
cut plane, bar flat to the bench, and drawn through the three
heads in one motion where the fence allows it. The saw does not
sight anything; it follows the plane the bench already holds,
which is the only way a hand-held saw makes a square cut at all.

**Cut all three heads on the same plane.** The three heads stick
past the fence by different amounts — the panel is a triangle, and
its overfits land where they land — but the *cut line* is one
plane, and all three cuts are made to it. That single plane is the
panel's final edge geometry, and a panel whose three heads were
cut to three planes is a panel whose neighbours will measure the
difference.

**Do not finish the stick, finish the panel.** The temptation at
the saw is to clean each head as it is cut — a shave here, a
touch-up there. Resist it: the flush cut's product is the panel's
truth, not three pretty ends. The offcuts go to the pile, the
panel goes to the stack, and the next panel takes its place. The
cut exists once per panel, not once per stick, and treating it as
a production step is what keeps the run of Chapter
{{ch.days_ten_to_twelve_forty_panels}} at its pace.

## Why this cannot be done first

The order is not arbitrary, and the reason is the whole tolerance
argument in miniature.

The flush cut cannot be made before assembly, because before
assembly there is nothing for the cut to be true *to*. A member cut
to its final length at the split is a member cut against a tape's
idea of where its neighbours will be — and its neighbours, when
they arrive, will be wherever the wood actually is. The panel is
the only object that knows the member's true length, and the panel
only exists after the butts are located, the overlaps screwed and
the clamps closed. So the cut must come last: it is the final
measurement, and the fixture performs it.

It cannot be made *after* the panel leaves the bench, either —
off the jig, the panel's own edges are the only reference the saw
has, and a saw referenced to the thing it is cutting is a saw
cutting in circles. The bench's fence is the fixed truth; the
panel's edges are the variable; and the order that works is the
one where the fixed truth cuts the variable.

So the sequence stands as the book has built it: split long, butt
against the stop, assemble, flush against the fence. Cut long,
locate, assemble, cut true. The flush cut is the last word of the
sentence, and the sentence is the method — every member of every
panel final-cut in place, against the only truth that could know
it.
