# The Composer

*The tool that furnishes a dome: a placement rule rather than a drawing, a cast that obeys its own rules, and what the shell refuses*

The chapters on the round room described what it is like to live inside a
dome: headroom falls as you walk out from the middle, so what fits where is a
function of where it stands. The project has a program that does that
reasoning, and it furnishes the building piece by piece.

This section is about how it decides. It is in the back matter rather than in
the book because it is machinery rather than method — but it belongs in the
book at all because it is the one place where the shape's real cost to a
furnished home is stated as arithmetic instead of as an impression.

## A placement rule, not a drawing

Nothing in the catalogue carries a list of legal places to stand. A sofa does
not know that it fits here and not there, and no file in the project says
where a bookshelf is allowed. Instead, every piece carries a footprint and a
height, and the program checks both against the shell before it will place it:

*Walk the piece's footprint outward from where it wants to stand, and find the
height of the shell above every point of it. If any part of the piece is
taller than the shell at that point, it does not fit.*

The piece being carried is drawn in one colour where it fits and another where
it does not, and the colour is a *measurement*, not a warning animation. A
reader who has read the round-room chapter has already done this by eye at a
window; the composer does it for forty pieces of furniture at once, and the
answer is always the same answer geometry would give: the closer to the wall,
the lower the ceiling, and therefore the shorter the thing that can stand
there.

Two consequences that are worth carrying back into the building:

**A fitting pod stops the editor.** A two point two metre fitting pod — a
shower, a wardrobe, a changing room — has nearly eight metres of headroom over
it at the middle of a thirteen-metre dome, and walked outward the shell comes
down to meet it. There is a line on the floor past which the pod cannot go, and
the program finds where it is rather than approximating it. That is the round
room's real argument in one tool: the perimeter of a dome is not small floor,
it is *low* floor.

**Nothing is placed twice.** Each placed piece is remembered by what it is and
where it stands, so a composition can be walked, turned, dropped, re-walked,
and the whole arrangement remains a list of named objects on a solved floor —
which is also how the plans in the round-room chapter are drawn.

## The cast, and the rules it obeys

The people in the films are drawn by the same program, and they arrive with
rules.

There are eight of them, each with her own skin tone, hair colour, hair style
and stance, and the loader refuses a cast in which two share a tone or a hair
style: the code raises rather than quietly drawing a room of near-identical
people. That is a small rule with a large effect on what the films look like,
and it is the sort of rule this project has applied elsewhere for the same
reason — the fixture that refuses a wrongly turned wedge, the marking scheme
that makes a backwards panel announce itself. A rule enforced by the tool is
worth a rule remembered by a person.

Hair is geometry rather than colour. Each strand leaves the scalp along its
growth direction, hands over to gravity about a head's radius later, and
carries a wave or a helix by style — which means a haircut is a set of
parameters rather than a texture, and the same head can be drawn wet, tied
back or cut short without redrawing anything. In a project whose whole subject
is members that are given their shape by geometry, a haircut is not an odd
thing to find in the back matter.

## Landmarks, not measurements

Clothing in the composer is a list, not a model. An outfit is a bodice, a
skirt, a sleeve, a heel — each piece lofted through the *body's* own
cross-section, so a garment cannot come out the wrong size for the person
wearing it: it is generated from her, in her dimensions.

And the lengths are landmarks rather than numbers. Hair is "mid-thigh",
"knee", "waist", "floor". A hem is "above the knee". Because those are places
on a body rather than measurements in inches, one wardrobe serves a cast of
any height, and a garment does what a real one does: it falls to a place
rather than to a figure.

There is a fifth mode with no garment at all: the silhouette in skin tone
only, like a shop's dress form. It exists to show what the clothes are doing —
a lofted ellipse carries no anatomy, so the form mode is the honest answer to
"what does this outfit actually add". Every catalogue in this book has a bare
version for the same reason. The stem cell is the form mode of a building.

## What the dome refuses

The set the composer works in is a thirteen-metre dome, warmer and lower than
the reference articles, furnished rather than fitted out: a sofa whose seat
height comes from the seated pose in the same way a garment's hem comes from a
landmark, and a stove whose flue wants the crown, because that is where a
dome's heat already is.

Walking that plan outward is an education. The middle of a dome is a room with
more headroom than any house — and the edge of it is a place where a person
cannot stand up, all the way around, in a ring whose width does not change
with the size of the building. That ring is the cheapest floor in the building
and it is also unusable floor until something is done about it, which is what
the chapter on the pony wall priced: lift the shell on a short wall and the
dead ring becomes furniture height, storage height, worktop height.

The composer and the pony wall are the same conclusion reached from two
directions. One is a rule that refuses to place a wardrobe, the other is a
course of studs that stops the refusal being necessary. A building this shape
asks that question once, in every room, and the answer is always the same
three options: accept the low ring, lift the shell, or put the bed in the
middle and the storage round the edge.

Which is what the composer is for. Not to draw a room, but to make the
building's own geometry answer for what can be put in it.
