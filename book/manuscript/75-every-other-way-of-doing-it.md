# 75. Every Other Way of Doing It

*Eight frame materials, eight strut sections, sixteen panel types, nine claddings and eleven foundations -- all priced on this dome, so the choice is a comparison instead of a preference*

## Every Other Way of Doing It

This book argues for one combination of material, section,
panel, cladding and foundation — the combination Chapters
{{ch.split}} through {{ch.closing_the_shell}} built. This
chapter is the counter-weight: all the others, priced on the
same dome, from the wedge book's own catalogues, so the
book's choice is a comparison instead of a preference.

The five tables below are the breadth of the whole geodesic
world, compressed onto one page: eight frame materials from
whole-tree trunks to structural steel, eight strut sections
from round tube to the quarter wedge, sixteen panel types from
open frames to precast concrete, nine claddings from nothing
to a poured shell, eleven foundations from gravel to a
treehouse platform. Every option is costed on the reference
build's own quantities — the same member count, the same
surface, the same span — which is the only way two options
can honestly be compared at all.

## The breadth tables

The page is the argument. The reference row — the solid split
wedge, bark out — is not the cheapest row in every table and
the book does not pretend it is. Whole-tree trunks are cheaper
as material; gravel is cheaper as a foundation; an open frame
is cheaper as a panel. What the reference combination wins is
the *combination*: the only row across all five tables that
needs no shop, no mill, no second trade and no supply chain —
which is the one property the tables cannot print but the
chapter can state, and it is the property the whole method was
built to buy.

## What the prices say

The pattern across all five catalogues, read once and stated
plainly.

The industrial rows — steel, aluminium, precast — win on
strength and lose on *access*: their prices assume a buyer who
can order them, a truck that can deliver them, and a building
that needs what they sell. The natural rows — trunks, split
wedges, bark — lose on uniformity and win on exactly what the
industrial rows cannot buy: they are already on the site, they
cost what the fuel costs, and they ask for no permission. The
prices are not a ranking of quality; they are a ranking of
*position*, and the builder's position decides which row is
right before any preference does.

## When a losing row is the right row

The chapter's closing honesty, because a table of options is
useless if it cannot crown its losers.

Every option in these tables is somebody's right answer. A
foundation for frost, a steel frame for a permanent public
structure, a concrete shell for a bunker, a treehouse platform
for a site with no ground to spare — the rows lose on this
dome and win on theirs, and the table exists so that choice
is made with the numbers up, the way this book makes every
choice. The reference combination is the book's answer to
*{{dome.trees}} trees, one saw, forty hours*. The tables are the same
question, asked eleven ways, with the whole catalogue
priced — and the honest close: the book's way is not the only
way, it is the way the numbers say fits the person this book
was written for, and every other row is here so that
sentence could be checked.
