# 41. Doors, Windows and the Rim

*Cutting holes in something that works by being closed*

## Doors and Windows

Every building needs holes, and a dome is the building that resents
them most: its strength is its closure, its panels are its
structure, and a hole cut wrong does not just let the weather in —
it un-triangulates the frame at the exact place the frame was
leaning on itself.

So this chapter is the craft of opening a dome without breaking
it. The principle, stated once and used throughout, is the one the
wedge book's own chapter taught: **an opening is a panel you did
not close** — take a whole panel out and the frame loses nothing
structural, because the panel was always its own independent unit.
Cut *into* a panel, and the triangle stops being a triangle, and
the load that was travelling through it now has nowhere to go. The
chapter runs the openings in order of mercy: the door at the rim,
the window in a panel, the risers that cheat height — and the
drawing that shows all three without a single un-triangulated
triangle.

## A door at the rim

The easy case, and the book says so plainly: the door belongs at
the rim, because the rim is the one place a hole costs the frame
nothing.

The base ring's {{frame.rim_edges}} edges join nothing — Chapter
{{ch.forty_panels_one_hundred_and_twenty_members}} separated them
from the seams for exactly this reason. Leave one rim panel out
entirely, and the gap is a doorway: the panels on either side are
complete, their seams close as they always did, and the dome loses
a panel that was never structural in the way the crown panels are.
The door is framed the way the missing panel was framed — two
jambs running the panel's full height, a header across its top
edge — and the frame's own triangle does the lintel's work,
because the triangle above the door is still a triangle.

The one requirement is the header's honesty: the panels above the
door lean their weight down through the ring, and the opening
interrupts the ring. The header takes that interruption — a beam
across the top of the opening, tied to the jambs, carrying the
ring's continuity across the gap. In a full-height rim door this
header is small, because the ring is low and the loads above it
are modest; in a taller opening the engineer's sentence from
Chapter {{ch.forces}} reappears, and rightly. The rule for the
chapter: take the whole panel, frame the gap, header the ring.

## A window in a panel

The harder case, and the craft is keeping the triangle a triangle.

A window wants light where the wall leans — mid-panel, high on
the dome — and a hole in the middle of a triangle is exactly what
the frame's logic forbids: the triangle's load runs around its
edges, and a hole in its middle interrupts the members that were
carrying it. So the window is not cut into a panel; it *replaces*
part of a panel, and the part it replaces is re-triangulated
around the hole.

The procedure is the panel's own logic, applied smaller: frame
the window opening inside the panel with members that run from
the window's corners to the panel's edges — a smaller triangle
above the window, two beside it, one below — so every region of
the panel is still triangulated, and the panel's loads still
travel along members, now detouring around the glass. The window
unit itself is fixed into its own sub-frame, flashed to the
panel's skin, and the panel remains one independently-built,
independently-lifted thing — the property Chapter
{{ch.own_edge}} paid for, spent here on daylight.

The honest cost, said aloud: a windowed panel is a panel built
twice — once as the triangle, again as the triangle-with-a-hole
— and its member count rises accordingly. That is why the
windows go high and few, where the glazing earns its keep in a
leaning wall and the view, rather than everywhere the floor
plan's habit would put them.

## Three openings that work

The drawing collects the chapter: the rim door, the windowed
panel, and the risers option of the next section — three ways to
open the dome, each one keeping the frame's triangulation intact.

Read the drawing against the rule it illustrates. The door takes
a whole panel and frames the gap; the window sub-frames inside a
panel and re-triangulates around the glass; the risers lift the
whole shell and put the openings in a *straight* wall, where
doors and windows stop being dome problems at all. Three openings,
three strategies, one invariant — no hole ever interrupts a load
path without a smaller triangle being drawn around it.

## Risers: cheating height at the rim

The third strategy is not an opening but an avoidance: raise the
whole dome onto a short vertical wall — a riser, a knee wall, a
stem wall — and the openings stop fighting the curve.

A riser is a straight cylinder of wall under the hemisphere's
brim: knee-high for a shed door, waist-high for furniture to
lean against, full-height where the building wants ordinary
windows and an ordinary door. The dome above it is unchanged —
the same frame, the same panels, the same seam schedule — and the
wall below it is ordinary stud framing, which is the entire
point: the expensive part of the building keeps its shape, and
the parts that want rectangles get rectangles.

What the riser costs is the ring beam's reappearance: the dome's
base ring now sits on a wall instead of on the ground, the wall
needs a top plate to receive it, and the wall's own top must be
as round as the ring it carries — the same layout discipline as
Chapter {{ch.the_footing_and_the_ring}}, moved up one storey.
What it buys, Chapter {{ch.pony_wall}} prices in full: usable
floor at the edge, headroom where the dome's wall leans hardest,
and the cheapest square footage in the building, bought by the
foot of wall height.

The book's order of operations closes the chapter: frame, shell,
then openings — and if the openings want a straight wall, the
riser is the answer, because it is cheaper to lift a whole dome
than to argue with forty curved triangles one window at a time.
