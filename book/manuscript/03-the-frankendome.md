# 3. The Frankendome

*Forty triangles, no two struts alike, and the failure that taught the method*

## The Frankendome

Before the dome this book is about, there was another dome, and it matters
that it existed, because every decision in the method that followed was a
reaction to what that first one got wrong.

It was built from whatever wood was lying around — offcuts, salvaged sticks,
mixed sections, boards that had been other things. Forty triangles, joined
edge to edge, each strut whatever shape the pile happened to supply. The
idea was deliberately forgiving: do not sort, do not select, do not machine.
Let the connector absorb the difference between one stick and the next.

The idea worked, which is the worst thing that can happen to an idea that is
secretly bad. The frame stood. It stood for six months, outside, through
weather, and it taught more about how a dome behaves than any drawing had.
And it was awful: lumpy, twisting, no two joints alike, and impossible to
reproduce. Every lesson in this book is downstream of that specific
combination — *it worked, and it was awful* — because it proved both halves
of the method at once. A triangulated frame will stand on almost any
consistent-ish sticks, which is the permission. And nothing about a frame
built that way repeats, which is the trap.

This chapter is the story of the trap.

## Strut-agnostic, and what that cost

The design rule of the first build was *strut-agnostic*: no stick is special,
no stick is measured, any piece of wood of roughly the right length can be
any member. Assemble the triangles from the top of the dome down, one at a
time, and wherever two edges meet, a folded bracket joins them. Eight screws
a bracket. Two bolts a seam. Tighten, move on.

The appeal was real. The pile was free. Nothing needed grading. A stick that
was two inches wide where the next was three could still be a strut, because
the bracket's flaps could be bent to whatever the wood wanted, and the screws
found the wood where the wood was. Building that way feels like freedom —
every decision made at the moment of assembly, by the piece in your hand —
and it took the whole build to learn what that freedom cost.

It cost everything that comes after a build. The dome stood, but:

**Nothing repeated.** No two members were alike, so no operation could be
jigged. Every cut, every hole, every bracket bend was a one-off, made to
fit the exact stick in hand. The fortieth panel took as much thinking as the
first.

**The connector did all the work.** Because the sticks agreed about nothing,
the bracket had to absorb every difference: width, thickness, angle,
wander. That is a lot to ask of a folded piece of metal, in forty places,
for the life of the building — and Chapter {{ch.the_v_bracket_afternoon}}
is the afternoon I spent learning exactly how much.

**It could not be drawn.** There was no cut list, because there was no
standard member. The frame existed only as itself; you could not hand the
build to anyone, and you could not build a second one.

**It was never finished.** A frame that is improvised joint by joint is a
frame you keep adjusting. It stood, but it never stopped being the thing
you had to keep an eye on.

The photograph on the next page is that dome. It is in this book because the
book's method is best understood as the answer to it, and because it is
honest to show the before. A book about a better way to build owes the
reader the worse way it is better than.

## The frankendome standing

Look at it. Lumpy where a solved dome is smooth. Every member its own
section, its own bow, its own idea of straight. The seams pinch and gap
where the sticks disagreed. And yet the shape is recognisably a dome, and it
is standing, which is the part that is easy to forget when the tidy version
of this story gets told: the thing did work. A frame of forty triangles,
joined any-old-how, will carry its own weight and a skin. Triangulation is
that forgiving — Chapter {{ch.triangles}} explains why — and the method in
this book inherits that forgiveness as its foundation.

What the photograph cannot show is the labour inside it. Ten days to stand
it, not because the work was hard but because none of it repeated. Every
bracket was folded to a different angle. Every screw row was laid out fresh.
The second dome took as long as the first would have, because there was no
first dome there was only a sequence of one-offs.

The wedge method exists because of what this picture makes obvious: the
geometry of a dome does not care what shape its sticks are, but the person
building it absolutely does, and the difference between a frame you build
once and a method you can build with is whether anything in it repeats.

## The ten-piece pentagon

One part of the frankendome was genuinely good, and it is worth separating
it from the rest, because it was the only piece carried forward.

The base — the ring where the dome meets the ground — was not improvised.
It was ten pieces, cut to a plan, joined into a decagon, and it went together
in an afternoon and never moved. The contrast with the chaos above it was
the whole lesson of the build in miniature: the one part that was drawn
before it was cut, the one part where every piece had a name and a number,
was the one part that behaved.

That decagon became the footing of the method. The frame this book builds
also meets the ground as a ten-sided ring — {{frame.rim_edges}} rim edges,
each a member in its own right — and the ring is the one place the book
tells you to set out with a string and a check rather than a shrug. Chapter
{{ch.the_footing_and_the_ring}} is that ring, given the treatment the
frankendome proved it deserved.

The pentagon, too, came forward in spirit. The frankendome's triangles were
joined edge to edge in whatever arrangement the assembly order produced; the
method's panels are assembled into the same two triangle shapes every dome
has — the same count, the same shapes, the same corners. What changed is
not the geometry. It is that in the method, every panel of a given shape is
built on the same fixture, from sticks of the same section, with the same
holes. The frankendome proved the shape. The method made the shape
repeatable.

## Why I stopped

The frankendome was not abandoned because it fell down. It was abandoned
because of a sentence I could not unthink, after watching the tenth improvised
joint go in:

**A thing you build twice is only worth doing by hand once.**

Nothing in that dome repeated, so nothing could be jigged, so nothing could
get faster, and nothing could get more accurate, and no second dome could
ever be the same as the first. The strut-agnostic idea had removed the need
for precision at the cost of removing the possibility of it. Every bracket
was a small negotiation between two sticks that had never met and never
would again. That is not a build method. It is a sculpture process that
happens to produce a room.

The question the wedge method answers is the question the frankendome left
on the table: what would the sticks have to look like for one fixture to
build every panel? The answer turned out to be in the next chapter — not
*which sticks to buy*, but *which way to open the log*. And the moment that
answer landed, the frankendome stopped being a failed building and became
what it is in this book: the control group. Same dome. Different pile.

Chapter {{ch.the_cut}} is where the pile changed.
