# 39. The Footing and the Ring

*What the dome stands on*

## The Footing

The frame is up — hanging, in this book's build, between the trees
— and the question of what it stands on, if it ever stands, is the
one the fortnight deliberately left open: the ground work comes
last, and it may never come at all. This chapter is the footing for
the day it does — the part of the building under everything, the
part the photographs never show, and the part where the book's own
warning from Chapter {{ch.where_error_goes}} is most literally true:
the ring's error accumulates, and the footing is the ring's floor.

A dome's weight arrives at the ground along one circle — the base
ring's {{frame.rim_edges}} edges, ten points where the panels meet
the earth. The footing's whole job is to receive that circle and
hold it: level, round, and against the wind that wants to lift the
whole thing away. The chapter covers the two ways to do it, the
layout that keeps the circle true, the uplift a dome invites, and
the point where the book stops and the engineer starts.

## Ten points, or one ring

Two approaches carry a dome's weight to the ground, and the book
recommends one of them for its own build while pricing both.

**Ten points.** Each of the ring's ten base vertices sits on its
own small footing — a pad block, a pier, a driven post — and the
frame bridges between them. It is the cheapest material answer, it
works where the ground is firm and level, and it leaves the middle
of the circle empty — no concrete where nothing stands. Its cost is
precision: ten separate points must land in one plane and one
circle, or the ring that spans them comes out twisted, and Chapter
{{ch.where_error_goes}} has already explained what a twisted ring
does to everything above it.

**One ring.** A continuous footing under the whole base ring — a
ring beam of concrete, or a wooden ring on blocks — receives the
dome's weight as one circle and spreads it as one circle. It costs
more material than ten points, and it is *forgiving* in exactly the
way ten points are not: the ring beam carries the frame's local
imperfections around the circle instead of exposing them at every
vertex. The book's own build stands on the continuous answer,
because the build's error budget — the chapter just spent on it —
is worth more than the concrete it saves.

The honest note, kept from the dome books that skip it: a dome
puts its whole weight on the *outside* of its circle. Pouring a
slab across the middle is concrete nothing stands on — the centre
of the floor carries no frame load at all, and the money it costs
is money the ring beam could have used.

## Setting out a circle accurately

The layout, because the footing's truth is the ring's truth, and
the ring's truth is the whole frame's.

**One stake, one string.** The dome's centre goes in first: a
stake driven where the middle of the floor will be, and it never
moves again for the rest of the build — every measurement of the
footing, the ring and the raising is taken from it. Then a string
the length of the dome's radius, a loop at each end, and the
circle is drawn the way circles have been drawn since before
anybody wrote this down: walk the string around the stake, marking
as you go.

**Set the ten points from the circle.** The ring's ten base
vertices sit on the circle at equal spacing, found by stepping a
second string — the chord between neighbouring vertices — around
the circumference and adjusting until the last step lands exactly
on the first. The step-and-adjust is the check: a ten-point layout
that closes on itself with the last step is round; one that misses
its starting mark by an inch is telling you where the circle
isn't.

**Check the circle before anything is poured.** Measure across
every opposite pair of points — the diameters — and demand they
agree. Then check the level of the ten points against a water
level or a laser, and shim until they share one plane. The checks
are the chapter's whole discipline: a footing that is round and
level on the day it is made is the only thing in this part of the
build that cannot be fixed later, so it is checked the way things
that cannot be fixed later are checked — twice, from different
directions, before the concrete is mixed.

## Anchoring against uplift

The sentence the footing chapter must say plainly, because the
shape itself refuses to let it be skipped: **a dome is a wing.**

A hemisphere is a lifting body. Wind over the crown does to the
dome what it does to an aircraft: pressure drops on the curved top,
and the whole building feels a pull toward the sky. The frame's
own weight resists — green pine is heavy, and the skin chapters
add their share — but a dome that is only *sitting* on its footing
is a dome the right wind can pick up by the brim, and the stories
of domes that went rolling are not folklore. The footing's job is
therefore two jobs: hold the weight *down*, and hold the building
*down against the wind*.

The anchor is the ring's connection to the ground, and it is not
optional. Every base vertex — or the ring beam at intervals —
needs a mechanical tie into the footing: a cast-in anchor, a
bracket, a strap over the ring member. The tie's job is uplift,
not weight, and it is sized for the worst wind the site's records
hold, which is exactly the calculation the last section of this
chapter hands to somebody else. The book's own build used straps
at every base point, and the straps are the one part of the
footing the book will not let a reader improvise.

## This is where to ask somebody

The safety page, and it is shorter than the chapter because the
book's rule from the front matter simply applies here with full
force.

Footings are load and soil, and neither is a matter of carpentry.
The dome's weight, the wind's uplift, the ground's bearing
capacity, the frost line, the drainage — every one of them is a
site-specific number that belongs to someone who can read the site
and the code together. This book tells you the *shape* of the
footing — a circle, not a disc; anchored, not sitting; checked,
not assumed. It does not tell you how deep, how wide, or how much
steel, because those answers come from your ground, not from a
book that has never seen it.

The division of labour is the one Chapter {{ch.forces}} drew for
loads, applied to the earth: the geometry is the book's, the
engineering is the engineer's, and the boundary between them is a
page you cannot miss. Build the circle the book describes. Have
the circle's ground signed off by someone who can. Then, and only
then, stand the frame on it.

And remember the order the title page promised: the hanging cabin
never owed this chapter anything. The foundation is the last
decision of the build, made when the cabin finally comes down from
the trees — and the chapters that follow this one are the reason
the decision could wait.
