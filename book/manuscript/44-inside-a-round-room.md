# 44. Inside a Round Room

*Living with curves*

## Inside a Round Room

This is the chapter about living in the thing, written the way the
rest of the book is written: the numbers first, the compromises
named, and the romance earned rather than assumed.

A round room is not a rectangular room with curved wallpaper. It
changes where you can stand, how furniture fits, how sound
behaves, and where the light falls — and the changes are
measurable. This chapter measures them: the honest floor, the
standing-height map, the furniture problem and its real
solutions, and the acoustic quirk a hemisphere brings for free.
It is the chapter to read *before* building, because the fixes it
describes are layout decisions that cost nothing on paper and a
renovation once the walls are up.

## Where the usable floor really is

The honest number, because the floor a dome advertises and the
floor a person can use are different things.

The dome's plan area — {{dome.floor_sqft}} square feet for this
build — counts the whole circle. But the shell meets the floor at
a tangent: the wall leans inward from the rim, and the last ring
of floor around the edge has almost no headroom over it. The
usable floor is the smaller circle where the ceiling is over
your head, and the difference between the two numbers is the
dome's real estate tax — floor you paid for, in frame and skin,
that only a dog can use.

The map on the next page draws the honest version: the full
circle, the standing-height contour inside it, and the dead ring
between. The dead ring is not waste in the moral sense — it is
where low storage, benches and beds belong, the furniture that
does not need to stand up — but it is real, and the chapter
states it because the alternative is the surprise every dome
builder watches a visitor have: they step in, they look up, and
they say the roof is lower than the picture suggested. The
picture suggested the plan area. The roof was always the
geometry.

The fix the book already spent a chapter on is the riser of
Chapter {{ch.pony_wall}}: lift the shell a few feet and the
tangent moves outward, the dead ring shrinks, and the floor
becomes genuinely usable to the wall. The round-room chapter
recommends it for exactly this reason — not for looks, but
because the standing-height map improves more per dollar than
anything else in the whole fit-out.

## Standing height, mapped

The map is the chapter's central object: a plan of the
{{dome.floor_sqft}}-square-foot floor, coloured by headroom,
with the standing-height contour drawn where the ceiling passes
head height.

Read the map as a furniture plan before it is a chart. The
central disc is the room: full height, {{dome.height_ft}} feet at
the crown, the space where a person stands, works, cooks. The
ring between the contour and the rim is the compromise zone:
overhead clearance shrinking smoothly to nothing at the wall,
exactly the profile a bed, a bench, a desk-with-a-view or a row
of shelves wants. And the rim itself is the dead zone the
previous section named — reachable, useful, not standable.

The map also settles the oldest argument in dome living: where
the bed goes. The answer is where the map says — in the ring,
head toward the wall, because the only dimension a sleeping
person needs at the wall is the one the dome has plenty of:
length. The furniture section follows the same logic for
everything else.

## Furniture, storage and the wall that curves away

The practical answers, including the ones that are honestly just
compromises, because a chapter that only offers custom joinery
is writing for a budget this book does not assume.

**The curve costs a wedge of dead air.** Any straight-backed
piece against a curved wall leaves a tapering gap behind it —
the round-room tax of Chapter {{ch.dome_costs}}, paid here in
inches. The cheap answers: pull furniture off the wall entirely
and let the gap be a cable run and a shadow line; angle the
piece so one edge touches and the gap opens somewhere
unreachable; or accept the gap and fill it with the things that
are soft. The honest answer for the pieces that matter is
built-in storage on the curve — the one place custom work pays
for itself, because it converts the dead ring into drawers.

**Low is the ring's whole vocabulary.** Beds, benches, window
seats, shoe racks, the wood stove's hearth — everything that
does not need headroom lives in the ring, and the centre of the
room stays open, which is how a round room ends up feeling
larger than its number: the walls are furnished, the middle is
air.

**The middle wants to be the table.** A round room's centre is
its tallest, brightest, most reachable point, and the instinct
to put the table there is the geometry's instinct, not a style
choice. Everything radiates from it; every path through the
room crosses it; and the light from the crown falls exactly on
it. Furnish the ring, leave the centre, put the table in the
middle — the round room's layout writes itself once the map is
accepted.

## Acoustics, and the whispering problem

The last section is the quirk nobody expects and everybody
notices in the first week: a hemisphere focuses sound.

A dome is half of a whispering gallery. Sound from one side of
the room runs up the curved wall and comes back down at the
centre — the geometry that lets a whisper cross a cathedral —
and in a house-sized dome the effect is not magical, it is
*tiring*: conversation pools at the middle of the room, a
kitchen tap at the rim is audible everywhere, and the building
has no quiet corner, because corners are where quiet lives and
the dome has none.

The fixes are the same ones theatres use, scaled down. Soft
surfaces break the focus: the quilted layers of Chapter
{{ch.shell_ladder}} do double duty as acoustic treatment, rugs
and fabric kill the floor's reflection, and the furniture in the
ring breaks up the wall's curve the way baffles do. The one
thing not to do is nothing — an empty dome is an echo chamber,
and the echo arrives before the furniture does.

The chapter closes with the round room's honest summary: a
floor that is smaller than its number, a wall that leans, a
centre that rings — and a room that, furnished the way the map
dictates, is the calmest, most enveloping space most people
will ever sleep in. The curves are not the problem. The curves
are the room, and this chapter is the manual for living inside
them.
