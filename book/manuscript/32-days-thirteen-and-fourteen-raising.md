# 32. Days Thirteen and Fourteen: Raising

*Forty panels become one building*

## Raising

The last two days of the fortnight are the ones the photographs are
taken on, and the ones the book is most careful about, because
raising is where a frame that was built flat, checked flat and
trusted flat suddenly has to stand in three dimensions — and where
every error the earlier chapters let through comes home at once.

The job: {{frame.panels}} finished panels become one standing
hemisphere, in an order that lets one person do it, held up by
temporary props until the shell closes and holds itself — and then,
in this book's build, winched off the ground entirely: three cables
from the apex hanger to saddles on the trees, the brake winch doing
the hoisting, and the cabin hanging where the trees hold it, with
no footing under it at all. The order
matters more than the effort. A dome assembled in the wrong order
needs a crane and a crew; assembled bottom ring first, course by
course, it needs one person, a ladder, props and a winch — the same economy
the whole method has been spending since the tree.

The chapter ends with the honest inventory of what a hanging frame
is not, because day fourteen feels like a finish line and the book
has four more parts to go.

## Bottom ring first

The assembly order, which is the whole secret of a one-person
raising.

**Set the ring.** The ten rim panels go down first, laid out flat on
level blocks — or on the footing of Chapter
{{ch.the_footing_and_the_ring}}, for a build that stays on the
ground; this book's build uses blocks, because its ground work
comes last. The ring is the only part of the frame that
touches the ground, and it is the one part that must be right
before anything rises off it — its {{frame.rim_edges}} edges set
the circle, and the circle sets everything above. Chapter
{{ch.where_error_goes}} names the ring as the one place error can
accumulate, and the check is done here, at the start, when a wrong
ring is still a morning's fix: measure the ring across its widest
points, both ways, and do not build up until the two numbers agree.

**Course by course.** The next ring of panels sits on the first,
each new panel's base edge meeting the top edge of the panels below
it — the seam, the key, the screws, the same joint the bench built,
now vertical. Work around the dome, not up one side of it: a dome
grows in levels, and a level that closes before the next begins is
the prop that holds the next level. The order is not optional; a
half-raised dome is a sail, and the wind has opinions.

**Key and screw as you go.** Every seam gets its key before the
neighbouring panel arrives — a seam closed later is a seam closed
from a ladder, reaching into a gap that has already tightened. The
keys are the schedule of Chapter {{ch.seams}}, and the schedule is
followed in place, panel by panel, the count climbing toward
{{frame.seams}}.

**Check the ring, again.** At every course, one measurement across
the open top tells you whether the dome is coming up round or
egg-shaped. The check costs a minute and it is the difference
between a raising and a rescue; the dome that fails it today gets
propped today, not discovered tomorrow.

## Propping, and what holds it up before it is closed

The photographs show the finished frame. The raising itself is a
building held up by sticks, and the props are the chapter nobody
writes.

A dome does not carry itself until the top closes. Until the last
panel ties the structure together, every course above the ring is
leaning its weight on the course below it, and the courses below are
leaning on props — lengths of scrap set against the inside, butted
to the ground, holding the shell at its current height. The props
are the temporary structure the method admits it needs: not
scaffolding, not a crew, just the same wood the frame is made of,
pressed into service as furniture.

The prop rule is simple and absolute: **a panel goes up, a prop
goes under it, and a prop never comes out until the course above it
has closed.** The dome rises prop by prop, and the props travel —
pulled from the lower course once the ring above it is screwed
closed, carried up, reset. It looks like chaos in the middle and it
is not: every panel in a properly propped raising has a load path
to the ground at every moment, which is exactly what the finished
frame will have, temporarily, in wood.

The one danger the props exist to prevent is the panel that leans
out. A panel set at the wrong angle, or a course started before the
course below it closed, will try to fall *outward* — and a falling
panel takes its neighbours' seams with it, opening the dome like a
flower in reverse. The prop is the answer and the order is the
prevention; together they make the raising a sequence of small,
held, stable moments instead of one long gamble.

## Closing the top

The last panel is the hardest, and it is worth saying why before
the raising reaches it.

Every seam closed so far was closed against air — the next panel
could be offered up, adjusted, persuaded. The last panel closes
against a *hole*: every one of its three edges lands on a seam whose
other side is already fixed, and the panel must fit the hole the
whole dome has been building toward for two days. If the ring was
set right, the courses keyed right and the schedule followed, the
hole is exactly the panel — the method has been aiming at this
moment since the first butt cut. If anything drifted, the drift
lives here, in the last triangle of sky.

The order of the last panel's edges matters more than its screws.
Offer the panel up gently, key the lowest seam first, then one side,
and let the last seam close under the panel's own weight — a final
seam that needs forcing is the dome telling you where the error
went, and the answer is never more force; it is a shim behind a
key, or a prop relieved, or, honestly, a panel taken down and the
neighbour adjusted. The method's tolerance budget — Chapter
{{ch.where_error_goes}} — was built for exactly this conversation,
and the conversation is short: the frame closes, or it does not,
and it almost always closes.

Then the screws go in, the props come out, and the frame stands on
nothing but its own shape — until the winch takes it.

## The winch, and the trees

This book's build does not leave the frame on the blocks. It hangs
it, which is the step the title page promised and this section
reports.

Three cables run from the apex hanger — the mast's lifting ring, or
a temporary sling through the crown on a first build — to saddles
on two of the three trees: two legs to one tree, one to the other,
because a tripod of cables on two anchors is what keeps a hanging
load from spinning. The brake winch does the hoisting, one slow
notch at a time, the frame lifting off its props the way a boat
lifts off its trailer, and the moment the props fall free the cabin
hangs — the trees holding the whole weight of it, the rim swinging
a few feet above the ground, and the builder standing under
something that yesterday was a pile of wedges.

Say it plainly, the way the floating-dome chapter does: this is a
tested construction and a design possibility, not an engineered
rating. The trees, the saddles, the cables, the mast and the winch
all carry real loads, and none of those ratings is in this book —
the engineer's sentence from Chapter {{ch.forces}} binds here
harder than anywhere else in the build. The saddles are saddles,
not holes: straps that spread the load over bark, so the trees
carry the cabin the way they carried their own crowns.

What hanging buys is the deferral the book has been promising: the
ground is now *available* — no footing, no ring beam, no pad is
owed, because the cabin does not touch the earth. The floor clamps
to the mast when it is bought (Chapter {{ch.the_mast}}), the skin
goes on in the air or on a lowered frame, and the foundation, when
it comes, is the last decision of all (Chapter
{{ch.the_footing_and_the_ring}}). The winch did not just raise the
cabin. It un-scheduled the ground.

## Standing

The plate is day fourteen: {{frame.members}} members, {{dome.trees}} trees, a
fortnight — the frame this book has been describing since the first
chapter, standing where the tree once stood, before the winch took
it up between the other two.

Look at it for what the book wants you to see. No hubs. No mitred
corners. Forty panels that each remember the bench. The seams
running in their two angles, the keys seated, the rim on the
blocks. It is the same object the simulator solved before the first
tree was felled, and the fact that a solved model and a fortnight's
labour can produce the same standing thing — and then hang it —
is the method's whole
argument, in one picture.

And the next section is the book's own correction of this
photograph, because the photograph lies in the way all finished
things lie.

## What was not done

The honest inventory, written while the frame is still warm from
the raising, because day fourteen feels like an end and is not one.

The hanging frame is **not a shell**. It has no skin, no weather
barrier, no insulation — the seams are open to the sky's weather,
and the first real rain will find the book's weakest chapters and
test them. Part Seven — Chapters {{ch.closing_the_shell}} through
{{ch.heat_power_and_the_small_systems}} — is the work that turns
this photograph into shelter, and it is a season's work, not a
fortnight's. The skin can go on in the air or on a lowered frame;
the order is the builder's, which is the point.

It is **not a home**. No floor but the air under it, no door but
the gap where a panel is missing, no power, no water, no heat. The
frame is
the part of the building the fortnight was sized for; the rest of
the building is sized in months, and the book's later parts are
honest about that calendar.

And it is **not finished** — it is *standing*, or rather *hanging*,
which is the
fortnight's actual deliverable and no more. The dome will move as
its green wood dries, the keys will settle, and the frame the
builder checks on day thirty is not quite the frame of day fourteen.
Chapter {{ch.what_broke}} is the record of that settling, kept the
way this book keeps everything: named, measured, and left in.

So the fortnight ends the way it began — with an honest sentence.
{{dome.trees}} trees, one saw, fourteen days, {{hr.with_error}} hands-on
hours: the frame is up, and up between the trees. The rest of the
book is what the frame makes possible, and the first thing it makes
possible is the next chapter, where the method's reference pages
begin.
