# 81. The Seam That Does Four Jobs

*The gap between two panels is not a joint waiting to leak. It is a channel: gutter, vent, dehumidifier and air barrier in one, and the services ride in it*

## The Seam That Does Four Jobs

The seam this book closed with a key has a second life. Open
it deliberately — plan the gap instead of merely filling it —
and the most feared line in the dome becomes the most useful
void in the building.

The {{frame.seams}} seams between the panels are a connected
network: every seam meets its neighbours at a vertex, and the
vertices join at the rim and the crown, so the whole shell's
seam system is one continuous channel running through the
entire frame. The wedge book's climate chapters — and the
films this chapter draws on — engineer that channel to do
four jobs at once, and the jobs are compatible for one
elegant reason: the channel runs on positive pressure, so a
leak stops being a leak and becomes a vent. This chapter is
the channel's mechanics; the next one is its climate.

## Gutter, vent, dehumidifier, air barrier

The four jobs, and the sentence that makes them one system.

**Gutter.** Water that reaches the seam — rain past the
skin's shoulder, condensation off the inside face — runs down
the channel's walls to the rim, where the lowest course
drains it. The seam does not merely resist water; it
*carries* the water that got in, to the one place it is
allowed to leave.

**Vent.** The channel breathes: air moves along it from the
rim to the crown and back, pushed by the stack effect — the
same warm-air-rises engine that drives every chimney. The
vent's job is to keep the cavity's air moving, so nothing
that should dry ever sits still long enough to rot.

**Dehumidifier.** Because the channel runs on positive
pressure — air pushed through it, not pulled — moisture in
the cavity is carried out rather than drawn in, and a leak in
the channel pushes air *out* instead of weather *in*. The
sentence from the opener, stated as physics: the channel's
pressure makes its failures vent, which turns the dome's
worst-case leak into an air change.

**Air barrier.** The channel's walls are the panels' edges,
and the edges, sealed along their length, are the building's
airtightness line — the continuous break that stops the
room's air from short-circuiting through the shell. One
system, four jobs, and the jobs are not stacked; they are
the same airflow, doing four things on its way past.

## What fits in the channel

The channel is the building's service corridor, and the
question of what fits is answered by the channel's own
cross-section: water and wire fit; a round duct does not.

The V between two wedges is a long triangular void — wide
at the outside face, narrow at the key. A small pipe, a
cable, a sensor line: all fit, following the seam from rim
to crown the way the services chapter of Part One
prescribed. A round duct — the diameter a ventilation system
wants — does not fit, and the honest answer to that refusal
is the chapter's most practical trick: the key becomes a
spacer. Open the seam wider, print the key in two profiles
that hold the gap apart, and the channel *becomes* the duct
— a triangular duct, sized by the gap, running where the
frame was already going.

![Water and wire fit. Round ducts do not.](../../deliverables/book/figures/seam-fits.png)

The spacer's cost is the dome's own: opening the seams grows
the shell slightly, and the film that worked this through
priced the growth in panels and dome diameter — the honest
note that the duct is not free, it is *bought with radius*,
and the builder who wants a round duct can pay in size
instead of in pipes.

## Keys in halves, and rosettes

The two details that make the channel buildable, borrowed
from the printing chapter of the film.

The key, printed in halves — two profiles, fed in from both
ends of the seam — because a long seam cannot be threaded
through the frame's closed corners, and a key you can push
in from either end is a key you can fit after the dome is
up. The halves meet in the middle, the gasket hides the
joint, and the seam's structural key and its channel walls
become one printed part — the film's answer to the
hand-cut key of Part One, for the builder whose patience
ends where a printer begins.

![Printing the key in halves.](../../deliverables/book/figures/seam-halves.png)

The rosette is the vertex's answer: where five or six seams
meet at a corner, their channels converge on one point, and
the rosette is the fitting that joins them — the channel
system's hub, doing at the vertices what the key does along
the seams. With rosettes in, the channel is one continuous
network top to bottom, and the four jobs of the first
section run through the whole shell instead of dying at
every corner.

## Two rules

The chapter's closing discipline, the two lines that keep a
clever channel from becoming a buried hazard.

**No pressure fitting inside a seam.** The channel's pipes
carry water only where the joint is accessible — a pressure
fitting buried in a seam is a leak the building hides until
the wall tells on it. The channel carries *chases*; the
pressure parts live at the core and the rim, where Chapter
{{ch.the_core}} put them.

**The cable in its chase is the inspector's call.** Wire in
a seam channel is a legitimate routing and a code question,
and the book's rule is the same as every load in Part One:
ask, before the skin closes over it, because the answer
afterward costs a seam.

The seam that does four jobs closes the chapter the way it
closes the building: gutter, vent, dehumidifier, barrier —
one channel, {{frame.seams}} lines of it, and the next
chapter is the air it carries.
