# 79. Why We Call It a Stem Cell

*One undifferentiated dome body, one cut list, and the decision of what the building is for deferred to a set of panels -- fifteen buildings from one frame*

## Why We Call It a Stem Cell

A stem cell is a body that has not decided what it will be.
The frame this book has been building is the building version
of that — and the name is a discipline, not a slogan.

The discipline: the frame, the skin, the cap and the core are
the same in every version of the building, and the decision of
what the building *is* — a home, a gym, a guest house, a
workshop — is deferred to a set of panels that can be decided
later, swapped later, and taken to the next dome later. The
name forbids building the decision in. A house with its use
poured into the walls is a house that can only ever be that
use; the stem cell is the frame that refused, and the refusal
is what the catalogue of Chapter {{ch.fit_outs}} prices.

## The undifferentiated body

The body that has not decided, listed the way the campaign
lists it, because the list is the argument.

One 2V frame — the {{frame.members}} members, the
{{frame.panels}} panels, the seams, the jig — identical
whether the building will hold a bed or a bench press. One
skin, one cap, one utility core, one cut list. The only thing
that differs between the fifteen buildings the campaign
names is which *panels* the walls are wearing: the windows,
the doors, the benches, the beds, the hooks, the sinks — a
set of interchangeable faces hung on the frame's own
openings. The undifferentiated body is the frame; the
differentiation is the fit-out; and the fit-out is panels,
which is the one part of a building that bolts on and off
without asking the structure anything.

The name does the work a name should: it stops the argument
before it starts. A "dome" is a shape; a "stem cell" is a
promise about *deferral* — the promise that the building's
purpose is the cheapest thing to change, because the design
put the purpose where change is cheap. The shape was always
the frame's; the name is the method's, extended to the
building's whole life.

## The stem cell

The plate is the body before the decision: the frame standing,
its openings still wearing the blank panels they will keep
wearing until somebody chooses what the building is for.
Read it as the book's own dome, one step later — the fortnight
done, the shell on, and the single most expensive question in
building left deliberately unanswered.

That question — *what is this building for?* — is the one
every other construction method answers first, with concrete
and studs. The stem cell answers it last, with panels. The
difference is the difference between a decision and a
*preference*: a building that decides its purpose in its
walls has bought the purpose for its whole life; a building
that defers the purpose to its panels can re-buy it in an
afternoon, and the second owner inherits the option the
first owner paid for.

## The seeds

The fifteen named versions, because "any building" is a
claim until the catalogue says which buildings.

Home, food, storage, guest, gym, studio, workshop, garage,
nursery — and the rest of the campaign's list — each one a
*set of panels*, not a new building. The gym is the body with
its walls wearing racks; the guest house is the body with a
bed wall; the nursery is the body with light panels and a
low threshold. The frame never changes. The core never
changes. The cut list never changes — which is the catalogue's
entire economic point: the expensive, hard, structural parts
of the building are bought once, and the cheap, easy,
panelled parts are what make fifteen buildings out of one.

## What the name buys

The chapter's close: what calling it a stem cell actually
buys the builder, beyond a better metaphor.

It buys a resale argument — the building that can change its
use is worth more to its second owner, and the network
chapters lean on exactly this. It buys an upgrade path — the
fit-out can grow with its owner's money the way the shell
ladder grows with its owner's winters. And it buys a
manufacturing argument — one body, one cut list, one jig, and
fifteen products, which is what turns a building method into
a product line and a fortnight's dome into the campaign's
catalogue. The name is not the project's poetry. It is the
project's economics, wearing a word it earned.
