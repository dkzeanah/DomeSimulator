# 78. Exactly Twelve Pentagons, Always

*A sheet of hexagons will not curve, curvature is bought with missing angle, and the missing angle always costs exactly twelve pentagons -- the hexagonal dome, and where it stops being flat*

## Exactly Twelve Pentagons, Always

The hexagon is the panel a factory loves: one shape, flat
sheets, six identical sides, and a tiling that covers a floor
with no gaps and no thinking. The question the hexagonal dome
asks is what it takes to make a *sheet* of hexagons into a
dome — and the answer is a theorem so tidy it sounds invented:
**exactly twelve pentagons. Always.** No more, no fewer, at
every size, for every hexagonal dome that ever closes.

This chapter is the theorem, the dome it builds, and the place
the hexagon's luck runs out — because the hexagonal dome is the
project's other cousin, the one the hex film teaches, and its
lesson is the geodesic's lesson from the opposite side: the
dome pays for its curve somewhere, and the somewhere is never
optional.

## Curvature is bought with missing angle

Flat hexagons tile a plane exactly — six sides, three meeting
at every corner, the angles adding to a full circle at every
vertex, the sheet lying flat because the angles *agree* to
lie flat. To make the tiling curve, some of that agreement has
to be broken: somewhere, the angles at a vertex must add to
*less* than a full circle, and the missing angle is what makes
the sheet buckle upward into a bowl.

Where does the missing angle go? It goes where the tiling
changes shape — a pentagon. Five sides instead of six means one
sixth of the circle is missing at that vertex, and the
pentagon is where the sheet's flatness is spent. The
theorem is the count of how many pentagons the curve demands,
and the count is the one fact about hexagons worth
memorising: a closed surface of hexagons needs exactly twelve
pentagons, every time. Not because twelve is a nice number —
because the total missing angle the sphere demands is one
full turn's worth, and each pentagon supplies exactly one
sixth of a turn. Twelve sixths make the sphere. The football's
pattern is the same theorem; so is the buckyball; so is every
hexagonal dome the world has ever built.

The dome's version is the same tiling with the same bill:
twelve pentagons pay for the curve, the hexagons fill the
space between, and the whole thing closes into a hemisphere
with one strut length running through it — which is the
hexagonal dome's gift to the builder, stated next.

## Twelve pentagons

The plate is the hexagonal dome standing: a field of
hexagons, twelve pentagons hidden in it like the theorem
promised, and one strut length cut ninety times.

The one-strut economy is the hexagonal dome's whole appeal.
Where the 2V geodesic cuts two edge families and the pinwheel
turns them into four lengths, the hex dome cuts one — every
edge the same, every panel the same sheet, the factory's
dream made into a frame. The pentagons are the small print:
twelve panels of a different shape, cut from the same stock,
whose only difference is one fewer side. The cut list is one
length and two panel templates, which is the shortest cut
list in this book — shorter than the geodesic's, shorter than
the zome's — and the reason the hex dome is the one a
manufacturer would pick first.

![The single-hexagon dome.](../../deliverables/book/figures/hex-one.png)

What the one-strut economy costs is the skin's flatness, and
the next section is where the cost lands.

## Where the panels stop being flat

The hexagon's luck runs out the moment the dome stops being a
hint and starts being a building.

The twelve-pentagon tiling curves, but its *panels* are flat
only while they are small enough that the curve is someone
else's problem. Step the hexagon count up — a bigger dome, the
same one strut length — and the panels themselves begin to
carry the curve: a hexagon spanning a curving surface is a
hexagon whose six corners no longer lie in one plane. The
panel *warps*, and a warped panel is a sheet that must be
bent, or cut into triangles, or replaced by the geodesic's
honest approximation — which is the point the hex film makes
with the two domes side by side.

The rule the chapter leaves on the bench: the hexagonal dome
is the cheapest dome in the book while its panels stay flat,
and the geodesic dome is the honest one after they stop. The
method — the wedge, the jig, the seam — builds either; the
split log never cared what shape its frame was pretending to.
But the *choice* belongs to the geometry, and the geometry's
sentence is the chapter's title: twelve pentagons, always,
and beyond a certain size, triangles — because triangles are
the only panel that can never be asked to warp, and the
geodesic dome is the building made entirely of that refusal.
