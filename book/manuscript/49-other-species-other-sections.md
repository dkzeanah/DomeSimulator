# 49. Other Species, Other Sections

*Hardwood, hemlock, and a trunk that is not round*

## Other Species

The book's tree has been pine throughout — the windfall of
Chapter {{ch.the_tree_that_was_already_down}}, straight-grained,
soft, forgiving. This chapter is what changes when the tree is
something else, because the method was never pine's; it was the
*sector's*, and sectors grow in other forests.

The chapter covers the three differences that matter: how other
species split, dry and hold; how to split a trunk that is not
round; and what building with green wood actually does over the
first year. The honest rule of the chapter, stated once and
applied throughout, is the one the wedge book's species page
gives: **use what grows where you are** — the method works with
whatever straight-grained stock the local woodlot offers, and
the differences are adjustments, not prohibitions.

## Density, splitting and the drying schedule

Species by species, the practical differences, because a book
that says "any species" owes the reader the list of what "any"
asks.

**What splits well.** Straight-grained softwoods — pine, spruce,
fir, cedar — split cleanly along the radius, which is what the
seven-cut procedure assumes. The grain runs the length of the
log, the pith is honest, and the sector comes off whole.

**What splits poorly.** Interlocked or spiral grain — elm, some
eucalyptus — fights the rip: the grain runs around the log
instead of along it, the split wanders, and the sector arrives
ragged. The tree's bark usually tells the story before the saw
does — spiral bark means spiral grain — and the rule is to read
the bark and let the tree opt out.

**What lasts.** Rot resistance is a species property, not a
build quality. Cedar, larch, black locust and osage orange sit
in the ground and shrug; pine and spruce do not. The rot-prone
species belong above the damp line, on dry footings, with the
bark charred or the face oiled the way the finish section
describes — and the rot-resistant ones are the free upgrade the
local woodlot sometimes hands over.

**Density and the drying schedule.** Hard, dense wood dries
slowly and splits harder; soft wood dries fast and forgives.
The schedule follows: dense species get longer in the stack,
thinner stickers, more patience — and the key-cut-last rule
below becomes more important the denser the tree, because a
dense sector moves more in its first year than a pine one does.

## A trunk that is oval

The tree is not always round, and the split procedure's first
assumption — a circle divided evenly into {{tree.sectors}} —
meets reality in the oval trunk.

An oval section still splits into eighths; it just produces
*unequal* eighths if the cuts are spaced by angle the way a
round log's would be. The fix is to space by *area*, not by
angle: mark the end grain into {{tree.sectors}} sectors of
equal wood, not equal degrees, so every wedge carries the same
section even though the trunk was an ellipse. The marks are
made by eye against the rings — the pith is the centre, the
rings are the map, and the sector boundaries are drawn where
the wood divides evenly — and the ripping passes follow the
marks instead of the habit.

The consequence travels, and the book says so: unequal spacing
means the sectors' *angles* vary slightly, which means the
seam keys vary slightly, which is exactly the tolerance the
gasket chapters exist to absorb. The oval trunk costs a little
more attention at the split and nothing downstream, because
the downstream was designed for "nearly" — the same word the
round trunk's own imperfections already earned.

## Green versus dry

The last difference is not a species; it is a state, and every
build in this book happens in it: the wood is green.

Green wood moves as it dries — shrinks across the grain,
barely along it — and a wedge moves *least* of all the shapes a
log could become, because a split follows the grain instead of
cutting across it. The sector's angle closes slightly as the
stick dries; a sawn board cups and twists; a boxed heart tears
itself open with a check. The method's geometry was chosen for
this behaviour: the frame is full of seams that can take the
movement, the keys are the last things cut, and the whole
structure is allowed to settle for a year before the skin is
final.

The one rule that matters most, stated for the bench: **cut or
print the keys after the frame has dried.** A key cut to a
green seam fits a dry seam the way a green pair of shoes fits
next year. Let the frame stand its first months, let the
sectors close their angles, then cut the keys to the seams that
actually exist. The rule costs patience and saves the dome; it
is the drying chapter's whole lesson, in one sentence.

And the finish, because the wood's bare faces are a joint, not
a surface: char the outside bark face — the yakisugi pass —
and oil it with linseed, and leave the sawn faces bare, because
a finish on the joint faces traps the moisture the seam channel
is trying to move. The one warning the finish carries is the
safety one: oily rags self-ignite, and the can lives outside
the building. The species change, the sections change, the
state changes — and the method, all the way through, is still
the one this book has been teaching: read the tree, split the
sector, let the wood be wood.
