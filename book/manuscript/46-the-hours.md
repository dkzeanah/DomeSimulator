# 46. The Hours

*Where fourteen days actually went*

## The Hours

The money chapter closed with a rate on hours; this chapter is the
hours themselves, logged the way the book logs everything — as a
table, with the surprises named.

A fortnight is fourteen days, and the days did not go where the
plan said. The plan said the ripping would be the mountain and
the rest foothills; the log says the ripping was a third of the
build and the *panels* matched it, with the jig and the raising
each taking their own full share. The table below is the actual
spread, and the two sections after it are the reading of it: what
took longest, and what a second build would cost now that the
learning is spent.

## The forty hours

The title's claim, counted before the calendar is, because the
title is an arithmetic: {{hr.with_error}} hours of hands-on work,
inside the {{hr.week}}-hour week the book is named after.

The clock, in full: the frame's {{frame.members}} members split at
the measured {{hr.session_wedges}} wedges a session is
{{hr.split_hours}} hours at the log; the same members erected at
{{hr.minutes_per_member}} minutes each is {{hr.erect_hours}}
straight hours of assembly; and the {{hr.error_pct}} per cent
error allowance on the erection — a first build's tuition, paid on
camera — brings the assembly
to {{hr.with_error}}, with {{hr.spare}} hours spare inside the
week. The splitting is the harvest; the forty hours is the
*erection* — panels to standing, props to winch. The fortnight is
where those hours live on a calendar: part days, weather days,
thinking days. The hands-on clock is the number the title makes,
and this chapter's chart is the same clock, drawn as days.

## The fortnight, hour by hour

The chart is the fourteen days as a stacked bar: each phase, its
share of the clock, and the total that has to come out at
fourteen.

The shape of it is the chapter's whole argument. The felling and
bucking — the dangerous, dramatic first days — are small slices.
The ripping is the longest single block, as the plan promised.
But the panels and the jig together match the ripping, and the
raising is a full two days, and the saw setup is a line item
nobody budgets and everybody pays. A builder reading the chart
before their own build learns the real lesson of the fortnight:
**the tree work is quick, the repetition is the mountain, and
the jig is the single best-spent day in the whole table.**

## What took longest and why

The surprises, named, because the chart's flat middle is where
the plan's assumptions died.

The ripping took its {{work.ripping_hours}} hours — the
{{work.struts_per_hour}}-struts-an-hour rate of Chapter
{{ch.days_five_to_eight_ripping}}, sustained across four
afternoons. It is the build's biggest single block, and it is the
one everybody expected. The surprise is that it is *only* the
biggest block — the panel run that followed matched it nearly
hour for hour, because forty panels of twelve motions is not
fast, it is merely *constant*, and constant work still sums to
days. The jig's single day looks like a small slice and it is the
one that kept the panel run from being twice as long. The
raising's two days are the least compressible of all: a dome
goes up one panel at a time, one person, one prop, and the
geometry does not care how practiced the hands are.

The honest reading: the method's *rate* claims were true — the
ripping ran at the promised pace — and the method's *schedule*
claims were optimistic at the edges, because the repetition that
makes each operation fast also makes each operation numerous.
The fortnight is the size it is; the chart is the size's
explanation.

## What a second build would take

The learning-curve estimate, marked clearly as an estimate,
because the book's rule about numbers applies to its own
predictions: a guess must be labelled a guess.

The first build paid for: the jig's design, the marking scheme,
the three practice panels, the seam arguments, the wrong ring
that got caught, the near miss that got survived. A second build
pays for none of them — the jig exists, the scheme is written,
the arguments are settled on the page — and the honest estimate
is that the repetition days shrink most: the ripping stays at
its physical pace, but the panels and the raising lose their
learning time, and the total lands near the rate the films
always claimed and the first build only approached.

The estimate is not a promise and the book will not dress it as
one. It is the answer to the question every builder asks at the
end of the fortnight — *how long would the next one take?* — and
the answer's form matters more than its number: the second build
is the first build minus its tuition, and the tuition is what
this chapter's chart was actually recording. The hours were
never wasted. They were the price of the next dome, and the next
dome inherits them.
