# 27. Days One and Two: Felling

*Putting {{dome.trees}} trees on the ground where you want them*

## Felling

The fortnight's first two days are its most dangerous, and the book
does not soften the sentence: felling is the operation that kills
people who have done it a hundred times, and these two days are
felling.

The job is simple to state — put {{dome.trees}} trees on the ground, where the
bucking and ripping can reach them — and every word after that
sentence is the reason it is not simple to do. The tree decides where
it falls; the feller only influences the decision, through the notch,
the hinge and the back cut. This chapter is the influence, in order:
the safety that comes before the first cut, the two cuts that steer
the tree, the drawing of the hinge that does the steering, the
limbing that follows the fall, and the story half — what actually
happened on this build's second tree, because the story half is where
the chapter earns its place.

Two days for {{dome.trees}} trees is not padding. Each tree is felled, limbed
and made safe before the next is touched, and the second day exists
because the first tree teaches something the next one needs.

## Read this before the notch

The safety page, first, because everything after it assumes it.

**The escape route.** Before the saw starts, walk the route away from
the tree — back and to the side, opposite the planned fall — and
clear it. The route is not "away from the tree"; it is a specific,
walked, cleared line, and it is the thing you use the moment the tree
moves. A tree falls in seconds and the escape takes seconds; the
route being pre-walked is the entire margin.

**The hinge.** The strip of uncut wood between the notch and the back
cut is the hinge, and it is the steering. It keeps the tree attached
while it starts to fall, aims it along the notch, and holds the butt
down so the trunk cannot kick back. Cut through it — the back cut
carried all the way to the notch — and the tree is free to go
anywhere, which is how the word "uncontrolled" enters the report.

**Barber chair.** A leaning tree, cut badly, can split up its own
trunk as it falls — the butt tearing open like a hinged lid, fast,
at head height. It happens when the hinge fails before the tree is
committed. The prevention is the hinge's size and the order of the
cuts, and the respect is: never stand behind a felling tree, not for
one second, not to watch.

**Widow-makers.** Dead branches hung in the crown, freed by the fall
or by vibration, that come down before, with, or after the tree.
Look up before the first cut. Look up again before the back cut. The
crown decides some of what happens next, and it has been deciding
since before you arrived.

**Two people, minimum.** The front matter's rule, restated here where
it binds hardest: never fell alone. The second person is the lookout,
the caller, and the one who goes for help — and their one job during
the fall is to be out of the fall.

## The notch and the back cut

The procedure, in the order that keeps the hinge whole.

**Face the fall.** Stand where the tree should go. The notch opens in
that direction, and the tree will follow the notch unless the hinge
says otherwise — so the first decision is the fall line, made from
the tree's lean, the wind and the landing zone, in that order of
authority.

**Cut the notch.** Two cuts meet to take a wedge out of the trunk on
the fall side: the first cut slants down into the tree, the second
comes in flat from below to meet it, and the wedge falls out. The
notch's depth is about a fifth of the trunk's diameter — deep enough
that the tree has somewhere to rotate into, shallow enough that the
hinge behind it is still the width of the trunk. On this book's
{{tree.butt_diameter_in}}-inch trees the notch is a hand's span, and
the two cuts must meet cleanly; a notch with a step in it steers a
tree sideways.

**Cut the back.** From the far side, level with the notch's flat
floor, the back cut runs toward the notch and stops short of it. The
wood left between — the hinge — is a strip the thickness of a couple
of fingers, even across the trunk. The tree is now standing on its
hinge, committed but held, and it will fall into the notch as the
hinge lets go.

**Leave.** The moment the tree moves, the saw comes out of the cut
and the feller takes the escape route, eyes on the crown, without
turning a back on the fall. The hinge finishes the job; the feller's
job from here is distance.

The hinge dimension is the whole art. Too thick, and the tree sits
on the saw and falls late or not at all; too thin, and the hinge
breaks early and the tree falls wherever its weight wants. The even,
fist-thin strip is the difference between a fall and an event.

## The hinge, drawn

The drawing shows the hinge doing its three jobs at once, because the
jobs are the explanation for everything above.

**It aims.** The hinge is the uncut wood across the back of the
notch, and it is flexible only in one direction — along the fall
line. As the tree tips into the notch, the hinge bends like a door
hinge, and the tree can only go where the hinge bends. Cut the hinge
uneven and the tree steers toward the thick side; cut it straight and
the notch's direction is the tree's direction.

**It holds.** While the tree tips, the hinge keeps the butt attached
to the stump. That attachment is what stops the trunk from kicking
back off the stump as the crown hits the ground — the butt stays
pinned, the tree hinges down like a gate, and the fall is one
motion instead of a throw.

**It lets go.** At the end of the fall the hinge's fibres tear, and
the tree separates from the stump cleanly. The timing of that tear
is set by the hinge's thickness — the feller's one real control over
the whole event, and the reason the dimension is measured, not
guessed.

## Limbing without pinching

The tree is down. The next job is the crown, and it is the part
where the saw gets pinched, because a downed trunk is a compressed
spring in disguise.

The trunk lying on the ground is bent slightly by its own weight:
the top side is in *tension* — the fibres stretched — and the bottom
side in *compression* — the fibres squashed. Cut from the top down
into that bend and the kerf opens as the tension side relaxes — safe.
Cut from the bottom up and the kerf closes on the bar as the weight
settles — pinched, stuck, and often the start of a fight with a
half-cut limb under load. The rule: **read the bend, cut the
tension side first, or cut from the outside of the bend inward.**

Limb the trunk in sections, cutting the branches off flush with the
trunk but never into it — the bucking days want clean wood, not
wounded wood. Work from the butt toward the top, keep the trunk
between you and the slope, and expect every branch to move when its
tension releases. A limb under a fallen trunk is loaded wood; the
moment the saw frees it, it springs. Stand where the spring is not.

By the end of day one the first tree is a clean, bare trunk on level
ground, the crown stacked where the firewood will be cut from it
later, and the saw has done the most dangerous work it will do all
fortnight.

## What actually happened

The story half, recorded the way the book's corrections are recorded —
named, not tidied.

The first tree went exactly where it was aimed. The notch was clean,
the hinge held, and the trunk landed inside a step of the planned
line, which the plan treated as a triumph and the book records as
luck doing its job: the tree was straight, the wind was nothing, and
the lean agreed with the aim.

The second tree did not go where it was supposed to.

It was the lean. The tree had grown with a slight bow toward the
open ground, and the feller — one person, reading the trunk by eye —
cut the notch for where the tree *should* fall and the back cut for
where the hinge would let it. The hinge steered; the lean overruled;
the tree fell a quarter-turn off the aim, taking a young neighbour
down with it and landing with its crown in the brush instead of on
the clearing.

Nobody was hurt. The escape route was where it was supposed to be,
and the miss happened in slow, visible degrees rather than in one
event — which is exactly what the hinge, even a mis-aimed one, is
for. The lesson cost a morning: the second tree had to be worked out
of the brush, the neighbour's remains cut clear, and the fall line
for the *next* tree chosen by the lean alone, which is the rule the
chapter opened with and the feller had half-ignored.

The book keeps the story because the story is the point of the
chapter. The notch, the hinge, the escape route — they are not
technique for a straight tree on a calm day. They are the margin
for the tree that is not straight and the day that is not calm, and
on day two the margin was spent exactly as designed: the tree fell
wrong, and the feller walked away. Two trees down. The fortnight
had begun.
