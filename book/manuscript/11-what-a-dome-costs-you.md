# 11. What a Dome Costs You

*The chapter that argues against the book*

## What a Dome Costs You

Every book about a building method owes its reader one chapter that tries
to talk them out of it. This is that chapter. It is not here for balance;
it is here because the costs it lists are real, they arrive late, and
they are the ones the rest of the dome literature leaves out — which is
exactly how people end up surprised by them.

The argument of this book is that a wedge dome is a remarkable way to get
a strong, cheap, buildable frame out of {{dome.trees}} trees. The argument of this
chapter is that a frame is not a house, and between the two sit three
costs the frame does nothing about: the room is round, the roof is many
angles, and the world of permits and appraisals expects a rectangle. Each
one is priced in time and money, each one is worse than the same problem
in a box, and none of them is fatal. They are the admission price of the
shape, and the honest builder pays it knowingly or pays it twice.

The chapter ends with the cases where the rectangle wins outright, because
those exist and the book's method is not a religion. If you read this
chapter and still want the dome, the rest of the book is yours. If you
read it and do not, the book has just saved you a year.

## Furniture does not fit a curve

The first cost shows up on moving day, and it never fully leaves.

A wall that curves is a wall that refuses the rectangle. Beds, dressers,
bookshelves, kitchen cabinets, a sofa — the furniture economy is built on
straight walls and ninety-degree corners, and against a dome's wall every
straight piece leaves a wedge of dead air behind it. That dead wedge is
real floor you paid for and cannot use, in every direction, at standing
height. The honest floor number for a dome is smaller than the plan-area
number, and Chapter {{ch.round_room}} computes the standing-height map so
the loss is visible before you build rather than after you move in.

The fixes are real and none of them is free. A pony wall under the dome
buys back vertical wall at the edge — Chapter {{ch.pony_wall}} prices it,
and it is the cheapest square footage in the building — but it is still a
cost the box never pays. Built-in storage shaped to the curve costs
custom work. Loose furniture loses its footprint to the geometry, piece
by piece. And the centre of a round room, where the headroom is highest,
is also where the room most wants its furniture to be — which is how a
round room ends up furnished as a ring around an empty middle, with the
good space in the middle spent on walking.

None of this is hidden. It is just not in the frame's price, and a frame's
price is what dome books quote. Count the round-room tax as real: a
fraction of the floor, a custom-furniture bill, or both, from the first
night you sleep in it.

## Roofing a compound surface

The second cost is the one that decides whether the dome survives its
third winter, and it is the reason dome builders talk about skin more than
they talk about anything else.

A dome's roof is one surface made of many flat panels meeting at
{{frame.seams}} interior seams, every one of them a line where water can
get in. A shingled rectangle sheds water downhill in sheets; a dome sheds
it down forty facets and fifty-five valleys, and the valleys are joints.
The frame chapters show the seam doing its job with a key and a gasket,
and the weather chapter (Chapter {{ch.weather_and_water}}) says the
number out loud — {{frame.seams}} of them — because the honest way to
think about a dome's roof is not "one roof" but "{{frame.seams}} small
roofs, all meeting".

What that costs: the seam system is not optional and not trivial. The key
must fit its fold angle, the gasket must take up the tolerance, and the
whole assembly must still shed water after the wood has moved through a
year of drying. The skin options are priced in Chapter
{{ch.closing_the_shell}}, and the pattern across them is the same — the
cheap skin costs more later, and the good skin costs now. A rectangle
with standing-seam metal and two valleys is a solved problem with a
hundred years of practice behind it; a compound surface is a solved
problem only if you do the work this book spends its middle chapters
describing. The roof is not where the dome is weak. It is where the dome
is *particular*, and particular costs attention, which costs time, which
is the currency a two-week frame is supposed to save.

## Permits, appraisals and resale

The third cost never touches the building and still moves the price.

Permits assume a rectangle. The building department's checklists, the
prescriptive code tables, the inspector's mental library — all of them
are written around conventional framing, and a hubless dome of split
wedges is not in any of them. That does not make it unpermittable; it
makes it *unusual*, and unusual means the plan review takes longer, the
official may want an engineer's letter (Chapter {{ch.forces}} tells you
what the engineer's letter does and does not cover), and the fee schedule
may have no line for you. Somewhere, the system will charge for being
surprised.

Appraisals assume a comparable. An appraiser prices a house by finding
three similar recent sales; a dome has no comparables, so the value comes
out of the method instead of the market, and the number is whatever the
method defends. That does not make the dome worth less — it makes its
worth *harder to finance*, and financing difficulty is a real cost for
anyone who is not paying cash.

Resale is where the two meet. The buyer's lender wants the same appraisal
the seller's did, the buyer's inspector has never seen a wedge, and the
pool of buyers who want a round house is smaller than the pool who want a
rectangular one. The network chapters in Part Two (Chapter
{{ch.the_network}} especially) exist because this project's answer to
resale is to make the dome *movable* — value it as a building that can
leave its pad — but that is a proposal, not a market, and the chapter
says so. The honest line for this chapter: a dome you build yourself and
keep is not exposed to any of this. A dome you build hoping to sell like
a house is exposed to all of it.

## When to build a rectangle

The closing test, stated as plainly as the method allows.

Build a rectangle when the building is a shed, a garage, a workshop, or a
storage room — anything where the square shape, the standard sheet goods,
the off-the-shelf door and the appraiser's familiarity are worth more
than the dome's savings. The catalogue this project prices shows the
border: the cheapest and dearest per-square-foot numbers in it are
{{dm.cheapest_sqft}} and {{dm.dearest_sqft}} dollars, and the box shed it
costs beside the dome shed comes out at {{dm.shed_box_cost}} dollars
against {{dm.shed_dome_cost}} — close enough that the shed decision is
about doors and shelving, not about the frame. Build a rectangle when the
site, the code, or the lender is already fighting you, and the fight
would eat the savings. Build a rectangle when you need the work done by a
crew whose language is studs and trusses, not jigs and seams.

Build the dome when the floor is round and wants to be, when the frame
must be cheap enough to build twice, when the tree is already down, or
when the building is a home and the home is yours. The finished-home
comparison in the same catalogue — {{dm.home_dome_cost}} dollars against
{{dm.home_box_cost}} — shows the dome's frame advantage persisting into
the finished house, and Chapter {{ch.money}} takes that number apart
line by line, including the lines that do not help.

The rectangle is a fine building. It is the right building in most cases.
This book exists for the cases where it is not, and the method it
describes is the strongest version of the alternative — measured against
the rectangle's own numbers, printed here, in the chapter that was
supposed to talk you out of it.
