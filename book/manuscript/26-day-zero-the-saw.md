# 26. Day Zero: The Saw

*A hundred and ninety-nine dollars and a sixteen-inch bar*

## Day Zero

Before the fortnight there was a day zero, and day zero was the saw.

The whole method claims to need one machine, and the claim deserves to
be looked at honestly before the felling starts. The saw this build
used was the smallest one on the shelf: a {{saw.displacement_cc}}-cc
consumer saw with a {{saw.bar_in}}-inch bar, {{saw.price_usd}} dollars,
bought new. Not a professional felling saw, not a mill, not a rental —
the same saw a homeowner buys for firewood. If the method works with
this saw, it works with almost any saw; if it secretly needed more,
better to find out on day zero than on day nine.

This chapter assesses the tool the way the book assesses everything
else: what it can do, what it cannot, what the counterfactual would
have bought, and the setup and safety that decide which of the two
lists you live on. The fortnight's days each open with a sentence of
their own; this one opens the whole clock, because every day after it
is this saw's day, and the saw is the only tool in the book.

## The smallest saw on the shelf

What {{saw.displacement_cc}} cubic centimetres and a {{saw.bar_in}}-inch
bar can actually do, because the honest list is shorter than the
advertised one and more useful.

**It fells a modest tree.** A {{tree.butt_diameter_in}}-inch pine is
well inside what this saw drops, as long as the notch and the hinge
are cut the way Chapter {{ch.days_one_and_two_felling}} describes and
not the way impatience does. The saw is not the limit at felling; the
technique is.

**It rips a six-foot section along the grain.** This is the real test,
and the saw passes it with a margin — a rip through wet pine is slow
work, the chain must be sharp and the bar must stay cool, but the
{{saw.kerf_in}}-inch kerf through a {{tree.section_length_ft}}-foot
section is a normal afternoon's work, not a stunt. Chapter
{{ch.days_five_to_eight_ripping}} is the whole ripping ledger, and
its honest finding is that the saw is adequate and the arm is the
bottleneck.

**It cannot be hurried.** A small saw has a small engine, a small oil
pump and a small appetite for abuse. Buried to the dogs in green
pine, it will pull its chain off the bar, overheat, and dull twice as
fast as a professional saw doing the same cut. The method works
*with* this saw by working at its pace — {{work.struts_per_hour}}
struts an hour, sustained, is that pace, and the pace is the method.

What it cannot do at all: mill a board, rip a full-length log in one
pass, or survive being used like a bigger saw. None of those is
needed. The saw's job in this method is exactly three cuts — fell,
buck, split — and every one of them is within this machine's honest
envelope. The method was not adapted to a small saw; it was designed
so a small saw could do it, which is a different and stronger claim.

## What I would have bought instead

The honest counterfactual, because a build book that never admits its
tool was marginal is not being read by somebody who owns the same saw.

A bigger saw — a professional 60-cc class with a longer bar — would
have made the ripping faster, the felling safer-feeling, and the
afternoons shorter. It would have cost several times the
{{saw.price_usd}} dollars, and it would not have changed the method
by one step. The split count, the bucking length, the jig, the
pinwheel — none of them are set by the saw's size. They are set by
the tree and the back, and a bigger saw changes neither.

What a bigger saw would have changed is the *margins*: fewer
sharpening stops, a cooler chain, a faster recovery when a cut went
wide. Those are real, and the book says so rather than romanticising
the small saw. But the method's claim was never "this saw is the best
tool". It was "this saw is *enough*", and enough is a fact you can
verify at the store, in the wood, on day zero — which is the day this
chapter is.

If you own a bigger saw, use it. If you own this one, the method
works with it. If you own neither, the cheapest saw that will hold a
{{saw.bar_in}}-inch bar is the one this book's numbers were produced
with.

## Setting it up

The setup, in order, because a chainsaw set up wrong is a saw that
punishes the setup, not the wood.

**The chain.** New or sharpened, tensioned so it snaps back against
the bar but still pulls around by hand. A loose chain comes off; a
tight chain burns the bar. Check it cold, then again after the first
cut, because a warm chain is longer than a cold one.

**The ripping chain.** Ripping with a crosscut chain is possible and
slow. A ripping chain — filed to a shallow angle — cuts with the
grain instead of fighting it, and it is the single cheapest upgrade
the fortnight has. If the budget allows one extra chain, make it a
rip chain, and keep it for the splitting days.

**Bar oil, always full.** Ripping runs the bar buried for minutes at
a time, which is exactly the condition that cooks a dry bar. The oil
cap is the only gauge that matters on day five through eight; check
it every tank of fuel.

**The file.** A round file matched to the chain, used every few
tanks, by hand, to the chain's own angle. Chapter
{{ch.days_five_to_eight_ripping}} counts what a dull chain costs in
kerf and in arms; here the rule is only: sharp is the state the saw
arrives in, and you are the one who keeps it there.

**Fuel, mixed right.** Two-stroke mix at the ratio on the saw, mixed
fresh, not last season's. The fuel ledger of Chapter
{{ch.fuel_ledger}} is built on a saw that starts
first pull every morning, and starting first pull is a fuel-quality
fact before it is a mechanical one.

## Chaps, helmet, and never alone

The safety page, placed here on purpose: the saw starts on day zero,
so the rules start on day zero.

**Chaps.** Chainsaw-protective trousers, worn for every cut, no
exceptions for a quick trim. They are the difference between a
bruise and an ambulance, and they cost less than the saw.

**Helmet, visor and ear protection.** A falling branch, a kicked
chain, a saw at full throttle for hours — all three are ordinary
events in this fortnight, and all three are aimed at your head.

**Never alone.** The single rule that overrides every other rule in
the book: do not run a chainsaw with nobody in earshot. The felling
chapter says it again, the ripping chapter assumes it, and the story
chapters record the one day it was nearly broken. A phone is not a
second person. A second person is a second person.

The book's warning from the front matter stands unrepeated here but
not unrenewed: felling kills people who have done it a hundred times,
a chainsaw is the most dangerous tool most people ever hold, and
nothing in this book is worth what a silent afternoon with a saw can
cost. Day zero is the day the tool arrives. The respect it deserves
arrives the same day, or not at all.
