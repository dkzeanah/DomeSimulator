# 47. What Broke

*The failures, listed*

## What Broke

Every build book should have this chapter and most do not. This
one lists the failures — the stick that split, the seam that
would not close, the panel built backwards, the near miss — each
one named, explained, and turned into the rule that would have
prevented it.

The chapter is story, not reference, because the failures were
events, and the book's value is in reporting them the way they
happened rather than the way a tidy narrative would have. The
frame stood; the failures were survived; and each one is here
because the next builder will meet the same stick, the same
seam, the same mirror image — and this chapter is the warning
written down while the memory was honest.

## The stick that split along the pith

The first failure, and the one the grading chapter was written
to prevent.

A wedge split in the pile, along its point — a crack running
down the pith line, the soft first-year wood giving way as the
stick dried. It did not fail under load; it failed in the stack,
overnight, the way wood fails when it was going to fail anyway.
The stick had carried its pith off-centre — the splitting pass
had wandered, the point held a rag of pith instead of honest
wood, and the drying tension found the weak line and opened it.

What it taught, now written as Chapter {{ch.small_units}}'s
fourth pass: **the pith is the member's weakest line, and a
point that misses the pith is a member with a crack scheduled
in it.** The grading pass was added to the sorting procedure
the same day — walk the pile, look down each point, reject the
rag — and the stick itself went to blocking, where it still is,
holding up something unimportant and never asked to do more.

The counting lesson followed: the tree chapter's
{{dome.spare_struts}} spare struts are not generosity, they are
this failure, budgeted. One split stick was one spare spent. A
build planned with zero spares is a build that assumes nothing
ever breaks, and this chapter is the record of why that
assumption is a lie.

## The seam that would not close

The fold-angle problem of Chapter {{ch.seams}}, encountered in
the flesh, and the week it cost.

The frame was up to its third course when a seam refused to
close — two panels that met at the right angle on the drawing
and refused to agree in the air, the key standing proud, the
gap tapering to nothing and back. The instinct was force: pull
it closed with screws, the way every frustration with wood
ends. The book's own seam chapter was not yet written, and the
week that followed was the research for it.

What the week found was the fold schedule — the two angles,
the two key profiles, the arithmetic that had been sitting in
the geometry the whole time. The offending seam was a
{{seam.fold_b_deg}}-degree fold wearing a {{seam.fold_a_deg}}-degree
key, and no amount of persuasion was going to make a key cut
for one angle seat in the other. The fix was a second profile
cut on the bench, and the lesson became the seam chapter's
opening sentence: the fold angle is not constant, the difference
is real, and the answer is two keys — never force.

The chapter that resulted is one of the book's longest, because
the week was one of the build's. The seam that would not close
is now the seam that taught the schedule, and the schedule is
printed so the next builder spends an afternoon where this one
spent a week.

## The panel I built backwards

Handedness, and the marking scheme that came out of it.

The two panel shapes of Chapter {{ch.forty_panels_one_hundred_and_twenty_members}}
are mirror images of themselves depending on which way the
triangle faces the dome — and one panel, built on an
afternoon's autopilot, came off the bench as the mirror of the
panel the frame wanted. It was perfect. It was perfect for the
other side of a seam that did not exist there. The panel's
members were all in the right places, and the whole triangle
was facing the wrong way.

The failure was not carelessness in the usual sense; it was the
absence of a mark that *meant* something. The sticks had been
sorted, but the *faces* had not been marked — bark up was a
habit, not a rule, and the habit had a mirror image. The marking
scheme that came out of the afternoon is the one Chapter
{{ch.days_ten_to_twelve_forty_panels}} now prescribes: mark the
sawn face, one stripe per class, and let the mark read as an
orientation cue — mark up, bark up, and a panel started wrong
announces itself before the first screw.

The backwards panel was rebuilt in an hour — the members
salvaged, the screws pulled, the marks applied. It cost an
afternoon total, which is the cheapest failure in the chapter,
and it paid for the scheme that made every panel after it
impossible to build wrong.

## What I would call a near miss

The safety incident, reported rather than tidied, because the
chapter's honesty is the point of it.

The raising was on its second day, the shell was half up, and a
panel — the outward-leaning failure of Chapter
{{ch.days_thirteen_and_fourteen_raising}} — let go of its prop
as it was being set. It did not fall far; the prop caught it
again, a corner's width lower, and the panel hung there, one
seam half-closed, swinging gently, while the builder stood on
the ladder holding a drill and, for a second, nothing else.

Nobody was hurt. The rule that was broken was the rule the
raising chapter now states first: a panel goes up, a prop goes
under it, and a prop never comes out until the course above has
closed. The panel that fell had been *reached past* — its prop
moved early, because the next panel was waiting, and the
waiting panel was the mistake. The near miss became the rule's
word-for-word text, and the rule is now the chapter's.

The book reports it the way the felling chapter reported the
second tree: named, not tidied. A build book that hides its
near misses trains the next builder to hide theirs, and the
next builder's near miss may not catch its prop. This one did.
The next one gets the rule instead.
