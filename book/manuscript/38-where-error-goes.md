# 38. Where Error Goes

*A tolerance budget for a building made of split logs*

## Where Error Goes

Every chapter so far has mentioned the method's tolerance — the
overfit absorbing a sixteenth, the key absorbing the fold, the
panel owning its edges. This chapter is the budget behind the
mentions: a complete account of where error can land in a frame
built from split logs, which of those places the method absorbs,
and which one it does not.

A building method that cannot say where its error goes is a method
that discovers the answer at raising. This book's method can say
it, because it was designed around the saying: every operation in
the fortnight either sends its error somewhere specific or refuses
to make the cut that would. The chapter walks the four landing
places — stock length, butt angle, seam gap, and the ring — marks
three of them as absorbed and one as accumulating, and ends with
the check that keeps the last one honest. It is the most
structural chapter in the book, in both senses of the word.

## Four places error can land

The complete list, because a tolerance budget that omits a landing
place is a budget with a hole in it.

**Stock length.** The member arrives at the bench longer than it
should be — the split drifted, the bucking was generous, the plan
was optimistic. This is the commonest error in the build and the
cheapest, because the overfit exists for exactly it: the head runs
long, the assembly locates the butt, and the flush cut of Chapter
{{ch.flush_cutting_in_place}} removes the error with the offcut.

**Butt angle.** The compound cut of Chapter {{ch.butt_cut}} lands
a degree off, and the butt no longer sits flat on its neighbour's
face. The error is real but bounded: the butt's job is to present
area to the side, and a butt a degree off still presents nearly
all of it — the screws pull the overlap closed, and the contact
settles into the wood. What the method refuses is the opposite
error: a butt cut *short*, which is why the allowance stays on
until the bench decides.

**Seam gap.** Two panels meet and the gap between them is a
fraction wider or narrower than the key's share — the fold is
right but the wood is itself. The seam absorbs it by design: the
key compresses, the gasket gives, and the {{frame.seams}} seams of
the frame share the difference so no single seam carries it all.
This is the landing place the whole seam system was built for, and
the one the book spent its middle chapters engineering.

**The ring.** The base ring is where the frame meets the ground,
and it is the one landing place that does not give. A sixteenth of
error in the ring is a sixteenth of error in the circle the whole
dome stands on, and the dome cannot compress its way out of a
wrong foundation. The ring is the subject of the last section,
because it is the budget's one honest failure point.

## Three of them are absorbed

Why three of the four land safely, and what "absorbed" actually
means in wood.

An absorbed error is one that leaves the build as something else:
offcut, compression, distribution. The stock-length error leaves as
the flush cut's offcut — Chapter {{ch.head_overfit}} priced it, and
the price is the board feet in its table. The butt-angle error
leaves as contact: the overlap's screw row pulls the butt into the
wood until the bearing is full, and a degree of bevel error
becomes a fraction of an inch of settlement that the frame never
sees again. The seam error leaves as compression: the key takes
up the difference, the gasket breathes it, and the dome closes
with the error spread invisibly across {{frame.seams}} joints.

The three share one property: each is absorbed at the *place it
was made*, by the design of that place, without travelling. The
stock error never leaves the bench. The butt error never leaves
the joint. The seam error never leaves the seam. That locality is
the whole secret — an error that dies where it is born cannot
compound with its neighbours, and a frame built from rough wood
needs exactly that: forty small absorpions instead of one long
accumulation.

## The tolerance budget

The drawing is the budget as a flow: error enters at the four
places, three of them send it sideways into absorption — the
offcut pile, the bearing, the gasket — and one of them, the ring,
passes it on.

Read the arrows as the method's real structure, because they are.
Every chapter of the build is somewhere on this drawing: the
overfit is the stock-length arrow, the screw row is the butt
arrow, the key is the seam arrow, and the ring is the arrow with
no exit. A builder who has internalised the drawing stops fearing
the ordinary errors of rough timber — they are budgeted — and
starts fearing only the one the drawing marks in the colour of
warning.

## The one that accumulates

The ring, named and checked, because it is the budget's single
honest failure point.

The base ring's error does not absorb; it *accumulates*. The ring
is a closed circle of {{frame.rim_edges}} panels, and an error
anywhere in it is an error everywhere: the dome above it rises
from whatever circle the ring actually is, and every course
multiplies the ring's truth by the frame's own height. A ring set
a half inch out of round is a dome that leans a half inch at the
base and more at the crown — the one error in the method that
grows as the building does.

So the ring is where the book's checking discipline concentrates.
The check is simple and it is done three times: when the ring is
first set, measure across every opposite pair of its
{{frame.rim_edges}} sides — the numbers must agree within the
gasket's give, and any pair that does not is the ring telling you
where it is wrong while the fix is still a shim. When the first
course closes, measure the ring again — the courses above have
already begun leaning on it, and a ring that moved has moved the
whole frame's opinion of round. And before the top closes, once
more, from outside: the dome's last chance to be told, cheaply,
what the raising is about to make permanent.

The ring's honesty is the book's last word on tolerance. The
method forgives stock, forgives butts, forgives seams — and it
never forgave the ring, because the ring is the one place where
forgiveness would have to come from the ground. Chapter
{{ch.the_footing_and_the_ring}} gives the ring its footing; this
chapter gave it its warning. Build accordingly.
