# 35. The Head Overfit

*Deliberately cutting it too long*

## The Head Overfit

The head overfit is the idea that makes the whole method tolerant,
and it is the single most counter-instinctive rule in the book:
**cut every member deliberately too long.**

A builder trained on dimensional lumber works measure-cut-fit:
measure the stick, cut it to the number, fit it into the building.
Rough timber will not survive that order. The stick is not the
shape the tape assumed — it bows, it tapers, its butt lands where
the neighbour's face happens to be — and a stick cut to the plan's
length before it meets the building is a stick that arrives wrong.
So the method reverses the order: cut long, locate in the jig, and
let the fixture — not the tape — decide where the end actually
falls. The overfit is the deliberate slack that makes the reversal
possible, and this chapter is its economics: where the error goes,
how much to leave, and what the tolerance costs in wood.

## Where error goes

The core argument, in one image: the head end of every member runs
{{jig.head_overfit_in}} inches past its final position, and those
inches are where the build's error goes to die.

Follow the sequence. The member is cut long at the split. It goes
into the jig with its butt located against the stop — the one
position the member must hold — and its head running free, past the
head fence, into the overfit zone. The panel is assembled: the
overlaps screwed, the triangle pulled tight, the neighbours
defining the panel's truth by *being* the panel. Only then — with
everything located, clamped and true — does the saw run along the
head fence and take the overfit off. The cut is made in place,
against the panel the member actually lives in, and the member
ends up exactly the length the *assembly* required, not the length
the tape predicted.

Every error the earlier steps could have introduced — a butt that
landed a quarter inch deep, a neighbour whose face sits proud, the
ordinary drift of split timber — was absorbed before the cut,
because the cut is the last operation and everything before it had
room to be wrong. The overfit is the method's crumple zone:
{{jig.head_overfit_in}} inches of deliberate waste on every member,
in exchange for every member being final-cut against reality.

The sidebar rule that pairs with it, stated once here and used
everywhere after: **locate first, cut second.** The fixture
establishes the cut; the cut never establishes the location. The
maxim is the opposite of how most people are taught to work with a
tape, and it is the sentence that makes rough timber buildable at
all.

## Long, then flush

The drawing shows the same panel twice: first with all three heads
running long past the fence — the overfit still attached, the
panel already a panel — and then after the flush cut, the heads
sawn down to the fence line, the panel exactly the triangle the
bench describes.

The two states are the chapter's whole teaching. In the first, the
panel is *assembled but not decided*: everything about it is final
except its outside edges, which still carry the slack. In the
second, the slack is gone and the edges are true — true to the
bench, not to a measurement, which is the only truth a panel's
edges can have that its neighbours will agree with.

Notice where the saw is in the drawing: riding the fence, the bar
flat, cutting all three heads on one plane. The cut is a single
motion repeated three times per panel — {{frame.members_per_panel}}
cuts — and it is the only operation in the whole panel where
accuracy and *finality* meet. Everything before it was reversible;
after it, the panel is committed. The overfit is what bought the
reversibility.

## How much to leave

The overfit's size is a build decision, and the book declares its
own: {{jig.head_overfit_in}} inches. The number comes from what the
overfit must absorb, and the honest way to size your own is to
count the variation in your own pile.

The overfit must cover: the drift in your splitting, the taper of
your sections, the seating of your butts against the stops, and the
settling of the sticks under the clamps. If the worst stick in
your pile wanders half an inch from the plan's ideal, a half-inch
overfit leaves the good sticks wasting half an inch and the bad
sticks with nothing to spare. So measure the pile, not the plan:
the overfit is set to the *spread* of your stock, plus a margin
for the day you are tired. {{jig.head_overfit_in}} inches was this
build's spread plus margin; a tighter pile could run less, a
looser one more.

The trade is one-dimensional: more overfit costs wood, less
overfit costs do-overs. The table below prices the wood; the
do-overs price themselves the first time a member arrives at the
head fence already short, which is the one failure the overfit
cannot fix — because the overfit *is* the fix, and a stick cut
short never had any.

## Offcut per dome

The table is the tolerance's bill, and the book prints it the way
it prints every bill: in full, with the units the builder actually
pays in.

Every member sheds its overfit into the offcut pile: the flush
cuts of Chapter {{ch.flush_cutting_in_place}}, one per member,
{{frame.members}} members, adding up to the figure in the table —
board feet of perfectly good wood, cut off and set aside. The
honest note is that the offcut is not waste in the moral sense: it
is blocking, bracing, wedges and firewood, and the method was
sized for it from the start — the tree's spare struts and the
offcut allowance are the same budget, counted in different units.
But it is wood the frame does not stand on, and the table says how
much, because a tolerance that is not priced is a tolerance that
will quietly eat the savings it was bought with.

![The price of the tolerance, in board feet.](../../deliverables/book/figures/offcut-total.png)

The overfit's ledger closes where the chapter began: cut long,
locate, assemble, flush. {{jig.head_overfit_in}} inches of slack
per member, a table's worth of offcut per dome, and in exchange —
a frame whose every member is final-cut against the panel it
actually lives in. That is the cheapest accuracy there is, and the
next chapter is the cut that collects it.
