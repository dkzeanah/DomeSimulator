# 30. Day Nine: The Jig

*One flat bench that makes forty panels the same*

## The Jig

Day nine is the day that pays for the other thirteen, and it is a
single day spent building a single thing: one flat bench on which
every one of the frame's {{frame.panels}} panels will be assembled,
identical to the last.

The jig is where the method stops being a pile of good ideas and
becomes a production line. The wedges are cut, and they are nearly
alike — nearly, because the tree was nearly round, the saw nearly
straight, the eye nearly true. The jig does not need them to be
exact; it needs them to be *held* the same way while the exact
parts — the butt cut, the head length, the screw positions — are
established by the fixture instead of by the eye. A jig is a decision
made once and repeated forty times in wood, and the rest of the
book's build chapters are that sentence, carried out.

The full reference for the fixture — every stage, labelled, straight
from the simulator — is Chapter {{ch.jig_reference}}. This chapter is
the story of the day it got built, and the argument for why a day is
the right price.

## Why a jig and not a tape

The temptation on day nine is to skip the bench and measure the
panels one at a time: lay three sticks in a triangle, tape them to
length, screw them. It works — for the first panel. The question is
what the fortieth panel looks like.

A tape measures each panel against an ideal that lives in the
builder's head, and no two measurements of a rough stick agree with
each other. The error compounds quietly: a sixteenth here, a
thirty-second there, and the panels drift apart in ways that only
announce themselves at raising, when two neighbours built a week
apart refuse to share a seam. The frankendome — Chapter
{{ch.the_frankendome}} — was forty panels built exactly this way,
and its lesson was the whole reason this book exists: **nothing
repeated, so nothing could be checked against the last one.**

A jig inverts the logic. The fixture holds the *position* of every
stick — the triangle's shape, the cut lines, the screw locations —
as physical stops, and the builder's job becomes loading the stick
against the stop. The measurement happens once, when the bench is
built; after that, the panels are not measured at all, they are
*located*, and locating is an operation that cannot drift, because
the stop does not move. Panel forty is built the same way panel one
was, and the only difference between them is the sticks — which is
exactly the difference the method is designed to tolerate.

Repeatability beats accuracy, when there are forty of something. A
jig that is half a degree wrong builds forty panels that agree with
each other, and the frame closes; a tape that is read forty times
builds forty panels that disagree, and the frame does not. Day nine
buys the agreement.

## Building it

The bench, in order, kept deliberately simple — the jig's only
requirement is flatness, and everything else is stops.

**The bench.** A flat surface bigger than the largest panel: two
sawhorses and a sheet of plywood are enough, levelled and braced so
it cannot rock. Flat is the one property that matters, because the
panel's truth is measured against the bench; a bench with a twist
in it builds a twist into every panel that lies on it.

**The triangle.** Lay out one panel full size on the bench — the
actual member lengths from the cut list of Chapter
{{ch.worked_build}}, the actual gasket allowance — and screw down
stops along all three outside edges: the triangle's outline, made
of scrap, exactly where the members will lie. The stops are the
jig's first truth, and they are set once, from the drawing, with a
square and a story stick — the tape's last appearance in the whole
fortnight.

**The fences.** Inside the triangle, two more lines of stops mark
where the head ends will be sawn off and where the butt ends land:
the inset of Chapter {{ch.pinwheel}}, built as wood instead of
remembered as a number. The fence is the cut line, physical, and
the saw runs along it the way a saw always wishes it could.

**The cut planes.** Where the saw must cut, the jig provides the
plane: a straight edge the bar rides against, set at the butt's
bevel and the head's square. Chapter {{ch.butt_cut}} gives the
angles; the jig holds them; the builder never sights one.

**The clamps.** Whatever holds a wedge still against the stops
while the screws go in — quick clamps, wedges, a foot on a pedal.
The member must arrive at its stops and stay; how it is persuaded
is the builder's business.

The bench takes a day, and the day is not wasted on accuracy — it
is spent on *position*, which is the cheaper thing to be right
about. A stop screwed in the wrong place is moved in a minute. A
panel built in the wrong place is discovered at raising, in front
of a crowd of its neighbours.

## The jig, complete

The plate is the finished fixture with a panel loaded on it, at the
moment the long heads are being sawn off against the fence — the
stage of the work where the jig's whole argument is visible at
once.

Look at what is holding the truth. The sticks are rough — bark
backs, saw marks, the ordinary drift of split timber. The triangle
they lie in is exact, because the stops are. The butt ends are
where the fence says, the heads run long past the cut plane, and
the saw is about to make the only cuts in the whole panel that have
to be right — and they will be right, because the plane the saw
rides is the plane the bench made, not the plane a hand held.

That is the day-nine bargain, paid in one afternoon and collected
for the rest of the fortnight: from tomorrow, nobody measures
anything. Chapter {{ch.days_ten_to_twelve_forty_panels}} is the
production run the bench was built for, and the run is the proof —
{{frame.panels}} panels, one after another, each one located, cut
and screwed in the same twelve motions, and each one leaving the
bench the same panel.
