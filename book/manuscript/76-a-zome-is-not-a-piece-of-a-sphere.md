# 76. A Zome Is Not a Piece of a Sphere

*Sweep a star of directions instead of subdividing a solid and every panel is flat by construction -- the zome, the one-panel golden zome, and what the flatness costs*

## A Zome Is Not a Piece of a Sphere

The geodesic dome starts from a solid — the icosahedron of
Chapter {{ch.forty_panels_one_hundred_and_twenty_members}} —
and projects its faces onto a sphere. A zome starts from
directions and sweeps. The difference is one sentence, and it
decides everything about the panels: **a geodesic panel is a
flat triangle standing in for a curved surface; a zome panel
is flat because the whole zome is flat, panel by panel, by
construction.**

The zome is in this book because the project's films teach it
and the wedge book gives it a chapter, and because it is the
method's natural cousin: same members, same seams, same jig —
a different *shape family*, with one property the geodesic
dome has to work for and the zome gets for free. This chapter
is the sweep that makes the property, the famous one-panel
zome that follows from it, and the honest bill the flatness
charges at the joints.

## The sweep, in four steps

From a star of directions to a frame of flat parallelogram
panels, with the guarantee built in at every step.

**One: choose a star.** Pick a handful of directions in space —
a star of lines through the origin, like the ribs of an
umbrella opened in three dimensions. These directions are the
zome's *edges*, and they are chosen, not inherited from a
solid.

**Two: draw the planes.** For every pair of directions, the
two lines span a plane. Each such plane is the face of a
panel, and because it is spanned by two straight lines, it is
perfectly flat — not approximately flat, not projected flat:
*flat*, the way a sheet of paper is flat.

**Three: bound the panels.** In each plane, the panel's edges
are the points where the star's lines meet the design's
envelope — cut the plane into a parallelogram or a triangle
using the neighbouring directions as the other two sides.

**Four: assemble.** The panels meet along the star's
directions, each edge shared by two flat panels that were
flat before they met. The frame that results is a zome: a
shell of flat panels hanging on a star of directions, with no
curved surface anywhere in its ancestry.

The guarantee is the whole point. A geodesic dome's panels
are flat *approximations* of a sphere, and the sphere keeps
its curve in the fold angles between them — the seam schedule
of Chapter {{ch.seams}} is the book's payment of that debt.
A zome's panels are flat because the geometry never asked
them to be anything else, and the fold angles fall out of
the star instead of the sphere. Nothing was projected.
Nothing was approximated. The flatness is not a
manufacturing achievement; it is a property of the
construction.

## The zome

The plate is the zome standing: a point on top, level rings
of hubs where the directions cross, and every panel a flat
parallelogram hung between them. Read it against the
geodesic plate of Chapter
{{ch.forty_panels_one_hundred_and_twenty_members}} and the
difference is the chapter: no two triangle shapes, no fold
schedule, no sphere under the surface — a shell that looks
like a crystal because it is built the way one is, face by
flat face.

The zome's counts are the sweep's counts: the directions
decide the hubs, the hub pairs decide the panels, and the
whole thing can be counted before any wood is bought —
which is exactly the discipline the book's method chapters
applied to the geodesic frame, transferred to the star. The
top comes to a point because the star's directions meet
there; the hubs land on level rings because the sweep is
symmetric; and the panels hang flat because they were always
going to.

## The famous one-panel zome

The star's freedom has a limit case, and the limit case is
the famous one: choose the directions *equal* — every
direction in the star at the same angle to its neighbours —
and every panel the sweep produces is the same panel.

One strut length, one panel template, ninety repetitions.
The one-panel zome is the flat-panel world's answer to the
geodesic's two-lengths-two-shapes economy, and it is
genuinely famous among zome builders for the same reason
this book loves the 2V dome: a frame of one repeated thing
is a frame a jig can build, and the jig does not care
whether the thing is a triangle or a parallelogram.

![The famous one-panel zome.](../../deliverables/book/figures/zome-golden.png)

What the golden zome charges for its single panel is the
same bill every simplification charges, moved to the next
line: the joints get particular, the floor meets the frame on
a slope that wants its own treatment, and the roof's point
concentrates the water the geodesic's crown was spreading.
The one-panel zome is not the better dome; it is the *simpler
one*, and the simplicity is bought with the star's freedom —
which is the chapter's closing distinction, stated once more:
the geodesic dome pays for the sphere at every seam, and the
zome pays for flatness at every corner. Pick which bill you
want, and the method — the split, the jig, the seam — builds
either one.

## Zome against geodesic dome

The comparison table's prose, because the chapter owes the
reader the choice the films make side by side.

A geodesic dome is rounder: its panels approximate a sphere,
so its skin is the smoother weather surface and its floor is
the larger circle. A zome is flatter: its panels need no
approximation, so its joints are the simpler ones and its
glazing — the one place flatness pays the builder directly —
is a matter of cutting rectangles instead of solving
triangles. The counts differ the way the constructions
differ: the zome's template count is set by the star's
symmetry, the geodesic's by the frequency. And the method's
answer to both is the same sentence the book has been
repeating since the tree: **the wedge does not care which
shape family it frames.** It cares that the frame is made of
triangles or of flat panels a jig can repeat — and both
families are, which is why the zome gets a chapter instead
of a footnote.
