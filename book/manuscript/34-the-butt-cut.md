# 34. The Butt Cut

*The one compound angle, made before anything is assembled*

## The Butt Cut

Name it honestly at the top, because this project got it wrong in public
once: the butt cut is a compound cut. A mitre and a bevel
together, on one end of every member, made once, off the jig, before
anything is assembled. The claim this book makes is not "no mitres"
— Chapter {{ch.pinwheel}} has the full correction of that claim. The
true claim is better: no member end ever meets another member end,
and the one compound cut the frame does need is made once per
stick, against a fixed setup, instead of twice per stick against a
guess.

This reference chapter is that cut: where its angle comes from,
how few distinct setups the whole dome actually needs, and the
procedure for making it with the allowance still attached. It is
the shortest of the jig chapters and the one the errata pages point at
most, because it is where the book's honesty and the saw's geometry
finally agree.

## The angle its neighbour presents

The butt end of a member is not cut square to the stick's length.
It is cut to match the two facts about the neighbour it will land
on: the neighbour's side leans away from the panel's plane by the
sector's angle, and the panel itself folds toward its neighbour by
the seam's angle. The cut that lands flat against both is a
compound — one angle in the plane of the panel, one across it — and
that compound is the butt cut.

Where the angles come from, precisely: the member's sawn face sits
{{seam.half_sector_deg}} degrees off the member's centreline, so
the butt must present that angle to sit flat on its neighbour's
face. The panel's fold adds the seam's angle on top — the
{{seam.fold_a_deg}} and {{seam.fold_b_deg}} degrees of Chapter
{{ch.seams}}. Together they make the one cut the dome cannot avoid,
and the reason it cannot be avoided is the same reason the seam
key exists: the sphere curves, the panels fold, and the wood that
meets that fold must be shaped for it.

The good news, stated as a count: the whole dome needs only a few
distinct setups, not one per member. The bevel is the same
everywhere, and the mitre takes only the values the member classes
demand. The table on the next page is the complete list, generated
from the simulator's own setup schedule — and the fact that the
list is short is the second half of the correction the pinwheel
chapter opened: the compound cut is real, and it is *few*, and
"few, fixed, and made on the jig" is the honest version of the
claim the film got wrong.

## Butt cut setups

The table is the entire saw-setting vocabulary of the build: every
distinct setup, straight from the simulator's own list, with the
member classes each one serves.

Read it as the answer to the question the sceptic is owed: if the
butt is a compound cut, how many compounds are we talking about?
The answer is the table's length — small enough to write on the
wall above the saw, fixed enough that a mistake is a failure to
read, not a failure to calculate. The saw is set once per setup,
the class is cut through, and the setting is never re-derived at
the bench. That is the discipline the whole method runs on:
measure the angles once, on the drawing, and let the jig hold them
forever.

## Making it

The procedure, at the saw, with the allowance still attached —
because the butt cut is never made on a stick that is already its
final length.

**Leave the allowance.** The member arrives at the butt cut with
the spare stock of Chapter {{ch.head_overfit}} still on its head
end and the butt allowance still on its butt end. The allowance is
not sloppiness; it is the room the cut needs to be made *against
the jig* rather than against a tape, and it is the reason a
mis-aimed cut is a do-over instead of a ruined member.

**Set the fixture, not the saw alone.** The butt cut is made with
the stick located on its sawn faces in the jig's cut plane — the
saw's own angle settings do the compounding, but the *position* of
the cut along the stick comes from the stop, and the stop is the
jig's. A butt cut made freehand is a compound angle guessed twice;
a butt cut made in the fixture is a compound angle held once.

**Cut, check, and only then cut the class.** The first stick of a
setup is the proof: cut it, offer it to its neighbour, and read
the contact. A butt that sits flat across the neighbour's face is
the setup's certificate; a butt that rocks on one edge sends the
saw setting back one degree and the proof is repeated. Only when
the first stick passes does the class get cut — the same rule as
the jig's first panel, applied to the saw.

**Keep the allowance until the member leaves the bench.** The
butt allowance is removed at the stage the jig schedule says, not
at the saw. Between the butt cut and the bench, the allowance is
still protecting the cut — and the head overfit is still
protecting the length. The two spare ends are the method's
crumple zones, and Chapter {{ch.where_error_goes}} is the
accounting of everything they absorb.

One cut, made once, on one end, off the jig. The butt cut is the
whole of the frame's precision joinery, and it fits in a table, a
fixture, and an afternoon — which is what "no two ends ever meet"
was always worth.
