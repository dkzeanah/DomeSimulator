# 29. Days Five to Eight: Ripping

*One hundred and twenty struts, thirty to an afternoon*

## Ripping

Days five through eight are the bulk of the labour and the heart of
the method: every section of the two trunks, split along its length
into {{tree.sectors}} wedges, until the frame's
{{frame.members}} members exist as a pile. Two trees become a
building on these four days; everything before was preparation and
everything after is assembly.

The arithmetic sets the pace and the pace is honest:
{{work.struts_per_hour}} struts an hour, sustained, means
{{work.ripping_hours}} hours of ripping — {{work.afternoons}}
afternoons — for the whole frame. That is the number the fortnight's
reputation rests on, and it was measured on this build, not promised.
The chapter is the measurement made into a procedure: the three
passes at the log, the cradle that holds a round section still, the
pith you aim at, the rhythm that sustains the rate, and the chain
sharpness that the whole four days secretly depend on.

## The three passes, at the log

The procedure of Chapter {{ch.split}}, now with the saw actually in
hand, at production pace.

**Stage the section.** Every section gets stood on end or laid in
the cradle before the saw starts — never ripped on the ground where
it can roll, never half-rolled between passes. The staging is the
first third of the work, and a section staged wrong costs more time
than a cut made slow.

**Pass one: halve it.** Aim down the pith line on the end grain and
rip the full length. The saw walks itself through a straight section
if the bar starts true; the feller's job is to start true and let
the saw cut — a ripping saw is steered with small corrections, not
pushed. The two halves fall apart with flat faces down.

**Passes two and three: quarter, then eighth.** Each half sits on
its flat face in the cradle and gets halved again; each quarter the
same. Seven cuts per section, all of them "through the middle", and
the pile of wedges grows by {{tree.sectors}} with every section
finished.

**Stack as you go.** The wedges go straight onto stickers, sawn
faces down, bark up, where the drying chapter wants them — not
thrown on a pile where the day's own moisture steams them. A wedge
stacked right on day five is a straight member on day thirty; the
stacking is part of the cut, not the cleanup after it.

**Count the pile against the plan.** {{tree.struts_per_tree}} struts
a tree, {{dome.struts_available}} for the two, {{frame.members}} in
the frame, {{dome.spare_struts}} spare for the grading rejects. The
count is checked at the end of every afternoon, because a shortfall
found on day eight is a fourth tree; a shortfall found on day
thirteen is a problem with no good name.

## Holding a round section still

The first pass is the hard one for a reason the second and third do
not have: a round section wants to roll, and a rolling log under a
ripping saw is where pinched bars and ruined cuts are made.

The cradle is the answer, and it does not need to be furniture. Two
stakes driven either side of the section, a pair of wedges under it,
or a simple V of scrap timber along its length — anything that stops
the roll and leaves the top of the section at a working height. The
cradle's job is negative space: it holds the log where the saw is
not. Once the first pass is made, the halves sit on their flat faces
and the cradle's work is mostly done — flat stock on a flat bench is
stable by itself, which is the second reason the halving pass goes
first, every time.

The wedges earn their place on the *first* pass and again at the
end. Driven under a section, they level it, lift it off the dirt,
and take the weight so the saw's kerf stays open. A section that
settles as you cut closes the kerf behind the bar — the saw bogs,
the cut wanders, and the pass is ruined in its last foot. The wedge
set under the cut line is the cheapest insurance in the method.

## Reading the pith

Every rip aims at the centre of the section, and the centre of a
real section is not a point — it is a small, dark, slightly
off-centre dot in the end grain, and the first skill of the ripping
days is finding it.

The pith is the tree's first year: a soft spot, usually darker than
the rings around it, not always exactly centred. The split geometry
does not care that it is slightly off — the sectors of a slightly
off-centre split are nearly identical, and "nearly" is what the
method budgets for. What matters is that every pass goes *through*
the pith, so every sector keeps a point that is honest wood, not a
rag of pith off to one side. A sector whose point misses the pith
by an inch is a member with a soft, split-prone edge exactly where
Chapter {{ch.what_broke}} records the failures beginning.

On a section that is not round — the oval butt, the flat-sided
trunk — the pith still rules. Aim at the centre of the rings, not
the centre of the shape; the rings are the tree's own record of
where its middle is, and they are right more often than the outline
is. The first pass is started from a stance where the pith is
visible the whole way — sighting down the bar, checking every few
feet — because a pass that starts an inch off arrives a foot off,
and a wedge is six feet long.

## The cutting rhythm

The chart is the claim and the proof together: {{work.struts_per_hour}}
struts an hour, sustained, for six hours, afternoon after afternoon.
It is not a sprint rate; it is the rate the body can hold while the
saw cools between cuts, the pile is stacked as it grows, and the
count is kept.

Read the rhythm as a sequence of stations rather than a series of
cuts. Stage, halve, quarter, eighth, stack, count — one circuit per
section, the same motions in the same order, the saw never waiting
on a decision. The chart's flatness is the point: a sustained rate
is a rate with no thinking in it, and the method was arranged so the
thinking happened once, in these chapters, and the ripping days just
repeat. The afternoons that beat the rate are the ones where
something was skipped — a staging, a stacking — and the debt shows
up the next morning as a pile to sort and a chain to sharpen before
the first cut.

## Chain, file, and the cost of a dull cut

The four days' secret dependency: a ripping chain is either sharp
or it is spending your arms and your fuel, and the difference is
visible in the pile.

A dull chain does not stop cutting. It starts *pushing* — the saw
walks off the line, the kerf widens into a groove, and the bar works
hot while the feller works harder. The kerf is the honest ledger:
every cut is a {{saw.kerf_in}}-inch-wide line of sawdust, and a dull
chain turns that line wide and ragged, eating the recovery figure
Chapter {{ch.recovery}} computed, one cut at a time. The file is
the fix and the cadence is the rule: a few strokes per tooth, every
few tanks, *before* the pushing starts, never after. Touch-up
filing at the bench costs minutes; a chain sharpened only when it
fails costs the afternoon it fails in.

The practical cadence on this build: file at the midday fuel stop,
whether or not the chain asks. The ripping days are the saw's
hardest work in the whole method — bar buried, grain sideways, wet
wood — and the chain that starts the afternoon sharp finishes it
dull. The file is the one tool in the fortnight that is used *more*
than the saw.

## What this is worth an hour

The sidebar's job is to put the four days' labour in the only
currency that survives the fortnight, and then to put the caveat
beside it.

Splitting your own struts is work, and the work has a rate. The
full accounting lives in Chapter {{ch.hour_at_log}} — the strut
valued three ways, the disagreement between the ways kept in the
table, and the rate computed against the dressed board that is the
honest comparison. Here the summary, once: an hour at the log
produces members worth more than the hour's alternative at a wage,
*if and only if* the alternative was going to be buying that timber
— and the substitution value is not money anybody has been paid. It
buys groceries only if the timber was going to be bought anyway.

The ripping days feel like the part of the fortnight where the
money is made, and in substitution terms they are — the frame's
timber is the single largest line the build avoids paying. But the
rate is a rate on *your* hours, priced against *your* alternative,
and the chapter that computes it is careful to say which parts of
the comparison flatter the method and which do not. The ripping
chapter's own verdict is narrower: the four days are the price of
the timber, the price is real, and it is paid in afternoons instead
of invoices. Whether that trade is worth it is Chapter
{{ch.hour_at_log}}'s question, asked properly, with the numbers up.
