# 1. The Tree That Was Already Down

*A windfall pine, a small saw, and a question nobody had asked out loud*

## The Tree That Was Already Down

This book starts with one specific tree, because every number after this page
descends from it, and because the story does not make sense if the tree stays
abstract.

A pine came down in a storm on land I could walk to. Not a tree anybody
planted for the purpose. Not a timber sale. A windfall: sixty or so feet of
pine that had stood in a line of others, gone over in one night, root plate
up, crown in the brush, and the trunk lying where it fell with the ground
still pressed into its bark.

The useful part of a windfall like that is the straight part between the
root flare and the first big limb. On this tree that ran {{tree.usable_length_ft}}
feet: {{tree.butt_diameter_in}} inches across at the butt,
{{tree.top_diameter_in}} at the top. The flare below it and the crown above
it were firewood. In the middle was a cylinder of pine the length of a
shipping container, and there was no way to use it.

That sentence is the whole book in miniature. The wood was not missing,
rotten, or in some faraway forest. It was on the ground, within carrying
distance of where the building now stands. What was missing was every
machine, service, and middleman that stands between a fallen trunk and a
usable stick of wood — and it is those, not the tree, that decide what a
piece of wood is worth. Chapter {{ch.middlemen}} walks that chain one step at
a time. This chapter is about the tree at the near end of it, before anybody
got paid.

## Sixty feet of pine, and no way to use it

Here is the problem stated plainly, because most of the ways it gets solved
are ways of moving the problem somewhere more expensive.

A standing tree is worth almost nothing until somebody converts it. A
windfall is worth even less: it cannot wait for a forester, it will not be
part of any sale, and it will rot where it lies. Convert it the normal way
and the trunk has to travel — dragged to a landing, loaded, trucked to a
mill, and then hauled again as boards to wherever the building is. Every one
of those journeys is paid by the mile and the hour, and a mill will not
touch a two-tree job without a setup charge that makes the exercise
pointless. The honest summary of the conventional route is that the tree
was worth less than the diesel to move it, which is why the tree stayed
where it fell.

The saw that changed that was a {{saw.displacement_cc}}-cc chainsaw with a
{{saw.bar_in}}-inch bar that cost {{saw.price_usd}} dollars on a shelf. The
whole shelf price of the machine that eventually turned this tree into a
building was less than what hauling the trunk to somebody else's mill would
have cost before a single board was cut — not an exaggeration, a comparison,
and it is the comparison the method lives on. The industrial route charges
for handling, and handling is most of the route. A saw you already own, on
the site where the wood already lies, removes the route and charges only
fuel, a chain, and time.

But on the day I am describing, none of that was obvious. What was obvious
was a {{tree.usable_length_ft}}-foot trunk that no mill wanted, cut from a
tree no buyer would bother with, on ground nobody was being paid to stand
on. The saw sat on its shelf because a saw alone does not make the trunk
usable. A saw makes cuts. What I did not yet know was which cut made the
tree stop being a problem and start being a frame.

Measured properly, the trunk held {{tree.solid_bf}} board feet of solid
wood. That figure is not a boast; it is what the tape said, and the way the
book counts it is explained in full in Chapter {{ch.recovery}}. The same
trunk, sawn into two-by-fours the way a mill would, keeps about
{{tree.sawn_recovery_pct}} per cent of itself as saleable lumber. Split the
way this book will split it, it keeps {{tree.recovery_pct}} per cent —
{{tree.recovery_gain}} times as much — without a second machine ever
touching it. All of that is later. Here it only matters as the reason the
question in the next paragraph was worth asking, because the difference
between {{tree.sawn_recovery_pct}} and {{tree.recovery_pct}} per cent of a
{{tree.solid_bf}}-board-foot trunk is the difference between a load of
firewood and a building.

## The tree, measured

The drawing matters more than it looks. Every chapter that follows — the
recovery arithmetic, the two sizing methods, the cut list, the fortnight —
takes its numbers from this same trunk, and if the book ever disagrees with
itself about the tree, the disagreement is a bug, not an editorial position.
So the tree gets its own page, drawn to scale, with the three measurements
the whole method hangs on.

**The butt.** {{tree.butt_diameter_in}} inches. The widest point that still
counts as trunk; below it is the flare, which splits badly and gets bucked
off.

**The top.** {{tree.top_diameter_in}} inches, where the straight stem runs
out at the first limb. Above it is crown, which is firewood.

**The usable length.** {{tree.usable_length_ft}} feet between the two. A
trunk tapers from {{tree.butt_diameter_in}} to {{tree.top_diameter_in}} inches
along that run, and everything about how the log gets cut is decided by
that taper: buck it into {{tree.section_length_ft}}-foot sections and each
section is close enough to a cylinder that one jig fits them all.

Those are the only three numbers a tree-first build needs from the tree.
Not species, not grade, not a timber buyer's walkaround. Butt, top, length.
Chapter {{ch.method_b}} turns exactly these three into a dome diameter, and
the round trip in Chapter {{ch.round_trip}} proves it is the same
calculation a designer runs from the other direction. The drawing here is
not decoration. It is the input.

## What I actually wanted

It is worth saying what the tree was *for*, because the method is only
interesting against the thing it was asked to build.

I wanted a round building, big enough to live in, built alone.

Round, not square: a dome, {{dome.diameter_ft}} feet across with
{{dome.floor_sqft}} square feet of floor when the arithmetic was done — a
serious room rather than a shed, and the largest size these trees
would fill. Chapter {{ch.method_b}} shows that {{dome.trees}} trees are what
it takes, and that the frame only actually consumes
{{dome.trees_strictly_needed}} of them; the rest of the pile is
spares, blocking, floor stock and firewood, and the book says so rather than hiding it.

Built alone, because that is the constraint that shaped every decision after
it. A crew can lift a sixteen-foot stick; one person cannot. So the members
are {{tree.section_length_ft}} feet, the panels are lifted one at a time,
and the whole frame is designed around what a single person can carry, set,
and fasten without a second pair of hands. The book never pretends this is
the fastest way to build. It is the way that works when the only labour
budget is your own, and the fortnight in the middle of this part is what
that constraint actually costs.

And from what was on the ground, because the economics only work that way.
Buy the timber and the frame costs what timber costs; Chapter {{ch.money}}
does that arithmetic both ways and prints the number that does not help the
argument. Here it is enough to say the goal was never to build cheaply in
money alone. It was to build with the tree that was already down, one saw,
and two weeks — and to find out what that made possible that the lumberyard
price could not.

That is the book. The rest of it is the tree answering.
