# 52. What I Would Do Differently

*The honest closing chapter*

## What I Would Do Differently

The story strand closes where it opened: one person, one saw,
one tree that was already down. Between the first page and this
one the tree became a frame, the frame became a method, and the
method grew a book around it — and the honest way to close a
story like that is the same way the book has handled everything:
name what would change, keep what was right, and say what the
next one is.

This chapter is not a confession and not a victory lap. It is
the builder's review of the build, written while the review is
still worth something — the five decisions that would change,
the parts that stay exactly, and the second dome the method
has already earned.

## The five decisions I would change

Specific, named, with what each would have cost — because
"learn from your mistakes" is advice until the mistakes have
names.

**The marking scheme came too late.** The backwards panel of
Chapter {{ch.what_broke}} was the tuition for a scheme that
should have existed on day one: mark the sawn faces, one
stripe per class, bark up. It cost an afternoon to discover
and it would have cost ten minutes to do first. The next build
starts with the crayon.

**The keys were cut too early.** The green-frame rule of
Chapter {{ch.other_species_other_sections}} — cut the keys
after the frame has dried — was learned the expensive way,
with a set of keys that fit a frame that no longer existed by
month three. The rule is now in the book; it would have been
in the build, if the build had had the book.

**The ring was trusted once too often.** The tolerance chapter
names the ring as the one place error accumulates, and the
build proved it: a ring checked once, at setting, moved a
fraction under the first course, and the correction cost a
morning of shims. The three checks of Chapter
{{ch.where_error_goes}} would have caught it in the first one,
for free.

**The skin waited for the wrong perfection.** The shell was
delayed while the "right" skin was decided, and the frame stood
in the weather longer than it should have. The shell ladder of
Chapter {{ch.shell_ladder}} now says what the build learned:
the first skin's only job is to keep the rain off, and the
perfect skin is the one you buy after the first one has taught
you what you actually need.

**The fuel was not logged.** The dashes in the money chapter's
table are this decision: the build kept its hours and not its
fuel, and the metabolic ledger of Chapter {{ch.fuel_ledger}}
had to be modelled where it should have been measured. The
next build logs every tank, because the book's rule about
numbers applies to the builder's own: what is not recorded is
not claimed.

## What I would keep exactly

The parts that were right the first time, listed with the same
specificity as the changes, because a review that only lists
failures is a review that learned nothing about what worked.

**The split count.** Eight, for the reasons Chapter {{ch.split}}
printed — the key fits, the stick carries, the cuts stay
"through the middle". Not one afternoon of the build ever
regretted it.

**The overfit.** The {{jig.head_overfit_in}} inches of Chapter
{{ch.head_overfit}} are the cheapest insurance in the method,
and the build spent them exactly as designed: every error that
had somewhere to go, went there.

**The jig, as built.** One flat bench, stops instead of
measurements, the day that paid for the other thirteen. The
build's single best decision, and the one the second build
will not have to make again.

**The order of the fortnight.** Fell, buck, split, jig, panels,
raise — the sequence the book now teaches — survived contact
with the weather, the wood and the calendar without a single
step being moved. The order was right before the method
existed, and the method is mostly the order, written down.

**The honesty habit.** The corrections, the dashes, the near
miss reported — the discipline that became Chapter
{{ch.corrections}} was the build's own habit first, and it is
the one thing in this chapter that costs nothing to keep and
everything to lose.

## The next one

What the second dome is, and why — the question every builder
asks at the end of the fortnight, answered the way the build
answered everything: with the numbers, and without the romance.

The second dome is the same method at the size the first build's
own leftovers suggest: the spare struts and the offcut pile
already hint at a smaller companion — a greenhouse, a workshop,
a guest pod — and the flat-rate argument of Chapter
{{ch.flat_rate}} says the second frame's parts list is the first
frame's parts list, with the problems already solved. The
second build's real change is the one Chapter {{ch.the_hours}}
estimated: the tuition is paid, the jig exists, the scheme is
written — and the fortnight that took fourteen days the first
time is the same fourteen days, spent where they were always
supposed to go, on the repetition instead of on the learning.

The next one is also the book's promise to its reader, stated
plainly: the method was never for one dome. It was for the
*second* dome — the one built without the discoverer, from
these pages, by somebody who has never watched it done. Every
number in this book was written for that person, and the
chapter's last line is the book's whole thesis in one
sentence:

**The tree was already down. The saw cost two hundred
dollars. The rest is in here.**

## The dome, a year on

The closing image, and the one plate in the book that is a
photograph's job rather than the simulator's: the dome, a year
on, standing where the tree stood.

The plate is a placeholder in this edition — the photograph is
still to be taken, and the book's figure system says so
plainly rather than substituting a drawing that would lie
about the year. When the picture exists, it will show what the
frame chapters only promised: the wood greyed and settled, the
keys seated, the skin doing its one job, and the building
looking less like a fortnight's work and more like the land's
own idea of a room. The book will be revised for it, the way
the book revises for everything — the photograph added, the
caption written, and the year's evidence given the same page
the fortnight earned.

Until then, the story strand closes on the frame at day
fourteen — the standing plate of Chapter
{{ch.days_thirteen_and_fourteen_raising}} — because that is
what a year looks like at its beginning, and this book is the
beginning's record, kept honest all the way to the end.
