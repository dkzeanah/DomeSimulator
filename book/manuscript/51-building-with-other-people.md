# 51. Building With Other People

*What changes when there are three of you*

## Other People

The method was designed for one person, and every number in the
book so far assumed that constraint — the six-foot member, the
one bench, the props a single pair of hands can set. This chapter
is what changes when there are three of you, because crews
happen: a partner, a neighbour, a build somebody else asked
for.

The changes are fewer than the fear suggests, and they cluster
in three places: the benches multiply, the teaching becomes a
step in the build, and the liability conversation stops being
optional. The method itself — the split, the jig, the seam —
does not change at all, which is the chapter's first and most
useful sentence: **the method was built from processes, not
from a person, and processes hand over.**

## Parallel jigs

Two benches, and the sorting problem that follows — because a
crew does not make the panels faster by crowding one jig; it
makes them faster by running two.

The second bench is a copy of the first, set from the same
panel drawings of Chapter {{ch.panel_drawings}}, checked against
each other before production starts — two jigs that disagree
produce two populations of panels, and the raising is where
they meet. The check is the same one the circle layout used:
measure both benches against the drawing, not against each
other, and demand they agree within the gasket's give.

The sorting problem is the real new work. Two benches pull
from the pile twice as fast, and the pile stops being a thing
one person holds in their head; it becomes a shared inventory
that two people mark, move and count — which is where the
marking scheme of Chapter {{ch.days_ten_to_twelve_forty_panels}}
earns its keep. The rule for the crew: one person owns the
pile, one person owns each bench, and the pile-owner's count is
the number the day ends on. Parallel work without one shared
count is how a dome ends up with thirty-nine panels and an
argument.

## Teaching the twelve steps

How to hand the procedure to somebody in an afternoon, because
the method's own claim — twelve motions, no decisions — is
exactly what makes it teachable.

The teaching order is the jig's order, and it starts with the
*why* in one sentence each: the bench replaces the tape, the
overfit is the crumple zone, the flush cut is the only true
cut. Then the twelve motions of Chapter
{{ch.days_ten_to_twelve_forty_panels}}, demonstrated once,
watched twice, and the watcher's first panel is the tuition
panel — the fourth-panel rule applies per person, not per
build, and a crew that budgets three practice panels per new
hand is a crew that plans for the method instead of against
it.

The one thing not to teach is the decisions, because there
are none: the stops decide, the schedule decides, the marking
scheme decides. The teaching is handing over *habits*, and a
habit handed over correctly survives the hand that taught it.
An afternoon, then: the method was designed to be this
teachable, and the teachability is part of the design — a
method that needed its inventor present at every joint would
be the frankendome again, wearing a crew.

## A build that is not yours

The third change, and the chapter's serious close: helping
somebody else build, and the conversation that must happen
first.

The liability conversation is not legal advice — the book is
not a lawyer, and it says so — it is the plain speech version
of the safety and engineering pages, delivered before the first
cut: this method is a prototype, not an engineered standard;
the loads are the engineer's job; the felling is the most
dangerous part and the rules of Chapter
{{ch.days_one_and_two_felling}} are not optional; and the
person whose tree, whose site and whose back are on the line
is the person who owns the decisions. Helping is not owning,
and the difference must be said out loud, because silence is
how a favour becomes a lawsuit.

Then the build itself is the easiest of the three changes:
the guest's dome is the same cut list, the same jig, the same
fortnight — and the guest learns the method by building it,
which is the only way anybody ever really learns it. The
chapter's last sentence is the book's, borrowed from the
campaigns it grew out of: **we make the method; you build on
it.** And when the build is somebody else's, the gift is the
procedure — the twelve motions, the honest numbers, the
warnings — and the rest is their fortnight.
