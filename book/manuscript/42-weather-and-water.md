# 42. Weather and Water

*Where it leaks, and where the water goes*

## Weather and Water

The honest chapter about domes and rain, because the dome's
reputation was made by this subject and the subject deserves the
book's full discipline.

The title page inverted the usual order on purpose: the frame is
the part you are allowed to get wrong, and the skin is not — a
frame out of line stands for fifty years, and a skin that leaks one
winter takes the frame with it. This chapter is the skin's side of
that bargain, and it is the reason the book treats the seams here
with more care than anywhere else.

Here is the fact the frame chapters have been circling: a dome's
roof is one surface made of {{frame.panels}} flat panels meeting
at {{frame.seams}} interior seams, and every seam is a place water
can get in. A rectangle concentrates its roof's risk in two
valleys and a ridge; the dome spreads the same risk across
{{frame.seams}} lines. The answer is not fifty-five seals; it is
the two principles this chapter teaches — shed, don't seal; and
where water must be managed, manage it deliberately, at the rim.
The chapter closes with the inside face, because the dome that
survives the rain outside can still be ruined by the water its own
breathing makes inside, and the book says that before the reader
discovers it in month three.

## Every seam is a potential leak

Say the number, because the number is the strategy:
{{frame.seams}} seams. Fifty-five lines on the outside of the
building where two panels meet, fifty-five keys, fifty-five
weather details — and the honest way to think about them is not
"one roof" but "{{frame.seams}} small roofs, all meeting".

The frame chapters already did the structural half of each seam:
the key locates the geometry, the gasket takes the tolerance.
The weather half is a different question, and it is the one that
decides whether the building lasts. A seam that fits is not
automatically a seam that sheds — water moves downhill, and a
seam's fit matters only where the water actually runs. The
dome's seams run in two families: the steep ones near the crown,
where water moves fast and the seam's own slope carries it away,
and the shallow ones near the rim, where water lingers, pools in
the key's shadow, and waits for a gap. The strategy follows the
slope: the crown seams need alignment, the rim seams need
*weathering* — a drip edge, an overlap, a path that takes the
water off the seam before it can sit on it.

The count is the discipline. A builder who treats
{{frame.seams}} seams as one repeated detail does the same
detail wrong {{frame.seams}} times; a builder who separates them
into the two families the geometry gives — the steep and the
shallow — has two details to perfect and a schedule to repeat
them by. Chapter {{ch.seams}} is the schedule. This chapter is
the weather it has to survive.

## Shedding, not sealing

The principle that actually works on a compound surface, and the
reason "waterproof" is the wrong word for what this book builds.

A sealed surface tries to stop water at the membrane — one
barrier, perfect, everywhere. Sealing fails on a dome because a
dome moves: the frame dries, the panels settle, the seams open
and close with the seasons, and a seal that was perfect in July
is a cracked line by January. The answer the roof-building world
already knows — and shinglers have known for centuries — is to
stop trying to hold the water back and instead *hand it
downhill*: every surface overlaps the one below it, every seam
wears a shingle, and the water is always on top of the roof,
never inside it.

So the dome is weathered as {{frame.seams}} little roofs, each
seam flashed like a valley: the upper panel's skin laps over the
lower's, the key is protected rather than depended on, and the
seam's own slope is the drainage. The skin chapters supplied the
panels; this chapter supplies the lapping order; and the result
is the honest claim the book makes about weather: not "the dome
never leaks" — no roof can promise that — but "where water
reaches the seam, the seam hands it down, and the lowest seam
hands it off the rim". Shedding is a path, not a promise, and
paths can be walked, checked and repaired — which seals, once
breached, cannot.

## Harvesting it instead

The one place the shape helps with water, and it is not small:
a dome is a very good rain collector.

Every drop that lands on the shell runs down the panels to the
brim, and the brim is a circle — one continuous gutter, already
built, waiting only for the collection the next chapter's
hardware provides. Chapter {{ch.brim_gutter}} computes the annual
catch: the footprint, the local rainfall, a declared efficiency,
and the answer comes out in thousands of gallons a year off a
building this size. The dome does not merely *tolerate* rain; it
gathers it, and the gathering is free — the roof was already a
gutter, the shape was already a funnel, and the only purchase is
the tank.

The honest note, kept from the films that made this point: the
catch is not drinking water without treatment. The filter makes
it clear; the lamp makes it safe; neither does the other's job —
and the book's water system, when Chapter
{{ch.heat_power_and_the_small_systems}} reaches it, is a
first-flush diverter and a UV lamp downstream of the tank, not a
rain barrel and optimism. For everything else — irrigation,
flushing, washing, the buffer against a dry well — the catch is
as good as the roof, and the roof is the dome's.

## Condensation and the inside face

The last section is the problem people discover in month three,
and it is worth a whole section because it is not rain.

A dome is warm inside and cold outside, and the meeting of the
two happens *inside the skin*: moist room air reaches a cold
surface, the water condenses, and the builder who sealed every
outside seam perfectly wakes up to water running down the inside
of the shell. The seams were not the leak. The air was.

The answer is the seam channel, and it is the reason the later
chapters of this book engineer it deliberately rather than
sealing it away: the cavity between the frame's wedges is the
one continuous void that connects the whole shell, and it is
exactly where condensation belongs — collected, drained at the
rim, vented at the crown. The full climate system — the dew
points, the levels, the drawer, the plate — is Chapters
{{ch.seam_jobs}} and {{ch.dew_point}}, and they are the book's
real answer to the inside face. Here the principle is enough:
**never seal the inside face against the air; give the water a
path, and drain it at the rim.** The dome that leaks from rain
failed its seams. The dome that leaks from condensation was
built by someone who thought the inside face was not part of
the weather.

The chapter ends where every weather chapter should: outside,
shed; inside, drain; at the rim, catch. The dome is not a box,
and its water is not a nuisance to be sealed away — it is a
flow to be directed, and the direction is downhill, through
{{frame.seams}} honest seams, off the brim, into the tank.
