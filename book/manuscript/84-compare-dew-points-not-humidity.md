# 84. Compare Dew Points, Not Humidity

*Never ask which air is more humid; ask which is drier, where it will condense, and which way the water moves -- the seam's climate, run by one controller*

## Compare Dew Points, Not Humidity

Humidity numbers lie about moisture. Dew points do not. This
chapter is the rule, the levels, and the drawer — the
seam-climate system that turns the channel of Chapter
{{ch.seam_jobs}} into the building's moisture brain, run by
one controller that decides where the water goes before the
water decides for itself.

The chapter's material comes from the wedge book's five
climate chapters and the film that built them, and its
central claim is a rule of thumb with real physics under it:
**never compare relative humidities; compare dew points.**
The rest of the chapter is that rule, spent — the three
questions it asks, the levels it sorts the seams into, the
metal that moves the heat, and the desiccant that lives in a
drawer instead of a seam.

## The rule

Two airs compared by relative humidity can point the wrong
way; compared by dew point they cannot — because relative
humidity is a ratio against *temperature*, and temperature
changes the denominator.

Warm air at fifty per cent humidity carries far more water
than cold air at fifty per cent; a number that ignores the
temperature has quietly answered the wrong question. The dew
point is the question done right: the temperature at which
the air's water begins to condense out. Two airs compared by
dew point rank by how *dry* they are — the property that
actually decides whether water appears on a surface — and
the comparison never flips the way humidity's can.

The rule produces the three questions the rest of the
chapter answers, and the film's phrasing is worth keeping
whole: **which air is drier? Will it condense? And which
way does the water move?** Every decision in the climate
system — where to vent, where to drain, what the metal
does, where the desiccant lives — is an answer to one of
the three, and the three are asked in that order, every
time, by one controller.

## The four levels and the ring that cannot drain

The dome's seams do not all slope alike, and the slope
decides the water's fate — so the system sorts the shell
into bands.

Near the crown, the seams run steep: water moves fast,
dwells nowhere, and the channel's job is simple — carry it
down. Near the rim, the seams run shallow, and one band is
the troublemaker: the pentagon ring runs **dead level**, a
horizontal seam all the way around the dome, and a level
seam cannot drain — water that reaches it sits there,
against the key, against the skin, waiting for a gap.

The answer is the chapter's second rule: **condense low,
drain at the rim.** The system arranges for the moisture to
appear where it is welcome — the coolest, lowest, most
reachable part of the channel — and gives that band a
deliberate drain to the outside, so the water the ring
catches is the water the drain releases. The dead-level ring
stops being a trap and becomes the system's collection
point, and the stack effect — warm air rising through the
channel, pascals of it — keeps the whole loop moving in
the direction the drain expects.

## A plate is a heat pump

Metal in the seam runs both ways, and the direction is the
climate's lever.

A Peltier plate — the solid-state heat pump the film
demonstrates — sits in the channel with one face to the room
and one to the channel. In winter it moves heat *in*,
warming the seam's inside face so nothing condenses where
the room can see it; in summer it moves heat *out*,
chilling the channel's surfaces to a steady fifteen or
twenty degrees below the room, so the moisture condenses
*there, on purpose* — on the plate's face, at the dew
point, where the drain of the last section is waiting. The
plate turns the chapter's threat into its instrument: the
water appears exactly where the design wants it, on a
surface built to receive it, and the frost that forms at
the dew point in the coldest weather is the system's own
thermometer, not its failure.

![A plate is a heat pump.](../../deliverables/book/figures/dew-plate.png)

Two disciplines keep the metal honest. **Plate first, wood
second:** the plate is the condensing surface, sized and
finned for it, so the wood behind it never becomes the
condensing surface by accident. And the galvanic table: the
channel mixes metals — aluminium plate, copper wiring,
steel screws — and dissimilar metals in a damp channel make
a battery that eats one of them; the table decides which
metals may touch and which need a barrier between them.
Moisture, in this chapter, is a *designed* resident of the
channel — and the design extends to the metals it lives
with.

## The desiccant belongs in a drawer

The last component is the one the chapter's title sentences
to the furniture, and the reason is two measurements.

A desiccant — molecular sieve, the beads that hold a fifth
of their weight in water — looks like the channel's perfect
resident: pack the seam, let the beads drink. The
measurements say no. A packed seam passes almost no air —
the beads' resistance rises steeply with the bed's length,
which is the Ergun equation doing what it does — so the
packed channel stops being the vent the whole system
depends on. And the beads cannot be regenerated where they
sit: drying them needs heat and airflow the seam cannot
supply, so a saturated seam is a wet seam that stays wet.

![Two drawers and a gate.](../../deliverables/book/figures/dew-drawers.png)

The answer is the drawer: two desiccant beds at the rim,
in removable drawers, with a gate that sends the channel's
air through one while the other dries out — a twin-tower
system, small, accessible, and *fails safe*, because a
drawer left out is a bypass, not a blockage. The stove's
heat never shares the stove's air: the combustion circuit
is sealed, and the desiccant dries on waste heat the
building was already paying for.

## Dew point decides

The chapter's close is the controller, because the system's
parts only cohere when one thing holds the rule.

Eleven weathers — the film's own list of the conditions a
year throws at a shell — one controller, and one decision
procedure: compare dew points, ask the three questions,
choose between the loop and the purge. The loop dries the
*structure* — air around the frame, through the channel,
slow, continuous. The purge dries the *room* — air through
the house, fast, wasteful if misused. The controller's
whole job is to know which drying the building needs, and
the knowing is the rule, coded: **dew point decides.**
The seam climate ends where the book began — with a number
computed, a decision made from the number, and nothing left
to the weather's mood.
