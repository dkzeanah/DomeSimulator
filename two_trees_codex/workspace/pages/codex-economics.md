# 25. Count the work the wood actually replaced

The discovery notes use an illustrative store price and a proposed production rate to ask whether wedge-making can have meaningful value. Preserve those figures as the origin of the question, with their original date and uncertainty. Replace them with receipts and measured labor when making a claim about this build.

Separate cash spent, reusable equipment acquired, consumables used, and labor hours. A saw bought for this project still has value afterward, while fuel and oil used during the project do not. A comparison can show either initial cash requirement or allocated project cost, but it should say which one it shows.

Count labor from setup through completed accepted parts. Record maintenance, movement, fixture work, inspection, failed attempts, and cleanup where they belong. A rate measured during one productive afternoon does not establish the average rate for the whole frame. The book can show both the good run and the full ledger.

To compare with purchased lumber, define the product the purchased material would have supplied. Volume equality alone does not establish equal structural function, connection cost, dimensional stability, or finish quality. The strongest comparison is between complete, specified alternatives that serve the same purpose.

Use the same discipline for the “two trees” claim. It describes the proposed source of wedge members. List added seam material, fasteners, supports, and anything bought for the base separately. The extra rows do not weaken the experiment; they show its actual boundary.

The economic ending can remain useful even if it is mixed. The method may save a machine while requiring more handling. It may preserve wood while demanding a more involved connection. Those tradeoffs tell a future builder where the method fits and where a conventional alternative may fit better.
