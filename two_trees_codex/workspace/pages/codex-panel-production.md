# 19. Make forty triangles, one checked batch at a time

Build the inventory around two panel families: ten A-A-A frames and thirty B-A-B frames. Preserve the member IDs through assembly so each completed panel can be traced to its source sections. A panel label should identify its family, handedness, orientation, and position in the assembly plan.

Treat the first checked panel of each family as a reference, not as permission to stop measuring. Recheck the fixture after adjustments and at sensible batch intervals. When a piece is rejected, keep the reason in the log. Reworking it, replacing it, and accepting it unchanged are different outcomes.

The short family is not simply whatever remains after making long members. Each B member still needs the right usable section and joint geometry. Allocate the demanding pieces first, then review how the remaining inventory can satisfy the rest of the schedule. The source tree assignment can change while the member's required geometry stays fixed.

At the end of each batch, update three counts: blanks remaining; finished accepted members; completed accepted panels. This avoids the common error of counting the same wood once as a member and again as part of a panel. Keep rejected and experimental material outside those totals.

Photograph completed panels flat from overhead and with a low angle that reveals their profile. Include a scale reference and a readable label. Those images serve the book's explanatory pages, but their first job is to document what was actually built.

Before transport or raising, check the completed inventory against the assembly map and the design's handling plan. Panels have mass and can be awkward to control even when each member was manageable alone. The modular method changes the unit being handled; the handling arrangements must change with it.
