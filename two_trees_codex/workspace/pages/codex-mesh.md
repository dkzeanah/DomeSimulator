# 5. A sphere made from straight lines

A geodesic construction begins with a regular organization of directions on a sphere. In this project's 2V model, the triangular faces of an icosahedron are subdivided once and the resulting points are projected to the sphere. The chords between those projected points become the straight edges of the triangulation.

The frequency describes subdivision. It does not say how many feet across the dome is. A 2V dome can be enlarged or reduced while retaining its geometric relationships. Every nominal edge scales with the sphere radius, which is why a dimensionless chord factor can connect a desired dome size to a length on the bench.

The hemisphere used here contains forty triangular faces, twenty-six vertices, and sixty-five unique edges. Its open rim is a ten-sided polygon. The vertices on that rim lie on a circle; the floor boundary between them is made from straight lines. This difference matters when quoting floor area.

The model separates two edge classes. With the book's lettering, A is the longer chord and B is the shorter. Ten panels have three A sides. Thirty panels have one A side and two B sides. The corresponding triangle shapes repeat, but the real wooden members also depend on how their corners and seams are made.

Look at the topology plate before the timber plate. In the topology, a line has no thickness, no end cut, and no fastener. Its value is precision: it establishes exactly what the assembly is meant to follow. The wooden model adds the decisions necessary to occupy space around those lines.
