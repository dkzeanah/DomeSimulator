# 9. Method two: let the tree set the size

Begin with a measured inventory rather than a desired radius. Identify usable straight sections in the available trunks. Record their lengths, their smaller-end diameters, and the defects that affect where a member can be obtained. A tall tree and a long run of usable stock are different measurements.

The book's starting proposal is eight sections of approximately six feet from each tree. Eight radial blanks per section produce 8 × 8 = 64 potential blanks from one tree. Reserving four for rejection or spares leaves sixty intended members. Two trees produce 128 potential blanks and a target allocation of 120 members.

This is a count-based first pass. A blank earns its place in the frame only after it meets the length, cross-section, straightness, and material requirements for its assigned part. Eight adequate pieces at the butt of the tree do not prove that eight adequate pieces also exist in every upper section.

Let S be the usable blank length after planned end trimming. A nominal-only starting estimate is R = S / fA. If S is 72 inches, that gives a radius of approximately 116.498 inches, or 9.708 feet, and a diameter of approximately 19.416 feet. The corresponding nominal short chord is about 63.670 inches.

That estimate intentionally ignores physical corner and seam geometry. To size the real frame, hold the selected cross-section and joint settings fixed and solve for the largest radius whose every required blank fits the available length. The Sizing lab's “Fit physical stock” action uses the raw-wedge solver for this check. Enter any additional fabrication stock rather than treating the final installed piece as the whole blank requirement.

The two methods meet here. Design-first asks how much wood a chosen radius requires. Tree-first asks what radius the available wood permits. With identical joint settings and allowances, going forward and then backward should return the same design. That round trip is an unusually useful check on a cut list.
