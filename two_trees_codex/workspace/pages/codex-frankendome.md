# 2. What the Frankendome taught me

The earlier project notes describe the Frankendome as an experiment in joining unlike triangular frames. Its members were deliberately varied. The problem was not only how to make a triangle, but how one completed triangle could meet another when their edges did not offer identical profiles.

That experiment made the interface important. The notes describe testing V brackets at edge-to-edge and corner relationships. A connector that can accommodate an awkward member is useful because it lets the designer ask what the material can do before demanding that every piece become conventional rectangular lumber.

The notes also describe a modified upper pentagon: ten pieces in that earlier configuration where another arrangement would use fifteen. That number belongs to the earlier experiment. It must not quietly replace the forty-face, one-hundred-and-twenty-member count used for the book's standard panelized 2V hemisphere. A prototype can teach the next design without being the same design.

The useful inheritance is a way of thinking. Build manageable units. Give each unit a defined boundary. Study what happens at that boundary. If the connector and the member profile are designed together, the member has more possible shapes than the lumber aisle suggests.

For the final story, place the earliest dated Frankendome photograph beside a later wedge prototype. Describe one specific connection that was troublesome, what was tried, and what it revealed.
