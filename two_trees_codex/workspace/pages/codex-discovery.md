# 3. Finding the wedge

A round trunk contains eight equal radial sectors if its cross-section is divided at forty-five-degree intervals. Extend one sector along a straight section of trunk and it becomes a long member with two sawn radial faces and the original curved outside. The curve that rectangular milling would remove becomes part of the proposed frame.

The discovery in the project notes is the relationship between this profile and a panelized dome. A small triangular frame provides a repeatable use for the wedge. A separate seam detail can accommodate how neighboring triangles turn relative to one another. The member no longer has to do every geometric job by itself.

This is also where the attractive claims need to become questions. Preserving the curved portion can preserve material, but an area calculation alone does not say how much usable framing emerges from a real tree. The bark, defects, taper, irregular saw path, and the ends of the member still matter. The useful discovery is a method worth measuring, not a percentage protected from measurement.

In the design software, the wedge can face in four cardinal directions. Its point can face into the dome, out toward the sky, into its own triangular panel, or across the panel boundary. Each orientation changes the relationships at the seams and corners. The original outward-curve description is one configuration of the larger investigation.

The book's most useful discovery scene will show a change in understanding. Preserve a sketch or photograph of the first wedge beside the first assembled corner. Record what the wood suggested, what the model corrected, and what the full-size piece later changed again. Those three moments give the explanation a human sequence.
