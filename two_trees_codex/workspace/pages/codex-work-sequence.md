# 15. Organize the work around the small saw

The intended tool is part of the constraint that makes the experiment interesting. It is also a machine with a defined operating envelope. Record its actual model, approved equipment combination, condition, and maintenance. Use its manual and appropriate instruction to determine operations and setup; do not infer capability from an illustration or from bar length alone.

The planning sequence begins with stock that has been safely obtained, supported, and made accessible. Divide the work into measurement and marking, material processing, inspection, and storage. Give each activity enough room that an interruption does not put an unmarked or uninspected member into the accepted stack.

Before committing the inventory, document one representative sample through the proposed process. Measure the resulting kerf and the usable piece. Record the time spent securing and repositioning work, maintaining the chain, and clearing material. Cutting time alone describes only one part of the operation.

Keep the production log short enough to use. A row needs the source section, resulting blank IDs, accepted count, rejected count, elapsed labor, and reason for losses. A photograph of the labeled group supports the row. If a process change improves the result, label when it started so the book can compare like with like.

The goal is a controlled batch, not a dramatic image of hurried cutting. The story can include fatigue, a dull chain, weather, or a setup that had to be changed if those events occur. They belong in the account because they explain the labor needed to transform a counted tree into counted parts.
