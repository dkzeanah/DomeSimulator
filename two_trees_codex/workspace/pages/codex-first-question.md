# 1. Start with what is here

I want to find out how directly I can get from a tree to a frame. Not from a stack of uniform lumber, not from a kit delivered with every decision already made, but from the wood itself. The shape of that wood has to enter the design early enough to matter.

The proposal is small enough to count. Forty triangular frames need three members each: one hundred and twenty members. Two suitable trunks, each divided into eight useful sections, could yield eight wedge blanks per section. That is sixty-four blanks from each tree, sixty intended members and four potential spares. Those are the starting numbers, before I know which pieces the wood will actually permit.

The time limit gives the question an edge. I intend to design, produce the members, and frame the dome within two weeks, using the small Husqvarna saw described in my equipment notes. A limited tool makes the length and repeatability of the parts important. The saw's role belongs in the story because it affects the process; its model name is not a performance guarantee.

There is a temptation to tell the ending first. A photograph of a finished dome would make the early uncertainty disappear. I want to keep that uncertainty visible. Where does a nominal six-foot dimension become a real part? How much straight trunk is there after defects and cut losses? Which joint detail survives a full-size trial? Those questions are what the build is for.

The first record will be the starting inventory. Photograph both source trunks or the available sections. Include a tape in the frame. Record the actual usable lengths, the smallest diameters, and every assumption used to choose the initial dome. If the available wood changes the plan, the change becomes a chapter in the experiment.
