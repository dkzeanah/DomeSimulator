# 26. What two trees will have taught me

At the beginning, the idea fits in one sentence: use the wedge that a tree already contains to make the repeated parts of a dome. By the end, that sentence should have acquired measurements, joints, mistakes, photographs, and a result.

I want the last chapter to return to the first inventory. How many sections did the actual trees supply? How many blanks became accepted members? Which allowance changed after the first physical trial? How much of the fortnight went into sawing, how much into making the geometry repeatable, and how much into joining the panels?

Those answers cannot be written in advance. What can be written is the standard they must meet. The count should reconcile. The final model should match the built configuration. The photographs should show the important details. The story should preserve the moments where the wood or the assembly contradicted the expectation.

If the frame comes together within the target, the account should explain what preparation and conditions made that possible. If it takes longer, the account should explain the difference. The discovery has value when another person can understand and evaluate it; a protected deadline would make the explanation less useful.

The closing scene belongs to the author after the work. Return to one object from the opening: a source stump, an unused wedge, the saw, or the first sketch. Describe it as it is now, after the experiment. Let the measured result and the lived scene occupy the same page.
