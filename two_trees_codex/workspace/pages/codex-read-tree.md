# 12. Read the trunk in sections

Give each source tree an ID before allocating members. Give each candidate section a second ID and mark its original orientation. Then record usable length, large-end diameter, small-end diameter, visible sweep, knots, checks, damage, and any reason a region must be excluded. Photograph the section beside its label.

A short-section method can respond to local curvature. If a bend interrupts a long run, the useful material on either side may still supply shorter members. This is a possibility to inspect, not a guarantee that any crooked trunk can be converted into the planned stock count. Every new boundary also consumes length through a cut and may create an end that needs trimming.

Use the smallest relevant cross-section when checking whether a blank provides the material the design requires throughout its length. An impressive butt diameter is not the diameter of an upper six-foot section. A circular model also needs to be reconciled with an oval or irregular real section before its area becomes an inventory claim.

Make assignments provisionally. A long, sound blank can remain uncommitted until the most demanding members are covered. Shorter acceptable blanks can be considered for the B family. Pieces awaiting judgment should stay in a separate recorded category so that uncertain stock does not appear in the accepted count.

The field ledger is more useful than a single yield percentage. Its rows can answer where a missing member went. Was it lost to end damage, excessive taper, a defect exposed during cutting, or a fabrication error? Those explanations turn an experiment into a process another person can evaluate.
